Summary 
AGRA’s monthly Food Security Monitor serves as a vital tool for sharing timely data with key stakeholders, supporting 
informed, evidence-based decisions across the agricultural sector. Below are key highlights from the June 2026 Food 
Security Monitor edition: 
Global Market Updates 
• In June 2026, global food commodity prices showed mixed trends, with the FAO Food Price Index averaging 
130.3 points, down 0.3% from May but 1.7% higher than a year earlier , as declines in cereals, dairy, and sugar 
prices, driven by strong supplies and improved production prospects, offset gains in vegetable oils and meat. 
Vegetable oil prices increased due to tighter palm oil supplies and strong biofuel demand, while meat pri ces 
reached a record high, supported by higher poultry and bovine meat prices. Similarly, the International Grain 
Council’s Grains and Oilseeds Index fell 1.4% month -on-month to 224.2 but remained 5.8% above its year -
earlier level, reflecting short -term market weakness amid generally stronger annual prices.  Rice prices rose 
the most, both monthly and annually, while barley recorded the largest monthly decline. Wheat and maize prices 
also eased during the month but stayed above year -ago levels, and soybeans posted the strongest annual 
increase among oilseeds despite a slight monthly dip. 
• Global fertilizer markets are showing signs of easing after a period of sharp increases, supported by the 
reopening of the Strait of Hormuz  for just short period: lower freight costs and increased urea exports from Iran 
and China, have driven urea prices down despite remaining above year-ago levels. Since the agreement, around 
640,000 metric tons of sulphur and 427,000 metric tons of urea have moved through the waterway, a significant 
increase compared with volumes shipped during the conflict. The resumption of trade has eased concerns over 
a potential global food price crisis that could have resulted from fertilizer shortages and reduced crop yields.  
However, analysts caution that recovery remains slow, with fertilizer markets expected to remain tight in the near 
term despite the gradual improvement in maritime flows.  Consequently, significant uncertainty persists due to 
geopolitical risks evidenced by the collapse of the ceasefire after few days , policy shifts, production costs, and 
climate-related disruptions. At the end of June, p hosphate fertilizer prices remained high, with DAP declining 
slightly to US$910/MT and MAP edging up to US$954/MT, both posting strong year-on-year gains of over 12%, while 
potash remained stable at US$494/MT; in contrast, urea fell sharply by 12.5% month-on-month to US$720/MT after 
peaking in A pril, though it remained nearly 10% higher than a year earlier . Across Africa, fertilizer affordability 
remains a major concern, particularly in East and Southern Africa, where countries such as Uganda, Rwanda, 
Malawi, and Mozambique have recorded steep increases in DAP, NPK, and urea prices, reflecting tight market 
conditions. In contrast, fertilizer markets in West Africa have been relatively stable, with modest price movements 
in Nigeria and unchanged prices in Ghana. Overall, the continued price volatility highlights the importance of 
expanding regional fertilizer production, strengthening intra-African trade, and improving market coordination to 
enhance resilience and reduce import dependence. 
• A strong El Niño is expected to persist through late 2026 and early 2027, posing significant risks to agriculture, 
food security, and livelihoods across Africa. Eastern and Southern Africa are likely to experience below-average 
rainfall, higher temperatures, drought, and water shortages, while parts of the Horn of Africa face an increased 
risk of flooding and severe storms. Countries most vulnerable include Keny a, Uganda, Ethiopia, Sudan, South 
Sudan, Somalia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Nigeria, and Cameroon. The resulting impacts 
could include reduced crop yields, livestock losses, food price inflation, water stress, pest and disease outbreaks, 
and increased humanitarian needs, particularly among smallholder farmers and vulnerable communities. As 
climate shocks intensify, millions of people could face heightened food insecurity, displacement, and livelihood 
disruptions, underscoring the need for early preparedness and resilience-building measures.

unchanged in Tanzania, but surged by 16.8% in Uganda despite staying 4.2% below last year’s levels. Wheat prices 
dropped significantly in Ethiopia (-17.3%) following improved Belg harvest supplies, while Kenya remained stable

Food Security Updates 
• Across East Africa , food security conditions in June 2026 reflected a mix of seasonal improvements and 
persistent humanitarian concerns . Favourable rainfall and the onset of harvests in parts of Ethiopia, Kenya, 
Tanzania, and Uganda improved crop production prospects, labour opportunities, and food availability, helping 
to stabilize or improve food security outcomes in some agricultural areas. However, acute food insecurity 
remained widespread in conflict -affected, pastoral, and displacement -prone areas, driven by insecurity,  high 
food prices, weak purchasing power, climate shocks, and limited livelihood recovery. Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and 
Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes persisted across parts of Ethiopia’s north and pastoral south, Kenya’s pastoral 
counties and refugee settle ments, and among vulnerable populations in Uganda, particularly refugees and 
households in Karamoja. Tanzania experienced seasonal food insecurity linked to poor production and economic 
constraints, although harvests were expected to improve conditions sig nificantly. South Sudan remained the 
region’s most severe hotspot, with widespread Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes, localized Catastrophe (IPC 
Phase 5), extremely high levels of acute malnutrition, and a credible risk of Famine in some conflict-affected areas. 
Overall, conflict, displacement, climatic shocks, high food and fuel prices, and constrained incomes continued to 
drive substantial humanitarian needs across the region, despite expected seasonal improvements in food 
availability in several countries. 
• Southern Africa’s food security situation showed overall improvement during the 2026 harvest period, driven 
by better agricultural production and increased household food stocks in countries such as Malawi, Zambia, 
and parts of Zimbabwe and Mozambique. In Malawi and Zambia, improved harvests and enhanced food access 
reduced the severity of food insecurity compared with previous drought -affected years, although localized 
pockets of Crisis (IPC Phase 3) persisted due to poverty, climatic shocks, flooding , pest infestations, and 
macroeconomic pressures. In Zimbabwe, favourable harvests improved food availability and supported Minimal 
(IPC Phase 1) outcomes in surplus -producing areas, but food security is expected to deteriorate in deficit -
producing regions as household stocks decline and market dependence increases, with many areas projected to 
move into Crisis (IPC Phase 3) from October 2026. Mozambique presents the most mixed outlook, with conflict -
affected areas of Cabo Delgado and northern Nampula conti nuing to face Crisis -level food insecurity due to 
insecurity, displacement, and disrupted livelihoods, while southern and central regions have benefited from 
improved production and seasonal income. Across the region, high food prices, weak purchasing power, recurrent 
climatic shocks, and constrained livelihood opportunities remain key drivers of vulnerability, while the ongoing El 
Niño event poses a significant risk to the 2026/27 agricultural season through delayed rainfall, reduced labour 
opportunities, and the potential for below-average production, threatening to reverse recent food security gains. 
• Across West Africa, food security conditions are expected to remain under significant pressure through late 
2026 and early 2027, driven by a combination of conflict, displacement, high food prices, weak household

Food Trade Updates 
• Ethiopia and Kenya have introduced a formal cross -border trade framework that allows licensed residents 
living near official border crossings to legally trade approved goods worth up to US$1,000 per month through 
regulated channels. Designed to support local livelihoods, strengthen supply chains, and curb informal trade and 
smuggling, the scheme covers designated border zones, prioritizes women and vulnerable groups, and permits 
trade in selected livestock, agricultural products, and consumer goods. All transactions must pass through official 
customs posts, with traders required to declare goods and comply with applicable taxes and regulations, 
providing a structured and monitored alternative to long-standing informal cross-border commerce. 
• Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Togo, Benin, and Nigeria have officially launched the Abidjan –Lagos Corridor 
Management Authority (ALCoMA) to oversee the development and long -term management of the 1,028 -
kilometre Abidjan–Lagos Multimodal Corridor. Expected to be operational by 2030, the project will feature a six-
lane coastal highway and a parallel high-speed railway linking five of West Africa’s largest economies, improving 
the movement of people, goods, and services along a key trade route. The corridor is projected to generate more 
than US$16 billion in annual trade and about US$1.3 billion in toll revenues, while boosting regional integration, 
industrialization, job creation, and intra -African trade under the AfCFTA, ultimately benefiting over 500 million 
people across the region.

Food Security Dashboard  
The Food Security Dashboard summarises trends in the number of people experiencing IPC/CH Phase 3+ or Equivalent 
(Table 2) and tracks average changes in food prices over the past year  (Table 1) across 17 countries in Eastern, 
Southern, and Western Africa. 
Based on the June 2026 IPC/CH Phase 3+ estimates (Table 2), Nigeria has by far the largest number of people facing 
Crisis or worse food insecurity, with 36.3 million people, followed by South Sudan (7.9 million), Kenya (4.1 million), Malawi 
(4.0 million), and Mozambique (3.5 million). At the other end of the scal e, the lowest absolute numbers are observed 
in Togo (0.33 million), Uganda (0.47 million), and Tanzania (0.51 million). In proportional terms, South Sudan faces the 
most severe burden, with 55 % of its population in IPC/CH Phase 3+, far exceeding all other countries. Uganda (32%) 
has the second-highest proportion, followed by Kenya and Malawi (22% each), while Mozambique (18%), Zambia (17%), 
Nigeria (16.7%), and Zimbabwe (16%) also face significant population-level food insecurity burdens. By contrast, Ghana 
(4%), Côte d’Ivoire (5%), Togo (5%), and Tanzania (5%) have the lowest proportional burdens among the countries 
listed. Overall, while Nigeria accounts for the largest number of food -insecure people, the most severe population -
wide impacts are concentrated in South Sudan and several East and Southern African countries, where a much larger 
share of the population is experiencing Crisis or worse food insecurity.  
Table 1 highlights diverging average maize price trends across African markets, with continued price declines across 
much of West and Southern Africa contrasting with rising prices in several East African countries. In West Africa, 
average maize prices in local currencies fell significantly over both the six-month and annual periods, particularly in 
Ghana (-27.7% over six months; -57.0% year-on-year), Nigeria (+3.7% over six months but -36.2% year-on-year), Niger 
(-11.1%; -39.0%), Togo (-11.8%; -34.8%), and Mali (-5.1%; -26.4%), suggesting improved market supplies and easing food 
price pressures. An exception is Burkina Faso, where maize prices surged by 52.8% over six months and 78.7% year -
on-year, indicating severe market stress and supply constraints. 
In East Africa, maize prices remained elevated in several markets. South Sudan recorded the strongest increases, with 
prices rising 24.2% over six months and 34.9% year-on-year, reflecting ongoing conflict-related disruptions and supply 
shortages. Kenya (+ 2.5% over six months) and Uganda (+6.4%) also registered recent increases, although prices 
remained below year -ago levels in both countries. Tanzania recorded substantial declines of 18.8% over both six 
months and year-on-year, while Rwanda also showed relatively stable conditions, with a slight 2.6% decline over six 
months but a 4.9% increase year-on-year. Ethiopia's prices also fell by 10.7% over six months and remained unchanged 
compared to a year earlier, indicating some recent market easing. 
Southern Africa presents a mixed picture. Malawi (-41.1% over six months; -26.8% year-on-year), and Zambia (-20.2%; 
-53.4%), pointing to improved availability and easing inflationary pressures. In contrast, Mozambique experienced a 
modest 2.7% increase over six months and a sharp 51.2% rise year -on-year, suggesting that recent stabilization has 
followed an earlier period of significant inflation. Overall, the data indicate easing maize prices across most West 
African and several Southern African markets, while South Sudan and, to a lesser extent, Kenya, Uganda, and 
Mozambique remain key hotspots of sustained price pressure.

Global Market Update 
Global Food Commodity Price Trends 
The FAO Food Price Index (FFPI) (Figure 2) averaged 130.3 points in June 2026, down 0.3% from May but 1.7% higher than 
a year earlier, as lower prices for cereals, dairy, and sugar offset gains in vegetable oils and meat. Global cereal prices 
declined on strong harvest prospects and ample supplies, while su gar prices fell due to increased production and 
exports from Brazil. In contrast, vegetable oil prices rose on tighter palm oil availability and strong biofuel demand, 
and meat prices reached a record high, driven by higher poultry and bovine meat prices. Dairy prices continued to 
soften across most products as improving milk supplies and export availability outweighed demand in key markets.1 
As of 30 June, the International Grains Council (IGC) Grains and Oilseeds Index (GOI) (Table 3) stood at 224.2, declining 
by 1.4% month-on-month but remaining 5.8% higher year -on-year, indicating that while grain and oilseed markets 
experienced some short -term weakness, prices generally remained above year -ago levels. Among the major 
commodities, rice was the only commodity that rose by 3% over the month and 3.8% year -on-year, suggesting firm 
demand or supply constraints. In contrast, barley recorded the steepest monthly decline (-6.4%), although it still rose 
by 4.9% year-on-year. Wheat and maize also weakened during the month, falling by 2.8% and 2. 1%, respectively, but 
both remained higher than a year ago. In the oilseeds market, soybeans showed the strongest annual increase 
(+8.9%), despite a modest monthly decline (-0.5%), highlighting continued underlying strength in soybean markets.

Fertiliser Prices Outlook 
Global fertilizer markets are entering a new phase of uncertainty: while the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz has 
restored trade flows, reduced freight costs, and resumed Iranian urea exports, fertilizer supply chains remain 
vulnerable to geopolitical ten sions, policy changes, production costs, and climate risks. Increased urea availability 
from Iran and China has pushed global urea prices lower amid weak demand, whereas phosphate prices remain 
elevated due to persistently high sulfur costs, which continue  to constrain production and keep supplies tight. For 
Africa, which relies heavily on fertilizer imports, this volatility underscores the need to strengthen regional production, 
boost intra-African trade, and improve market coordination to build a more resilient and self-sufficient fertilizer sector. 
Following the interim agreement on 15 June 2026, fertilizer shipments through the Strait of Hormuz have begun to 
recover after being severely disrupted during the 3½‑month conflict. Since the agreement, around 640,000 metric tons 
of sulphur and 427,000 metric tons of urea have moved through the waterway, a significant increase compared with

93.8% year-on-year, respectively. By contrast, Zambia recorded significant declines, with NPK and urea prices falling

remain comparatively stable.

Update on the Impacts of the US-Iran Crisis 
The Iran war has precipitated a global input crisis by severely disrupting energy and fertilizer supply chains, 
particularly following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz6. This chokepoint is critical given that the Middle East supplies 
roughly one‑third of global urea exports, and since late February 2026, approximately 3.9 million tonnes, about 30%, of 
Gulf fertilizer exports have been suspended.7 Consequently, urea prices doubled within weeks, while phosphate prices 
also increased, sharply raising agricultural input costs worldwide. Rising input costs are transmitted through the food 
system, amplifying risks to food security. Elevated prices for fertilizer, energy, and transport discourage fertilizer use, 
depress crop yields, and increase production costs, which collectively place upwa rd pressure on food prices. These 
effects are intensified by ongoing supply constraints, higher prices for key inputs such as natural gas and sulphur, and 
reduced production rates in affected regions. Although fertilizer prices moderated slightly in May 20 26, partly due to 
China lifting export restrictions , persistent supply disruptions and price volatility remain significant risks, especially if 
the conflict continues8. 
Crucially, the burden of this emerging crisis falls disproportionately on poorer countries and vulnerable households, 
which already spend a large share of their income on food. The trajectory from an input crisis to a full-scale food crisis 
will depend on the duration and severity of the conflict, but risks are clearly escalating. Prolonged disruptions could 
lead to significant fertilizer shortages, reduced agricultural output, and worsening food insecurity, particularly in 
structurally vulnerable regions.  IFPRI finds that disruptions linked to the Iran war , especially through the Strait of 
Hormuz, have driven sharp increases in global fuel and fertilizer prices, both of which are essential for food production 
and distribution9. Developing countries are especially exposed because of their heavy reliance on imported fuel and 
fertilizers, meaning global price spikes quickly translate into higher domestic costs, currency pressures, and rising food 
prices. Using economywide models covering 20 developing countries, IFPRI shows that while overall macroeconomic 
impacts may appear modest, they are uneven and disproportionately affect agriculture and low‑income households. 
These price shocks propagate through supply chains by raising transport and production costs, while higher fertilizer 
prices reduce application rates and lower yields. As a result, food availability declines and affordability worsens, even 
in cases where GDP losses remain relatively small. In human terms, the consequences are substantial: IFPRI estimates 
that more than 20 million additional people could fall into poverty, while around 2.5 million more may face 
undernourishment, illustrating how relatively small percentage shocks can translate into large absolute increases in 
hardship.

At the country level, IFPRI’s analysis of Nigeria illustrates how these global shocks are transmitted domestically through 
interconnected fuel, fertilizer, and food price channels 10. Nigeria remains highly vulnerable due to its reliance on 
imported refined fuel and key fertilizer inputs, despite being an oil producer. This dual structure means that, while 
higher global prices could boost export revenues, they simultaneously increase domestic costs and inflationary 
pressures. Food inflation in Nigeria had already peaked at about 40.9% in June 2024, driven by earlier global shocks 
and domestic policy reforms, before easing to roughly 21 –23% by mid‑2025. However, renewed increases in fuel and 
fertilizer prices linked to the Iran conflict threaten to reverse this improvement. Rising fuel costs increase transport and 
processing expenses, while higher fertilizer prices constrain agricultural output, rei nforcing upward pressure on food 
prices and household living costs.  Although IFPRI modelling suggests Nigeria could experience net welfare gains if 
higher export earnings outweigh rising domestic costs, these benefits are uneven and delayed. In the short term, 
households face rising prices and reduced purchasing power, und erscoring the complexity of the shock. The report 
concludes that Nigeria’s policy response will be critical in balancing immediate inflation risks with longer ‑term 
opportunities to strengthen domestic refining, fertilizer production, and economic resilience. 
At the farm level, similar dynamics are evident in countries such as Mozambique, where the closure of the Strait of 
Hormuz is already reshaping farmers’ expectations regarding input access and affordability. Mozambique depends 
on imports routed through thi s corridor , including about 22% of its fertilizers , making it highly exposed to supply 
disruptions and price increases. 11 Farmers, particularly during key agricultural periods of harvest and planting, face 
growing uncertainty about whether they can access or afford sufficient fertilizer.  These pressures are compounded 
by a domestic fuel crisis marked by rising diesel and petrol prices and inconsistent supply, which increases transport 
costs for both inputs and outputs. Evidence from farming communities indicates that fertilizer prices have  already 
risen by 10–20% in some areas, while transport disruptions are limiting market access and raising operational costs. 12 
In response, farmers are adjusting behaviour , delaying planting, reducing fertilizer use, or selling produce early at 
lower prices to secure cash , often at the expense of future productivity and income.  Although fertilizers remain 
available in some areas, affordability is deteriorating rapidly, and concerns about further shortages and price spikes 
are intensifying. These trends point to the risk of reduced input use, lower yields, and worsening food secur ity 
outcomes, particularly for smallholder farmers. Taken together, the evidence highlights how global geopolitical shocks 
are translating into tangible, on‑the‑ground impacts, reinforcing vulnerabilities across food systems and threatening 
agricultural livelihoods.

Update on El Niño conditions 
A strong El Niño event is developing and is expected to persist through late 2026 and early 2027, with the World 
Meteorological Organization estimating an 80 –90% probability of occurrence and NOAA indicating a 63% chance of 
a very strong event. Across Afri ca, the phenomenon is expected to significantly disrupt weather patterns, bringing 
below-average rainfall and higher temperatures across much of Eastern and Southern Africa, while increasing the 
risk of heavy rainfall, flooding, and severe storms in parts of the Horn of Africa. These climate shocks threaten 
agricultural production, water resources, and livelihoods, particularly for millions of smallholder farmers who depend 
on rainfed agriculture. 
According to FAO and WFP assessments, the country’s most vulnerable to El Niño -related impacts include Kenya, 
Uganda, Ethiopia, Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Nigeria, and 
Cameroon. In the Greater Horn of Africa, prolonged dry spells, delayed rainfall, and rising temperatures are expected 
to reduce cereal production, deteriorate pasture conditions, and constrain water availability. Southern Africa faces a 
heightened risk of agricultural drought, heatwaves, and significant reductions in maize and livestock production, 
raising concerns as several countries are still recovering from the severe 2023–2024 El Niño episode. Meanwhile, West 
Africa and the Sahel could experience more erratic rainfall patterns, localized droughts, and increased production 
uncertainty. 
The impacts are expected to extend beyond agriculture and place additional strain on food systems and economies 
across the continent. Reduced crop production could drive food price inflation, increase livestock feed costs, and 
deepen import dependence, whi le water shortages may affect irrigation, hydropower generation, and household 
access to water. Livestock producers are likely to face declining pasture availability, animal health challenges, and 
increased mortality rates, while climate anomalies could he ighten the risk of pest and disease outbreaks, including 
fall armyworm and desert locust infestations. In fragile and conflict-affected areas, these pressures may exacerbate 
displacement, resource competition, and humanitarian needs, underscoring the impor tance of early preparedness 
and anticipatory action to safeguard food security and livelihoods through 2027.

Seasonal Monitor and Cropping Conditions 28 
In Eastern Africa, rainfall deficits are increasingly affecting agricultural activities, disrupting Belg harvests and the 
development of main/Meher season crops across much of the northern part of the region. In contrast, conditions have 
been more favorable in southern Eastern Africa, where main -season cereal harvesting has been completed in 
Tanzania’s unimodal areas and is progressing in the bimodal zones of Uganda, Rwanda, and Burundi. Meanwhile, 
crops in Somalia, Kenya, northern Uganda, and bimodal areas of Tanzania continue to develop, although below -
average rainfall and emerging moisture stress are raising concerns in several areas. 
The Greater Horn of Africa (GHA) Climate Outlook for 2026 warns of a sharp two -phase climate pattern across the 
region. From July to September (JAS), much of northern and equatorial East Africa , including Sudan, South Sudan, 
Ethiopia, Uganda, and western Kenya, is expected to experience significantly below-normal rainfall and above-normal 
temperatures, with the most severe drought risk concentrated in northern and central Ethiopia. 29 These dry conditions 
are likely to reduce water availability, lower river flows and hydropower generation, increase food insecurity, and place 
additional stress on livelihoods, agriculture, and livestock systems. 
The outlook then shifts dramatically during the October to December (OND) season, when above -normal rainfall is 
forecast across Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, much of Somalia, Tanzania, and southeastern Ethiopia, with some 
areas having a greater than 90% probability of excessive rainfall. Driven largely by a strengthening El Niño event and 
potentially supported by a weak positive Indian Ocean Dipole, the wet season could improve soil moisture, replenish 
water resources, and support agricultural recovery. H owever, it also raises the risk of floods, landslides, crop and 
infrastructure damage, disease outbreaks, and disruptions to energy systems. The report highlights similarities with 
past El Niño years such as 1997 and 2023 and urges governments and partners  to prepare for both drought impacts 
in the near term and flood-related emergencies later in the year. 
In Kenya’s North Rift region, maize farmers are facing the prospect of heavy harvest losses after a prolonged four‑week 
dry spell, compounded by the delayed distribution of fertilizer during critical crop growth stages. 30 The drought has 
caused crops to wither across hundreds of acres, raising concerns about food security in one of the country’s key 
agricultural zones. Agricultural officials warn that if dry conditions continue, losses could extend beyond maize to 
beans, tea, coffee, and horticultural crops, with significant implications for national food supplies and rural livelihoods. 
The situation highlights the growing impact of climate change on Kenya’s farming sector and could lead to reduced 
yields, higher food prices, and greater economic vulnerability for households that depend on agriculture.

Ethiopia and Kenya 
Ethiopia and Kenya have introduced a formal cross -border trade 
framework that allows licensed residents living near official border 
crossings to legally trade approved goods worth up to US$1,000 per 
month through regulated channels. Designed to support loc al 
livelihoods, strengthen supply chains, and curb informal trade and 
smuggling, the scheme covers designated border zones, prioritizes 
women and vulnerable groups, and permits trade in selected livestock, 
agricultural products, and consumer goods. All transactions must pass 
through official customs posts, with traders required to declare goods 
and comply with applicable taxes and regulations, providing a 
structured and monitored alternative to long -standing informal cross-
border commerce. 
 
Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Togo, Benin, and Nigeria  
Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Togo, Benin, and Nigeria have officially launched the 
Abidjan–Lagos Corridor Management Authority (ALCoMA) to oversee the 
development and long -term management of the 1,028 -kilometre 
Abidjan–Lagos Multimodal Corridor. Expected to be op erational by 2030, 
the project will feature a six -lane coastal highway and a parallel high -
speed railway linking five of West Africa’s largest economies, improving 
the movement of people, goods, and services along a key trade route. The 
corridor is projected to generate more than US$16 billion in annual trade 
and about US$1.3 billion in toll revenues, while boosting regional 
integration, industrialization, job creation, and intra -African trade under 
the AfCFTA, ultimately benefiting over 500 million people across the 
region.
