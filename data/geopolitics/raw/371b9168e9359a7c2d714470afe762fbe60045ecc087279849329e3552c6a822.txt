Methodology Overview
The Situation Hub's analytical process uses a structured Human-in-the-Loop workflow that combines automated analysis retrieval
through GANNET Virtual Assistant, DFS's AI-powered humanitarian assistant, with expert review by the DFS analysis team. GANNET
automates the extraction and generation of crisis-related insights based on structured prompts, which are aligned with a tailored
analytical framework. These outputs are then reviewed, validated, and refined by analysts to ensure accuracy, contextual relevance,
and operational utility. The workflow is organized into four key phases: planning and setup, ongoing operations, quality assurance, and
system evolution, ensuring a balance between speed and analytical rigor.
Planning, Setup & Quality Control
In the planning phase, analysts develop a contextualized analytical framework, largely based on the Joint Intersectoral Analysis
Framework (JIAF), and convert it into structured prompts for AI-assisted data extraction. These prompts are refined over time to
improve output quality. Data sources are vetted for reliability, and translations are reviewed for accuracy. Using a centralized Content
Management System (CMS), analysts review and curate AI-generated outputs, validate them against external sources, and make
refinements to enhance clarity and relevance. The CMS enables collaborative editing and version tracking, ensuring outputs align with
humanitarian needs.
Bias Mitigation
To minimize bias, the team diversifies data sources, cross-verifies information, and continuously evaluates the AI's performance.
Analysts are crucial in identifying and correcting inaccuracies, especially in automated categorization. Future improvements aim to
enhance source credibility checks and sentiment analysis, further strengthening the reliability of the Situation Hub's outputs.

with real-time updates. Regular reviews and verification with in-country partners ensure accuracy, while manual updates are used for

Operational Environment
Economy
Current Economic Situation
Lebanon remains in sovereign default with a persistent banking crisis, blocked or amputated deposits, and a Moody's credit rating of
'C' in the absence of debt restructuring and a comprehensive stabilization programme [6][1]. War-related economic damage is
substantial, with direct and indirect losses since 2024 estimated at $20–25 billion, and public revenues have collapsed — war-related
losses cut government income by roughly half, with the 2026 estimated income tax of $644 million accounting for about 11% of public
revenue [1][14]. The balance of payments is under acute pressure from high import dependence (energy, medicines, food) and
plunging tourism: total passengers fell 34.2% and arrivals fell 40.2% in the first five months of 2026, reducing essential foreign-exchange
inflows [6][1]. Sharp price shocks are eroding purchasing power, with fuel prices rising approximately 84% between mid-February and
mid-May 2026, food prices 10–20% higher by mid-May 2026 versus 2025, and bread prices increasing approximately 15% following the
March escalation [5]. The industrial sector is contracting sharply — with reported declines of around 45–50% — alongside factory
closures in insecure areas and heavy export disruptions, while agriculture, construction and industry jointly represented about 16% of
GDP in 2024 and services (education and health) about 19% of GDP, underscoring broad sectoral damage to output and human
capital [20][1][6]. Authorities and international actors have discussed rapid IMF financing of up to $1 billion for budgetary and
humanitarian support, and calls have been made for tax relief and fiscal measures to support the private sector, but such emergency
funding would not replace the need for debt restructuring and full financial sector remediation [6][1][14]. The unresolved banking crisis
and lack of a credible recovery path continue to limit credit, investment, and Lebanon's ability to absorb and finance reconstruction
needs [6][1].
Post-Conflict Economic Landscape
Lebanon faces a massive reconstruction bill estimated at approximately USD 20–25 billion in war-related direct and indirect losses
since 2024, with more than 61,000 housing units totally or partially damaged during March–early May 2026 [1][6]. A UN-led assessment
by UNDP found around USD 365 million in building damage in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, estimating approximately 649,000 cubic
metres of debris, 146 buildings completely destroyed, 264 partially destroyed, 3,168 housing units destroyed and 4,437 damaged in that
area [10]. UNOPS estimated Lebanon generated 14.5 million metric tonnes of conflict debris in 2024 and promotes circular rubble-reuse
approaches to reduce material costs, create local jobs and accelerate recovery [21][37]. Critical infrastructure damage is constraining
recovery: key bridges and roads — including Litani crossings — have been destroyed or damaged, isolating over 150,000 people and
restricting humanitarian access, while the health system has been eroded by repeated attacks (WHO recorded 196 attacks since 2
March, with 131 deaths and 379 injuries) and education infrastructure has been widely disrupted [5][10]. Southern Lebanon, estimated
to account for approximately 25% of national economic activity, has concentrated a major part of the economic shock, with
agriculture, construction and industry — which together represented about 16% of GDP in 2024 — hit particularly hard; assessments
report more than 22% of agricultural land damaged and 76% of farmers in southern Lebanon affected by evacuation orders and
disruptions to farming activities [1][6][5]. The private and industrial sectors report steep declines — some industry observers cite
production contractions of roughly 45–50% in affected segments — with factory closures in insecure areas, sharp export reductions
and substantial job losses among daily and seasonal workers [20]. Humanitarian financing and immediate recovery funding are
severely short: the revised 2026 Flash Appeal (March–August) increased needs to USD 639.9 million for an estimated 1.4 million people,
while only USD 185.9 million had been received by 31 May, leaving a funding gap of approximately USD 454 million [5]. Oxfam's digital
needs assessment found livelihoods overwhelmingly the top priority (47.12% of respondents), with displaced households reporting
livelihoods as their primary need more frequently than other groups [25]. Return and reintegration prospects remain weak: monitoring
in neighbouring Syria and returnee assessments show limited immediate employment opportunities — 51% of assessed locations
reported no immediate work — with continued reliance on savings, remittances and unsustainable coping mechanisms among
returned or displaced households [15][80]. Recovery will require large-scale international finance and clear debt resolution,
coordinated reconstruction planning, and transparent, people-centred approaches, as emergency IMF financing and current
humanitarian aid levels are insufficient by themselves to meet the scale of physical reconstruction and socio-economic recovery
documented to date [1][6][5].
Stability of Currency and Bank System
The Banque du Liban is described as able to preserve apparent monetary stability but lacks the capacity to finance large-scale
reconstruction without creating new macroeconomic imbalances; using foreign currency reserves for reconstruction imports would
risk further eroding those reserves and repeating past confusion between monetary policy, public financing and bank rescue [1].
Apparent exchange-rate stability has coexisted with reserve losses and a lack of implemented reforms, underscoring that restoring
central-bank credibility, completing debt restructuring, unifying accounts and strengthening governance are prerequisites for
sustainable monetary stability and banking-sector recovery [6][1]. Credit rating agency Moody's assigns Lebanon a 'C' rating,
highlighting prolonged sovereign default, an under-restructured banking sector, lack of credible debt-restructuring and governance
reforms, and noting that limited emergency IMF financing — possible rapid support of up to about USD 1 billion — would not substitute
for a comprehensive stabilization programme [6]. The fiscal and liquidity response space is constrained: prolonged economic crisis
has depleted state resources and weakened national response capacity, estimated income tax receipts for 2026 are reported at USD
644 million (about 11% of public revenue), and authorities have signalled using part of existing fiscal margins to support the economy in
the short term [5][14]. Domestic price shocks are increasing inflationary and fiscal pressures, with food prices rising about 10–20% by
mid-May 2026 versus 2025, fuel prices climbing approximately 84% between mid-February and mid-May 2026, and bread prices rising
approximately 15% after the March escalation [5].

Employment
Livelihoods represent the most critical priority for affected populations, with Oxfam's digital needs assessment finding households
reporting loss of income and an affordability crisis covering rent, food, medication and other essentials [25]. Availability of unskilled
day work has declined sharply: casual labour fell by 22% to an average of 1.8 days per week, while the purchasing power of a day's
wage for basic food is about 30% below 2025 levels and approximately 20% below the three-year average [69]. Continued large-scale
displacement — nearly one million people displaced by early June — has further disrupted livelihoods and market access, with
migrants and non-Lebanese groups reported as disproportionately affected by loss of formal accommodation and income
opportunities [34][23]. The least mobile productive sectors — agriculture, construction and manufacturing, together about 16% of GDP
in 2024 — have been particularly disrupted; around 22% of agricultural land had been damaged and evacuation orders affected an
estimated 76% of farmers in southern Lebanon, undermining rural employment and seasonal labour opportunities [6][5]. The crisis has
a gendered employment impact: women face reduced access to income-generating opportunities due to childcare, mobility and
safety constraints, and women-headed households report particular difficulty covering basic costs [25][12]. Humanitarian actors are
prioritising cash responses to protect livelihoods, with planned multi-purpose cash assistance and government-shared beneficiary
lists for emergency MPCA/E-MPCA distributions covering tens of thousands of households to mitigate income loss where markets
remain functional [34][16].
Politics
Governance Structures and Rule of Law
Lebanese leaders have publicly framed a choice between restoring a state monopoly on arms and remaining subject to militia
influence, with President Joseph Aoun and Prime Minister Nawaf Salam calling for the exclusivity of arms and for the Lebanese army to
assume central roles in the south [164][166][143]. Foreign Minister Youssef Rajji has similarly argued the state can resolve Hezbollah's
weapons issue if appropriate decisions and international support are provided, while noting the government lacks the unilateral
political and military means to impose disarmament [165][143]. Analysts describe this as a governance dilemma in which Lebanon's
legal sovereignty coexists with limited state control over armed actors, producing a national-legal versus regional-military tension in
decision-making and accountability [143][165].
Reconstruction and recovery have been explicitly conditioned on stronger governance and transparency, with calls including
publishing a damage map, clear compensation criteria, traceable aid, municipal involvement under oversight, and phased
programming to reduce the risk of clientelist reconstruction [131]. Credit-rating and economic analyses warn that sovereign default,
unresolved banking sector losses, and the absence of credible budgets, audits, and legal reforms undermine the state's capacity to
finance and execute reconstruction and to restore public-sector functionality [119]. Sectoral reforms are being presented as
institutional tests — ending Middle East Airlines' monopoly, for instance, is framed as a test of regulator independence requiring a
professional, financed civil aviation authority able to enforce safety, licensing, and equal access rather than ad hoc political licensing
[152]. Humanitarian coordination planning includes an IOM Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) to monitor displacement and inform
decision-makers for March–August 2026, for which IOM requested USD 1,240,000 to sustain DTM operations [156].

Security And Stability
Security Overview
Renewed hostilities between Israel and Iran on 7–8 June increased regional instability, and Lebanon has experienced continued
instability, renewed strikes, evacuation notices, and access constraints that are disrupting health services and response operations
[253][256]. More than one million people in Lebanon have been displaced since the escalation in March 2026, with Lebanese
government-linked figures citing over 3,600 killed and thousands injured during the period [223][271][255][265][264]. WHO has
warned of acute health threats in affected areas — including high trauma caseloads, communicable disease risks linked to

Secretary-General António Guterres has called for urgent de-escalation, noting the fragile ceasefire environment and the risk of

Security Forces and Law Enforcement
Lebanese security forces have been directly affected by the conflict since 2 March: ACLED recorded at least 21 Israeli strikes on
Lebanese security forces that killed about 30 personnel and injured 17, with Israeli drones targeting identified LAF vehicles, undermining
state institutions' operational capacity and morale [225]. In early June, an Israeli strike hit a Lebanese Army vehicle operating beyond
the so-called "yellow line", killing three soldiers and prompting Lebanese military sources to dispute Israeli claims that the area
required coordination with the ceasefire mechanism [225]. The expected expansion of Lebanese Armed Forces deployments in
southern areas remains politically sensitive and operationally risky: mediators have pushed to place the Army at the centre of
stabilization efforts, but observers note this exposes the institution to heavy pressure and casualties while it lacks the capacity to
enforce disarmament of non-state armed groups on its own [218]. Attacks have also affected first responders and emergency
services, including critical damage to Jabal Amel Hospital on 2 June and emergency responders injured while assisting victims, further
complicating protection of civilians and operational emergency response [253][247]. Humanitarian access and civil-military
coordination remain constrained and heavily regulated by notification mechanisms, with 203 humanitarian movements supported
through the Humanitarian Notification System between 2 March and 11 June — 170 of them south of the Litani — and multiple missions
cancelled or postponed due to security concerns between 26 May and 9 June [242]. On 10 June, the UN human rights office
announced a deployment of investigators to Lebanon to assess potential violations of international law by all parties during the
conflict, signalling international scrutiny of conduct by state and non-state security actors [233].

Demography
Demographic Overview
Since 2 March 2026, more than one million people have been internally displaced in Lebanon, equal to about 20% of the country's
population, according to UNFPA (data as of 1 June 2026) [294]. As of 11 June 2026, 134,800 displaced people (35,700 families) were
registered in 642 collective shelters, with shelters in Beirut and Mount Lebanon at or near capacity and North governorate emerging as
a primary destination for newly displaced families [290][304]. Most displaced people remain outside formal sites in host communities,
informal settings or open spaces, with major arrival and shelter pressure reported in Saida, Tyre, Beirut's southern suburbs, Bekaa and
northern areas such as Akkar and Tripoli [304][306][295].
A digital needs assessment (DNA) conducted mid–April to end-April 2026 (885 households, 4,616 individuals; implying an average
household size of roughly 5.2 persons) found 76.16% of the sample were currently displaced [288]. Among displaced households,
41.39% lived in apartments, 33.23% in formal collective shelters, 17.94% with host families, and 7.44% in tents or informal shelters [288]. In
that assessment sample, 69.94% identified as Lebanese, 24.75% as Syrian, and 5.31% as other nationalities (predominantly Ethiopian
and Sudanese) [288].
IOM/DTM and DTM Syria recorded significant cross-border flows into the Syrian Arab Republic, with 418,589 cumulative movements at
four points of entry and 120,349 arrivals across 1,234 locations between 2 March and 8 June 2026 [309][303].
Of the 1.4 million people targeted in the Flash Appeal, UNFPA estimates approximately 390,000 are women of reproductive age,
including an estimated 16,000 pregnant women, highlighting protection and health service needs among adult female cohorts [294].
Ministry of Public Health casualty data show children accounted for 8% of total casualties since 2 March (247 child deaths and 983
injured), while males constituted the majority of casualties (86%), indicating disproportionate mortality and injury by sex and notable
child impact among conflict-affected cohorts [305].

Education to reduce tensions and build resilience, but funding and access constraints limit scale and sustainability [323][322].
Gender Dynamics
Displacement has disproportionately affected women and girls: since 2 March 2026, an estimated 620,000 women and girls have been
displaced, with 85% living outside formal shelters, increasing protection risks [320][322]. As of 31 May, nearly 52% of people sheltered in
collective sites were women and girls, with 130,369 individuals remaining displaced across 632 collective shelters, pressuring shelter
capacity [345]. Overcrowded, privacy-lacking collective shelters heighten risks such as harassment, exploitation, intimate partner
violence, unsafe access to WASH facilities, and psychosocial distress for women and girls [340][332]. Protection and justice services
have been disrupted, making it harder for women and other at-risk populations to report violations or seek support [322]. Reproductive
health needs are acute: an estimated 390,000 women of reproductive age, including some 16,000 pregnant women, are affected, and
disruptions to maternal services have forced some women to give birth in unsafe conditions [340]. Women-headed households and
other vulnerable groups—including persons with disabilities, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and widows—face elevated housing,
food, and protection vulnerabilities requiring targeted support [331][332]. Gendered economic pressures are driving different coping
patterns: men and boys face intense pressure to provide, debt accumulation, and risky coping, while women face reduced access to
income-generating opportunities due to childcare, mobility, and safety constraints, as well as limited control over household resources
[332]. UNHCR and protection partners report having reached more than 242,500 individuals with targeted protection interventions,
including GBV prevention and response, while gaps in GBV and specialised support are widening due to resource and capacity
constraints [331][323]. Humanitarian responses include GBV prevention and response, case management, multipurpose and regular
cash assistance, mobile SRH and MHPSS services, and shelter upgrades and partitioning to improve privacy, but limited funding and
operational constraints are reducing coverage and leaving significant needs unmet [331][340][347].
Impact of Socio-Cultural Drivers on Aid
Cultural norms and social hierarchies are shaping access to and acceptance of assistance in Lebanon. Displaced households
disproportionately rely on negative financial coping mechanisms: 32.26% of households reported debt as a primary protection
concern, and displaced households account for roughly 75% of debt cases, increasing eviction, legal, and psychosocial risks [332].
Many households also rely on host-family and displaced community solidarity networks for assistance, which affects targeting,
acceptance, and the perceived legitimacy of formal aid [332][362]. SOGIESC populations and women-headed households face
compounded barriers to services, as reported in Oxfam's digital needs assessment (April–May 2026) [332][324].
9
A GBV safety audit of 180 collective shelters found major protection gaps: 28% of shelters lack adequate space and 20% fail to separate
female-headed households from unrelated men, with overcrowding, lack of locks, partitions, and lighting, and non-segregated or
inaccessible WASH facilities heightening GBV risk [340]. Shelter monitoring shows concentrations of vulnerable groups in collective
sites—approximately 6,800 older people (55+), 1,200 people with physical disabilities, 661 with mental disabilities, 386 with hearing
impairments, and 392 with visual impairments—requiring inclusive, accessible, and culturally sensitive modalities and
communications [362]. Access to culturally acceptable SRH and maternal care is further constrained by 182 verified attacks on
healthcare (99 affecting medical transport and 61 affecting facilities), causing 125 deaths and 311 injuries [340]. Sustained pressure on
host communities and the nearly 48,000 displaced migrants facing language barriers and restricted service access continue to
undermine equitable aid reach, as noted in the situation report of 8 June 2026 [345][362]. Humanitarian coordination efforts are
scaling GBV, PSEA, and survivor-centred services through partnerships with MoSA, UNHCR, and local actors, but entrenched social
norms, protection gaps in shelters, healthcare attacks, and language barriers limit effective uptake and equitable distribution of
assistance [340][362].
Environment
Environmental Conditions
Renewed hostilities since 2 March have caused widespread damage to homes, health facilities, roads, agricultural land and critical
infrastructure, disrupting water and electricity services across Lebanon [440]. Hostilities have also sparked extensive fires, and
independent reporting and government submissions note use of white phosphorus, raising risks of immediate fires, respiratory injuries
and long-term soil and water contamination [445][448]. As of early June, nearly one million people remained displaced, placing
significant pressure on local water and sanitation services [440]. Humanitarian operations continue to face constrained access —
including cancelled or delayed missions, route restrictions, communications disruptions, GPS spoofing and unexploded ordnance
contamination — which limit environmental assessments, rubble removal and the scale-up of WASH and contamination-mitigation
activities [432][417].
Environmental Challenges
Use of white phosphorus and other munitions over populated areas has reportedly poisoned soil, water and air, causing long-term
ecosystem damage and severe health effects including burns and respiratory injuries, with government figures linking more than 600
fires in southern Lebanon to white phosphorus use and increasing risks of soil degradation and contamination of agricultural land and
groundwater [441][445][448]. Conflict-generated rubble and debris — Lebanon generated 14.5 million metric tonnes in 2024 — are
compounding waste-management and environmental hazards and require structured rubble-recycling and environmental
management plans to mitigate dust, hazardous contamination and polluted runoff [442][413]. Immediate public-health impacts
include heightened risk of waterborne disease due to water infrastructure damage, contamination and service disruption,
underscoring the need for monitored water quality, safe waste disposal and environmental risk assessments in recovery planning
[445][417]. In response, WASH partners reached approximately 1,072,601 people with emergency water, sanitation and hygiene services
as of 8 June [417][432], delivering 3,765,725 litres of potable water and 107,230 m³ to collective shelters via water trucking, supplying
851,000 litres of fuel to water establishments, and distributing 111,911 hygiene kits, 66,579 menstrual hygiene kits and 1,488 cleaning kits to
affected populations [417].
Environmental Risks and Mitigation
A UNDP geo-rapid assessment in early June 2026 found extensive building damage and debris in Beirut and Mount Lebanon — 146
buildings completely destroyed, 264 partially damaged, 3,168 housing units destroyed and 4,437 damaged — producing an estimated
649,000 cubic metres of debris that poses immediate health, safety and environmental hazards for recovery operations [404].
Unexploded ordnance (UXO), explosive remnants of war (ERW) and unstable structures are impeding safe clearance and
humanitarian access, increasing the need for specialized risk-management and safety protocols during debris removal [417][413].
International guidance recommends people-centred, circular rubble management to reduce environmental harm and speed
reconstruction: prioritizing and pre-identifying removal and reuse sites, segregating hazardous materials (including asbestos and
ERW), securing environmental permissions, and incorporating rubble management costs into response budgets [413][442][435].
UNOPS and related guidance further highlight rubble reuse as a mitigation measure that can cut environmental impacts, lower
reconstruction costs and create local economic opportunities, provided adequate sorting and safety measures are in place [442].
Persistent funding shortfalls in the 2026 Flash Appeal — approximately 32% funded as of 11 June — and reduced in-kind contributions
risk undermining sustained environmental mitigation actions, including safe debris management, restoration of waste and water
systems and monitoring of contamination risks [417][432].
Conflict
Overview of the Ongoing Armed Conflict
Since 2 March 2026, hostilities between Israeli forces and Hezbollah have produced sustained airstrikes, drone and artillery strikes, and
limited ground operations in southern Lebanon, with a notable escalation and geographic expansion of Israeli operations from 26 May
2026 onward [534][554][566]. Fighting has concentrated in southern governorates—particularly South and Nabatiyeh—and has
extended beyond the Litani River into areas south of the Zahrani River, while Mount Lebanon and parts of Beirut's southern suburbs
have also been affected [535][534]. By 1 June 2026, Israeli forces had reportedly occupied roughly 2,000 km² of southern Lebanese
territory [554][534].
Lebanon's Ministry of Public Health (MoPHPHEOC) reported 15,194 total casualties (deaths and injuries) from 2 March to 11 June 2026,
with the South (6,379), Nabatiyeh (6,333), and Mount Lebanon (1,182) the most affected; children comprised 8% of casualties (247 dead,
983 injured), and 94% of casualties were Lebanese (14,297) [535].
10
ACAPS estimated over 3,600 killed and nearly 11,100 injured since 2 March 2026 [534].
Humanitarian actors estimated large-scale displacement and severe protection needs. ACAPS estimated approximately 1.5 million
people displaced nationwide by 7 June 2026 [534], while national DRM dashboards recorded sudden movements on 7 June
2026—approximately 4,000 families displaced in Nabatieh, 30,000 families in South governorate, and ~50,000 families (≈200,000
people) from Beirut's southern suburbs—placing collective shelters at or beyond capacity [535]. A mobility assessment reported
135,669 people sheltering across 636 collective shelters as of 4 June, with thousands more in open spaces [521].
The conflict has significantly degraded critical services and infrastructure: strikes and access constraints have damaged health
facilities and ambulances, reportedly killed and injured medical personnel, left some hospitals unable to accept patients due to
capacity and financial/coordination gaps, and prompted WHO-supported nationwide trauma preparedness and shelter-based
surveillance efforts [534][554][535]. Active hostilities, repeated strikes, and territorial occupation have constrained humanitarian
access, forced temporary evacuation of collective shelters, and pushed many displaced people into open or tented sites with limited
services, complicating relief operations [534][535][554].
Conflict Stakeholders
The principal actors are Hezbollah (a powerful Iran-aligned non-state armed group), the Lebanese state and its armed forces, and
Israel as the external belligerent [580][547][591]. Hezbollah retains significant independent military capacity and political legitimacy,
making disarmament contentious and heightening the risk that forced disarmament could trigger internal instability [547]. On 13 June
2026, Prime Minister Nawaf Salam publicly called for consolidation of state authority and the deployment of the Lebanese Armed
Forces to conflict-affected areas to restore the state's monopoly on force [591]. The Lebanese Armed Forces have also sought to
strengthen external military cooperation, including senior-level talks and visits with partners such as Pakistan, to bolster capacity amid
the security crisis [596].
The United States reduced planned assistance to the Lebanese Army to $36 million for fiscal year 2027, down from $100–200 million
annually in recent years, constraining a key state alternative to Hezbollah's military presence [580]. War-related losses and
reconstruction needs are estimated in the range of approximately $20–25 billion, with extensive infrastructure and property damage
reported [590][582].
Peace Negotiations
Negotiations between the United States and Iran advanced toward a Pakistanbrokered memorandum of understanding that
mediators said would be signed in midJune and that reportedly includes Lebanon in its ceasefire framework [500][522]. Key
mediators and regional actors engaged in the process included Pakistan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey, while the United States led
talks with Iran and hosted parallel Lebanon–Israel negotiations in Washington [522][500][481]. The United States pressed to keep talks
on track and publicly urged Israel to curb strikes in Lebanon to avoid undermining the diplomatic process [606][504].
Despite this diplomatic momentum, Israeli strikes and repeated airspace incursions in southern Lebanon continued. Lebanese
authorities reported 3,491 Israeli ceasefire violations (airspace incursions, strikes, and ground incidents) since 17 April [482], and
Lebanese officials reported 47 Lebanese military and security personnel killed since 2 March [482]. These ongoing violations have
weakened adherence to understandings reached since April [493][482][554].
The main obstacles to a durable ceasefire are Hezbollah's rejection of the arrangements negotiated directly between the Lebanese
Government and Israel, Israel's reluctance to formally link the Lebanon file to broader US–Iran talks, and ongoing onground violations
that reduced incentives for a sustained ceasefire after the early June clashes [529][534][532]. Analysts and briefs warned that any
durable ceasefire in Lebanon is operationally linked to progress in US–Iran negotiations, narrowing the diplomatic window after the 7
June escalation [534][529].
An estimated 620,000 women and girls have been displaced since 2 March, with 85% living outside formal shelters and facing
heightened protection risks including exploitation and genderbased violence, increasing protection risks and complicating recovery
and negotiations [532][529].
Conflict Outlook
The 7 June 2026 Israeli strikes on Beirut's southern suburbs (Dahiyeh) triggered Iran to launch ballistic missiles at Israel in direct
response, illustrating how Lebanese operations now generate wider regional escalation [532][534][519]. Parallel US–Iran military
exchanges and intense diplomatic mediation through Pakistani and Qatari channels left talks reportedly close to a framework
agreement but fragile amid strikes and reprisals in early-to-mid June [550][486][477][542].
Hezbollah's rejection of Lebanon-specific ceasefire arrangements and Israel's reluctance to link the Lebanon file to US–Iran
negotiations significantly reduce the likelihood of a durable ceasefire in the near term and have narrowed the diplomatic window
following the 7 June escalation [534][529]. Progress toward a US–Iran agreement could create incentives for deescalation, but recent
crossborder strikes and Israeli actions in Beirut risk derailing that prospect, making a negotiated settlement possible but fragile and
contingent on sustained external mediation and restraint [486][504][616][534].
Continued hostilities, the operational linkage between the Lebanon front and US–Iran negotiations, and the risk of titfortat strikes
make further largescale displacement and protection deterioration likely in the coming weeks [534][529][521]. Humanitarian
conditions are already severely strained, with thousands sheltering in open spaces and collective shelter systems overstretched,
compounding the humanitarian toll of any further escalation [521].

Displacement
Displacement Overview
As of early June 2026, nearly one million people remained internally displaced in Lebanon, with the vast majority hosted outside
collective shelters [624][677]. Official counts show 130,369 individuals in 632 collective shelters as of 31 May 2026, rising to 135,669
individuals in 636 collective shelters as of 4 June 2026, reflecting recent increases driven by renewed displacement orders [662][621].
Renewed displacement orders in late May and new orders in early June produced sharp spikes in movement: orders around 27 May
and further population movements on 7 June led authorities to report an estimated 4,000 families displaced in Nabatieh and 30,000
families in South governorate, alongside preliminary estimates of approximately 50,000 families displaced from Beirut's southern
suburbs on that day [652][628]. Shelter capacity has been overwhelmed in Saida and Tyre (South Governorate), driving newly
displaced families toward North Governorate and parts of Bekaa where additional shelters and tented sites have been activated
[662][677][669]. Women and girls represent around 52% of those in collective shelters, and nearly 48,000 displaced migrants in
Lebanon face specific vulnerabilities and barriers to services [662][619]. Cross-border flows into the Syrian Arab Republic remained
substantial: IOM/DTM recorded 418,589 movements into Syria at tracked points of entry as of 8 June 2026, with Syrian nationals
comprising about 97% of recorded movements and approximately 32,738 people returning to Lebanon since the 27 April peak [650].
Displacement patterns are highly fluid, with repeated returns and secondary displacement complicating registration and producing
variability between institutional estimates [652]. IOM, UN agencies, and national authorities are scaling Displacement Tracking Matrix
(DTM) monitoring and coordinating under the revised Flash Appeal to track trends, inform prioritization, and adapt the multi-sectoral
response as the situation remains protracted and returns largely temporary and unsustainable [624][638][652].
Living Conditions of the Displaced Population
The vast majority of nearly one million internally displaced people in Lebanon are hosted in informal arrangements outside formal
collective sites, placing sustained pressure on communities and services [624][619]. Collective shelter capacity is critically stretched,
with sites in Beirut, Tyre, and Saida reported at or near full capacity as of early June 2026 [621][628]. Children, older people, persons
with disabilities, and people with chronic conditions face additional barriers to services; the Shelter Monitoring Dashboard recorded
roughly 6,800 older people, 1,200 persons with physical disabilities, 661 persons with mental disabilities, 386 persons with hearing
impairments, and 392 persons with visual impairments in collective shelters [628]. An estimated 620,000 women and girls have been
displaced since 2 March 2026, with approximately 85% living outside formal shelters, heightening protection and GBV risks [628][635].
Non-Lebanese displaced people — Syrians, Palestinians, and migrants — face acute barriers to formal accommodation and access to
services, as well as increased legal insecurity linked to loss of documentation, while UNHCR continues targeted support for birth
registration and residency assistance [652][618].
Access to health care is constrained by facility damage, workforce shortages, and attacks on health care — WHO has recorded 202
attacks since 2 March — and the Ministry of Health reported 3,526 deaths and 10,733 injuries since 2 March (data as of 9 June 2026),
undermining continuity of emergency, trauma, MHPSS, and SRH services [630]. SRH needs are acute, with UNFPA estimating large
numbers of women of reproductive age and pregnant women among the displaced and emphasizing urgent gaps in maternal
services, safe deliveries, and SRH supply provision [667]. WASH and sanitation conditions are poor in many displacement settings, with
a digital needs assessment finding 56.93% of latrines non-functional and pervasive concerns about cleanliness, privacy, and safety in
shared facilities [668]. Food and nutrition support has been scaled but remains insufficient: partners report distributions totalling
approximately 12.6 million meals and 141,192 ready-to-eat rations since 2 March, yet around 80% of collective shelters are providing
only one hot meal per day and minimum food security needs are unmet in many sites [632].
Psychosocial distress and protection needs are widespread; protection partners and UNHCR have reached hundreds of thousands
with interventions, but gaps remain for GBV, MHPSS, and specialized child and disability services, and UNFPA reports urgent needs for
dignity kits and expanded safe-space and mobile GBV services [618][667][668]. Cash assistance is being scaled — MoSA shared
beneficiary lists covering roughly 100,000 households for E-MPCA Batch C, and UNHCR/WFP reached over 137,000 refugee households
with EMPCA in May — while significant funding shortfalls persist for the emergency response, constraining longer-term income support
and shelter solutions [632][618][628]. Humanitarian operations are further constrained by security, access, and logistical challenges,
with 203 HNS-supported humanitarian movements recorded between 2 March and 11 June and multiple cancellations, limiting delivery
and scale-up of multi-sector assistance and reinforcing the need for urgent, flexible funding to sustain life-saving services [669][628].
Drivers of Displacement
The primary driver of displacement is renewed hostilities since 2 March 2026 — including Israeli attacks and evacuation orders — which
have forced more than one million people to flee their homes [668][675][661]. Widespread destruction of homes, hospitals, schools,
and other critical infrastructure has reduced access to basic services, with about 17 schools destroyed and roughly 500,000 children
out of classrooms, further impeding return and recovery [675]. Humanitarian assessments identified livelihoods and food as the top
needs of displaced and host households, and reported heightened protection risks including increased exposure to harassment,
exploitation, gender-based violence, and psychosocial distress among displaced populations [668][631].
Pre-existing socioeconomic collapse and loss of livelihoods have compounded these displacement drivers, with sharp price shocks —
bread prices up approximately 15% and fuel prices up approximately 84% between mid-February and mid-May 2026 — contributing to
projected acute food insecurity affecting about 1.24 million people (IPC, 29 April 2026) [652][643]. Regional spillover and deteriorating
security in host countries have also contributed to return movements from Lebanon and Iran to Syria and Afghanistan, often under
adverse conditions [641][626]. Secondary displacement is a persistent dynamic, as returnees frequently face deteriorating conditions
in areas of origin — including ongoing hostilities, damaged housing, and lack of services — leading to repeated and largely
unsustainable returns [652].

Integration
Large-scale, prolonged displacement and heavy reliance on informal hosting arrangements are constraining integration prospects
and placing sustained pressure on host services and communities in Lebanon [619][632]. Social cohesion is under strain, with reports
documenting rising host community tensions, perceptions of displaced populations' affiliations increasing mistrust, and risks of
host-community exhaustion; social-stability partners have delivered community dialogue, explosive ordnance risk education, and
other engagement activities to mitigate these tensions [652][668][635][632].
Education access is severely disrupted, with 446 schools non-operational as of 28 May, though Education Sector partners reached
262,384 displaced children through formal modalities (94,073 in-person; 163,420 online) and temporary learning hubs have been
established to mitigate loss of schooling [652][632][669]. Health and social services are overstretched: WHO reported widening gaps,
medicine and workforce shortages, and an estimated 13,500 displaced pregnant women in shelters, while IOM and partners have
supported access to essential health services for tens of thousands (IOM: 18,394) and WHO has delivered trauma and surgical kits to
priority hospitals [630][619]. Protection and MHPSS capacity has expanded but remains insufficient — UNHCR and partners provided
MHPSS to nearly 8,700 people and IOM to 4,301 since 2 March, alongside national MHPSS outreach, trainings, and referrals — yet gaps in
GBV and specialized support are widening [618][619][644].
Displaced households face mounting financial pressures from rising rents, limited income opportunities, and depleted savings,
undermining access to durable livelihoods [618][627]. Humanitarian livelihood programming includes IOM-led emergency
cash-for-work targeting 14,400 individuals and other livelihood activities within the Flash Appeal [624][627]. Government and
institutional responses show active engagement: the Ministry of Social Affairs shared beneficiary lists for emergency MPCA covering
approximately 100,000 households and is coordinating with humanitarian actors; IOM and MoSA are co-leading Site Management and
Coordination; and partners are working to harmonize targeting and transition emergency cash into regular MPCA from June, while
UNHCR is implementing cash-for-shelter, shelter rehabilitation, and tenure-support measures to sustain longer-term settlement
options [632][619][618][624][622][627].
Humanitarian Access
Humanitarian Access Overview
Humanitarian access in Lebanon remained highly constrained and increasingly reliant on notification mechanisms despite the
declared ceasefire, with movements to areas south of the Litani River and parts of Nabatieh continuing amid heightened insecurity
and operational unpredictability [702][695]. With support from the Humanitarian Notification System (HNS), partners facilitated 184
humanitarian movements between 2 March and 8 June and 203 movements by 11 June, the majority south of the Litani, coordinated
with UNIFIL and civil-military notification arrangements [702][695]. Operational challenges—including delays, restricted operational
windows, route constraints, communications disruptions (including GPS spoofing), unexploded ordnance (UXO) contamination, and
cancellations—frequently disrupted missions; between 26 May and 9 June, 22 movements (primarily to Tyre District) were cancelled
and one postponed [695]. Primary entry points for humanitarian cargo remained the Port of Beirut and the Al Masnaa border crossing,
while domestic convoy movements to hard-to-reach southern locations were coordinated with WFP and the Logistics
Cluster—planned convoys included Sarba (9 June), Rmeish (11 June) and Borj el Moulouk (12 June) in Nabatieh governorate [702].
Coordination bodies including the Inter-Sector Coordination Group (ISCG), Operational Coordination Groups (OCGs), the Ministry of
Social Affairs and the Disaster Risk Management unit continued to monitor displacement and harmonize targeting and operational
modalities to inform and facilitate humanitarian movements under these constraints [702][695].
Barriers to Humanitarian Access
Multiple overlapping barriers continue to severely restrict humanitarian operations across Lebanon. Key physical and logistics
obstacles include destroyed bridges—notably over the Litani River—that have severed connectivity between Sour (Tyre) and Nabatiye
and isolated over 150,000 people, while hard-to-reach areas, UXO contamination, threats of additional infrastructure destruction,
growing domestic route congestion, and supply-chain strain compound operational difficulties [704][702]. Sustained attacks on
health care have sharply reduced service availability: since 2 March, 196 attacks have been recorded (131 deaths, 379 injuries),
including 50 attacks since the 17 April ceasefire and a 1 June attack near Jabal Amel Hospital that injured 39 health workers; as a result,
42 primary health care centres and 3 hospitals have been closed and 17 hospitals damaged [737][703]. Affordability barriers, medicine
shortages, workforce shortages and reduced specialized services further limit access to care, and the Health Sector estimates an
urgent need of USD 61.5 million under the Revised Flash Appeal with only USD 12.8 million currently available—representing
approximately 17% of revised Flash Appeal funding (June–August 2026) [703][702]. Displacement and shelter constraints also impede
assistance: nearly one million people are internally displaced, the vast majority hosted informally outside collective sites, while roughly
135,000 remain in collective shelters where overcrowding, poor sanitation, lack of privacy and dependence on host families elevate
protection risks [699][714][693]. Negative coping strategies were reported by 32.26% of households, and protection emerged as a
major need among displaced respondents (16.38%) in assessments [699][714][693]. Shelter populations include significant numbers
of people with specific vulnerabilities: approximately 6,800 older people (55+), 1,200 persons with physical disabilities, 661 persons with
mental disabilities, 386 persons with hearing impairments, and 392 persons with visual impairments in collective shelters [700].
Non-Lebanese displaced populations and an estimated 48,000 displaced migrants face additional legal and administrative
barriers—including movement restrictions, risk of arrest, repeated displacement, and government restrictions on access to shelter and
basic services—that further limit their ability to access assistance [700][704][730].
Political and social obstacles include perceptions of displaced people's affiliation with armed groups and intensified competition over
scarce resources, which have fuelled sectarian polarisation, social tension, discrimination, and localized harassment or attacks
against displaced persons [704].

Administrative bottlenecks and an absence of state-led displacement data produce information gaps and delays, making targeting
and timely scale-up dependent on humanitarian assessments [704]. Operational constraints are further compounded by sharply
reduced in-kind donation volumes (reported at approximately 18% of previous levels) and wide funding shortfalls across appeals,
including IOM's revised request of USD 42.37 million and UNFPA's appeal of USD 25 million with approximately 16% funded, collectively
undermining the capacity to expand and sustain assistance at scale [702][697][730][728].
Humanitarian Access Gaps
Ongoing hostilities have produced large-scale internal displacement and severely strained humanitarian access across Lebanon.
Nearly one million people are internally displaced, with IOM estimating over 48,000 displaced migrants who face specific barriers to
assistance and often avoid government-managed collective sites [730][699][712]. As of 4 June 2026, 135,669 people remained
displaced across 636 crowded collective shelters, with thousands more in informal arrangements [718]. Displacement is concentrated
from southern areas toward Beirut, Mount Lebanon and northern governorates (Akkar, Baalbek-Hermel), and returns are largely
temporary and unsustainable, undermining access planning [704]. South and Nabatiye governorates face worsening food insecurity
at IPC Phase 3 through at least September 2026, driven by market collapse and constrained humanitarian access [704]. Humanitarian
delivery is impeded by limited access to healthcare, shelter and WASH services, overcrowding in hosting areas, and stretched service
points and response capacities [699][706]. Migrants face language, information and status-related barriers that reduce uptake of
assistance, while women and girls face heightened protection risks including gender-based violence (GBV), and children are reporting
elevated psychosocial distress [730][704][706]. Key sectors with critical shortfalls include health, shelter, WASH, food security,
protection (including GBV response), mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), livelihoods and humanitarian border
management [730][699]. Most displaced people rely on informal hosting outside formal sites, limiting humanitarian reach and
monitoring. In response, humanitarian actors are calling for expanded multi-sectoral assistance, strengthened mobility and
early-warning data, and targeted site-management support; IOM is supporting NGOs on site management for migrants, and Oxfam's
April–May needs assessment is being used to refine targeting and programme design [699][730][693][706][707].

Information on the Lebanon escalation is compiled from coordinated inter-agency and government sources (OCHA/ISCG flash
updates and operational coordination groups), national authorities (MoSA, DRM, MoPH), UN agencies (UNHCR, IOM, UNFPA),
international NGOs (IRC), and community-facing digital assessments, which are used together to inform response planning and
monitoring [781][765][786][797][806][774]. Key operational datasets and tools in use include the Emergency Rapid Needs Assessment
(ERNA), real-time digital dashboards tracking displacement, aid distribution and in-kind donation flows,
site-management/occupancy trackers, and complaint-and-feedback monitoring systems used by site-management actors
[781][765][797]. IRC reported more than 1 million people displaced as of 8 June 2026 [806], while OCHA/ISCG situational reporting
described nearly one million internally displaced people and approximately 48,000 displaced migrants in early June, with 134,800
people registered in 639 collective shelters as of 8 June 2026 [777][765]. WASH sector partners reported reaching 1,074,702 people as
of 11 June and delivering 851,000 litres of fuel since 2 March to sustain public water services for an estimated 841,128 people [781].
Coordination and data-sharing practices have been intensified since the revised Flash Appeal (launched 5 June), with ISCG/OCGs
working with MoSA and DRM to harmonize targeting and operational modalities, and sectors reviewing indicators to align with SMART
criteria to strengthen response monitoring [781][765]. UNFPA and partners plan deployments of rapid needs-assessment teams and
intend to boost national and UN information-management capacity to support continuous field assessments and sector coordination
[773]. Data reliability remains constrained by access and political limitations that can filter institutional reporting, while open-source
and community reporting have expanded coverage but can diverge from institutional datasets; humanitarian actors are calling for
strengthened mobility and displacement monitoring and early-warning systems to improve timeliness and validation [793][777].
Communication Channels
Oxfam's Digital Needs Assessment (April–May 2026) found 78.98% of surveyed households preferred WhatsApp as their feedback
mechanism, with face-to-face reporting second at 11.98% [764]. Oxfam recommends prioritising accessible, secure WhatsApp-based
feedback channels while retaining limited in-person options for people without reliable digital access or privacy [764]. OCHA Flash
Updates noted that humanitarian access is increasingly reliant on notification mechanisms; the Humanitarian Notification System
(HNS) supported 203 humanitarian movements between 2 March and 11 June, though missions frequently faced delays,
communications disruptions, and GPS spoofing [781]. OCHA-coordinated logistics movements included convoys to Sarba (9 June),
Rmeish (11 June), and Borj el Moulouk (12 June) to deliver food and life-saving assistance in Nabatieh governorate [765]. WHO and the
Lebanon Health Sector called for expanded risk communication and community engagement, and reported support for radio/CBRN
preparedness alongside urgent needs to bolster surveillance and risk communication capacities [795][785]. Displaced residents are
also using commercial satellite imagery to check on home damage where physical access remains restricted [790].
Barriers to Information
Multiple overlapping barriers limit information access for affected populations in Lebanon. According to Oxfam's Digital Needs
Assessment (April–May 2026), while WhatsApp is the preferred feedback channel, many women lack personal phones, share devices,
or have no privacy to report sensitive issues; migrant women face language barriers; and older persons and persons with disabilities
often prefer verbal or face-to-face channels [764]. The assessment also notes selection bias: households with limited phone or
internet access, low digital literacy, or unstable communication channels were less likely to participate, excluding some of the most
vulnerable from digital information and feedback systems [764].

Poor internet connectivity, lack of devices, and limited learning spaces further restrict digital information access and remote outreach
for displaced students and remote learners [766][765]. According to IOM, over 48,000 displaced migrants in Lebanon face language
constraints, limited information on services, and fears linked to migration status that reduce their access to information and
assistance [797]. Infrastructure and operational disruptions — including repeated power outages, burning of power poles, road
closures, communications disruptions, GPS spoofing, unexploded ordnance contamination, and cancelled or delayed humanitarian

Journalists have been killed, displaced, or cut off from information sources, and misinformation risks have risen, narrowing the public

Misinformation and Rumors
Social media and peer comments are major vectors of harmful information that can reshape perceptions of health care, with a small
number of highly active or inauthentic accounts able to dominate discussions and influence wider public sentiment [793]. Many
people in the region rely heavily on social networking sites for news, a consumption pattern that algorithms and viral content can
amplify into sensational or misleading narratives [813]. Harmful information has eroded trust in health services and can discourage
care-seeking, prompting the Health Sector and the Ministry of Public Health to call for expanded risk communication, community
engagement, and public information to restore confidence and encourage early care-seeking [793][785]. Operational efforts to
counter misinformation include MoPH hotlines and helplines, sector-led public information dissemination (24 public information
activities recorded), and UNHCR and partners' outreach reaching tens of thousands through information sessions and awareness
activities to provide verified guidance and protection messaging [791][765][769]. Health workers and community leaders — identified
as influential, trusted voices — are being engaged in outreach and GBV/SRH awareness campaigns, while MHPSS actors are using
social media posts and digital tools to share verified psychosocial support and referral information [773][792].
Accountability Needs and Transparency
Inter-agency coordination has prioritized strengthening response monitoring through real-time digital dashboards tracking
displacement, aid distribution, and in-kind donation flows to improve transparency and accountability [765]. The Humanitarian
Notification System supported 203 humanitarian movements between 2 March and 11 June, facilitating delivery of life-saving
assistance but remaining constrained by insecurity and operational unpredictability [781]. Awareness of complaint mechanisms
remains critically low: according to Oxfam's Digital Needs Assessment, 69.3% of surveyed households did not know how to file a
complaint, with awareness lowest among returned households (22.43% aware) and displaced households (31.26% aware) [764].
Feedback channel preferences show 78.98% of households prefer WhatsApp (59.89% of displaced households), 11.98% prefer
face-to-face, and SMS and other channels account for under 9%, indicating a strong need to prioritize secure WhatsApp channels
while retaining limited in-person options [764]. UNFPA is leading enhancements to collective PSEA and accountability to affected
people (AAP) as host agency for the Inter-Agency PSEA Network and the AAP Working Group [773]. The Government of Lebanon relief
platform demonstrates large-scale digital registration potential, with over 1 million people self-registered as displaced as of 1 April,
which could be leveraged for information sharing and feedback mechanisms [782]. Key gaps undermining accountability include
widespread lack of awareness on complaint channels, constrained access due to insecurity, and severe funding shortfalls that limit
partners' capacity to maintain monitoring and AAP systems — the initial 2026 Flash Appeal achieved approximately 60.3% coverage
(March–May), and the revised appeal left a funding gap of approximately USD 454 million at extension outset [764][781][765][766].
The Health Sector has specifically called for expanded risk communication and community engagement to improve awareness of
services and referrals, reinforcing the need for investment in accessible, context-appropriate information and feedback channels
[785].
Legal and Policy
Legal and Policy Framework
The Government of Lebanon is leading the national humanitarian response under the Lebanon Response Plan (LRP), with the Ministry of
Social Affairs (MoSA) as the focal point and close coordination with UN agencies (OCHA, UNDP, UNHCR) and humanitarian partners.
UNHCR co-leads key sectors including Protection and Shelter and co-chairs cash coordination mechanisms alongside MoSA and
NGOs [861][869][886]. IOM, working with MoSA, the Ministry of Public Health, and the Ministry of Interior, supports site management,
displacement tracking (DTM), and sector coordination to align humanitarian interventions with national authorities [876][852].
International humanitarian law protections for health care — including Geneva Conventions principles and UNSCR 2286 — are being
referenced amid repeated attacks on medical facilities and personnel [894]. Calls for accountability and strengthened legal recourse
have been made by rights organisations, including recommendations that Lebanon ratify the Rome Statute and adopt domestic
legislation criminalising war crimes and crimes against humanity to enable investigations and prosecutions [860][878]. The UN
human rights office (OHCHR) announced deployment of an independent assessment mission to Lebanon in mid-June 2026 to
document alleged violations of international humanitarian law and international human rights law by all parties since 2 March, with
findings expected at the end of July 2026 [875][882][897][879]. Ceasefire and mediation efforts have proceeded through negotiated,
external mediation including a US-brokered ceasefire agreement in mid-April, but rejection of some government-brokered
arrangements by armed actors and the linkage of the Lebanon file to wider US–Iran negotiations have undermined prospects for a
durable ceasefire and complicated legal-political pathways to conflict resolution [858][853].

Humanitarian Impact of Legislation
Lebanese authorities have restricted access to shelter and basic services for non-Lebanese populations, including displaced Syrians,
Palestinian refugees, and migrant workers [858]. Non-Lebanese displaced people report movement restrictions, risk of arrest, and
repeated displacement that further limit access to services and livelihoods [863][858]. IOM estimates nearly 48,000 displaced
migrants face language barriers and restricted service access, with many unable or unwilling to use government-managed collective
sites, increasing their vulnerability [855][876]. Many displaced and returnee populations also face lost, damaged, or expired civil
documents, creating legal insecurity and limiting access to public services and assistance [884][873]. In May 2026, UNHCR assisted 221
refugee children with birth registration and 144 refugees to access legal residency, reflecting ongoing documentation support needs
[869]. An estimated 620,000 displaced women and girls — 85% of whom live outside formal shelters — face heightened protection
risks, while disrupted justice and support services hinder reporting and assistance [853]. UNHCR and protection partners have reached
over 242,500 people with targeted protection interventions since the escalation, but the revised Flash Appeal remained severely
underfunded at US$206.2 million (32.2% of the US$639.9 million requested as of 11 June), constraining the scale and continuity of legal
assistance and protection programming [869][856]. The WHO Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care recorded 203 attacks in
Lebanon resulting in 135 deaths and 394 injuries among on-duty healthcare workers as of 11 June 2026, with multiple hospitals and
primary health centres closed, indicating breaches of IHL and sharply reduced health system capacity [856][903]. Repeated strikes
damaging hospitals, ambulances, and health personnel have delayed emergency response times and constrained aid delivery,
undermining protection for patients and humanitarian workers [858][903]. Increased threats to peacekeepers and humanitarian staff
— including multiple UNIFIL peacekeeper fatalities between 29 March and 3 June — further exacerbate operational risks for aid delivery
[858].
International Agreements Impacting the Crisis
Multiple US-brokered ceasefire arrangements have been announced since 16 April 2026, but implementation has been inconsistent.
Hezbollah rejected key arrangements reached through direct Lebanese government–Israel negotiations, reducing prospects for a
durable ceasefire [875][862][853][858]. The 7 June escalation — including Iran's ballistic-missile response to strikes in Lebanon —
demonstrates that the Lebanon front is linked to broader US–Iran negotiations, a dynamic that has narrowed diplomatic space for
stabilizing agreements [853][858]. International actors and regional groups have called for respect for international law and
implementation of relevant UNSC resolutions, including calls to implement UNSC Resolution 2803, and policy proposals have urged
stronger measures to address impunity for violations of IHL, but operationalizing these demands has been limited to date [917][907].
UN peacekeeping assets are operational but under strain: three UNIFIL peacekeepers were killed on 29–30 March and a fourth on 3
June, and humanitarian movements increasingly rely on coordination with UNIFIL and the Humanitarian Notification System (HNS) to
access southern areas [858][864]. Between 2 March and 11 June 2026, 203 HNS-supported humanitarian movements were facilitated,
including 170 missions south of the Litani River, but operations faced cancellations, delays, route constraints, communications
disruptions, and UXO contamination — including 22 missions to Tyre District cancelled between 26 May and 9 June due to security risks
[864]. Humanitarian coordination is led under the Lebanon Response Plan, with MoSA, UNHCR, OCHA, and UNDP co-leading inter-sector
groups and protection/shelter clusters [869][861]. The initial 2026 Flash Appeal (March–May) reached 60.3% coverage (USD 185.9
million of USD 308.3 million requested), with in-kind donations at just 18% of prior volumes, and the revised appeal (June–August)
raised total requirements to USD 639.9 million with a major funding gap remaining [852][858]. Despite government engagement with
UN coordination mechanisms, the combination of constrained access, funding gaps, and regional linkage to US–Iran talks has limited
the effective implementation of ceasefire arrangements and other international measures addressing the crisis [875][869][864].
Limitations of Current Policies
The Government's inability to fully manage internal displacement and administrative bottlenecks mean displacement data is largely
produced through humanitarian assessments rather than state-led systems, creating persistent information gaps that delay and
complicate response planning [858]. Widespread infrastructure damage — including destruction of key bridges over the Litani River
that have severed connectivity and isolated over 150,000 people — has compounded access constraints for civilians and responders
[858]. Protection and GBV responses are insufficiently covered by current policies and capacities, particularly given the scale of
displacement and the concentration of vulnerable populations outside formal shelters [853]. Accountability and legal gaps persist at
the policy level: Lebanon has yet to ratify the Rome Statute or enact domestic legislation criminalizing war crimes and crimes against
humanity, limiting avenues for investigation and prosecution of alleged violations documented since 2 March 2026 [860][875].
Health-care protection and service provision are critically weakened: WHO and health-sector partners recorded 196 attacks on health
services since 2 March 2026, resulting in 131 deaths and 379 injuries, with many facilities operating at reduced capacity and limiting
access to essential care [880]. Severe funding shortfalls continue to erode response capacity across all sectors, with the initial Flash
Appeal achieving only 60.3% coverage and the revised appeal facing a substantial gap against USD 639.9 million in requirements
[852][858]. Together, these structural, legal, and operational gaps underscore the need for policy reforms including domestic
accountability legislation, improved civil documentation systems, dedicated protection frameworks for marginalized displaced
populations, and sustained international funding commitments.

Sectoral Analysis
Food Security and Livelihoods
Access to Food and Market Functionality
Food security in Lebanon is worsening, with acute food insecurity projected to affect approximately 1.24 million people (up from
874,000 before the March escalation), driven by displacement, income loss, and rising living costs [939]. By mid-May 2026, overall food
prices were 10–20% above 2025 levels, bread prices rose about 15% after the March escalation, and fuel prices increased roughly 84%
between mid-February and mid-May 2026, amplifying transport and distribution costs [939]. Market functionality has collapsed or is
severely constrained in southern areas, with crisis (IPC Phase 3) levels expected across South and Nabatiye governorates through
September 2026 due to sustained attacks, collapsed markets, and constrained humanitarian access [939]. Attacks and infrastructure
damage — including the 8 April strike on the Qasmieh Bridge, which damaged the last operational bridge linking areas south of the
Litani River — have disrupted supply chains, damaged roads and bridges, and isolated more than 150,000 people, limiting commercial
and humanitarian access [939]. While most markets across areas of operation continue to function, affordability and eroding
purchasing power are increasingly undermining households' ability to meet needs and to fully use cash assistance, and movement
restrictions in the Israeli-declared military zone affect an estimated 28,500 people [951][939]. Local food availability is further
threatened by weakened agriculture — more than 22% of agricultural land damaged and 76% of farmers in southern Lebanon affected
— combined with prior drought (2025 rains approximately 50% below average) and a likely 10% below-average rainfall forecast for
June–August 2026 [939].
Coping Mechanisms
Harmful coping strategies are increasing among displaced and non-Lebanese households. According to Oxfam's Digital Needs
Assessment (April–May 2026), 32.26% of households reported using debt as a financial coping mechanism, with displaced households
accounting for roughly 75% of these cases; rent-related debt is driving eviction risks and psychosocial distress [928]. UNHCR and IOM
reporting indicate negative coping strategies include reduced household spending, child labour, early and forced marriage, and
transactional or survival sex, as shelter and accommodation options shrink [939][940]. Food consumption has been widely reduced in
collective sites, with around 80% of shelters providing only one hot meal per day [944][951]. Many returnees from Lebanon lack
immediate work opportunities, and households are increasingly relying on savings, remittances, loans, and external assistance to
bridge income gaps, with casual daily labour being the most common income source where available [962][979]. To mitigate these
negative coping strategies, UNHCR and cash-sector partners have scaled emergency cash responses, including emergency
protection cash and multipurpose cash assistance (e.g., 13,550 EPCA recipients and integration of refugee households into
MPCA/EMPCA reaching tens to hundreds of thousands), though needs remain unmet [940].
Livelihood Disruption
Livelihoods across Lebanon have been severely disrupted by the combination of conflict, displacement, and economic shocks. Oxfam's
April 2026 digital needs assessment found livelihoods to be the top priority among respondents (47.12%), with displaced households
disproportionately affected [928]. Price shocks — including food prices rising 10–20% by mid-May 2026 versus 2025 and fuel prices
increasing approximately 84% between mid-February and mid-May 2026 — have significantly eroded household purchasing power,
raised transport and production costs, and reduced employment prospects [939][956].
Key economic sectors have been hard hit. Tourism and related services have contracted sharply, with airport arrivals down 40.2% and
total passenger traffic down 34.2% in the first five months of 2026, reducing incomes for hotels, restaurants, transport, and service
workers [956]. The industrial sector is reported to be contracting by 45–50%, with factory shutdowns, rising closures, and loss of daily
and seasonal jobs as employers struggle to pay salaries [963]. Agriculture, construction, and manufacturing — sectors with limited
flexibility — have been hit by damage to agricultural land and evacuation orders affecting 76% of farmers in southern Lebanon, further
undermining local income sources [939][956].
In response, humanitarian actors are scaling cash-based livelihood support. The Cash Working Group mobilized MPCA targeting and
disbursements starting 9 June, and IOM's Flash Appeal included emergency cash-for-work livelihoods activities targeting 14,400
individuals with USD 1,000,000 to provide short-term income [951][954].
Impact on Agricultural Activities and Production
Lebanon's agricultural sector has been severely affected by compounding shocks since 2019. By 27 March, more than 22% of
agricultural land had been damaged and Israeli evacuation orders affected 76% of farmers in southern Lebanon, directly reducing
local production capacity [939]. The escalation has driven large-scale displacement of over 1 million people nationally, which,
together with evacuation orders, has removed labour from farming communities and risks longer-term loss of agricultural activity in
affected southern areas [930][939]. Agriculture, together with construction and manufacturing, accounted for approximately 16% of
GDP in 2024, amplifying the broader economic consequences of agricultural disruption [956][967].
Infrastructure destruction has compounded these impacts: attacks have destroyed water systems, roads, bridges, and transport
routes, disrupting supply chains and isolating conflict-affected communities [939]. Rising input and transport costs — with fuel prices
up approximately 84% between mid-February and mid-May 2026 — are increasing agricultural production costs and further reducing
the purchasing power of displaced and refugee households [939].
Climatic conditions add further pressure. Lebanon experienced drought in 2025 with rains approximately 50% below average that
damaged crops; forecasts indicate a likely 10% below-average rainfall for June–August 2026, and expected El Niño conditions over
2026–2027 could aggravate medium-term agricultural impacts [939].

Food and Livelihood Needs and Current Response
Since 2 March, Food Security and Agriculture partners have distributed over 13.3 million hot and cold meals, approximately 145,436
ready-to-eat (RTE) rations, and roughly 170,286 bread bundles, alongside food parcels and core relief items to displaced and affected
households [951][940]. As of 8 June, 134,800 people (35,600 families) were registered in 639 collective shelters; due to resource
constraints, approximately 80% of shelters are providing only one hot meal per day rather than the recommended two, and partners
report that minimum food security requirements are not being met across the majority of sites [927][944]. An RTE pipeline shortfall is
projected from mid-June, with estimated gaps of approximately 700 RTEs in the North and 384 in Akkar [927][944][951].
Cash modalities have been scaled up significantly. UNHCR and WFP reached over 137,000 vulnerable refugees with emergency
multi-purpose cash assistance by May, and the Ministry of Social Affairs shared beneficiary lists covering approximately 100,000
households for Batch C of a blanket E-MPCA [940][944][954]. The Cash Working Group scheduled targeting and disbursements to
commence from 9 June, while IOM's appeal includes MPCA for 49,500 individuals (USD 6.0 million) and livelihoods activities targeting
14,400 people (USD 1.0 million) [954]. A needs assessment by Oxfam reports displaced households prioritize food baskets (49.5%) and
RTE kits (9.22%), and identifies livelihoods and cash as the primary need to cover rent, fuel, infant items, and other essentials, with
disproportionate impacts on women-headed households [928].
Coordination mechanisms are active: ISCG and Operational Coordination Groups work closely with MoSA and the Disaster Risk
Management unit, real-time digital dashboards track displacement and aid flows, and sector working groups — including the Cash
Working Group, UNHCR, and IOM — are harmonizing targeting and operational modalities with government counterparts
[951][927][944][932][954]. Critical funding gaps persist: the revised Flash Appeal for March–May 2026 was only 60.3% funded (USD
185.9 million of USD 308.3 million), and in-kind donations are at approximately 18% of prior levels, underscoring that scale-up of both
cash and in-kind assistance remains urgently required [927][944][951].
Education
Learning Environment and Facility Conditions
As of 11 June, 446 of Lebanon's 3,341 schools (13.3%) remain used as shelters or otherwise nonoperational — including 331 public, 66
private, and 49 technical institutes — substantially reducing available education infrastructure and safe learning spaces [999][993].
UN agencies have reported widespread physical damage to education infrastructure: UNESCO estimated around 500,000 children
forced out of classrooms and more than one million learners affected, with 17 schools destroyed and over 100 damaged, while a
UNDPled geoAI rapid assessment identified 146 destroyed and 264 partially damaged buildings in Beirut and Mount Lebanon
[992][1002][993]. Temporary Learning Hubs (TLHs) and examination hubs have been established to preserve continuity of learning —
the Ministry of Education (MEHE/CERD) ran exam hubs in four priority locations (Aley, Chouf, Saida, and Beirut) enrolling approximately
600 Grade12 students — and the sector plans to scale up TLHs ahead of the new school year [999][991]. Key barriers to restoring
functional learning environments include overcrowded shelters, poor internet connectivity, lack of devices, and limited learning space,
which are driving learning loss and increasing protection risks for girls and children with disabilities [993][991][999].
Access to Education and Barriers
Ongoing insecurity, repeated evacuation orders, and mass displacement have severely disrupted access to schooling for children
across Lebanon. UNICEF reported that by April the conflict had affected nearly 265,000 students, while UNESCO estimated up to 500,000
children forced out of classrooms [993][992]. As of 8–11 June, Education Sector partners reported reaching 262,384 displaced children
through formal modalities — 94,073 inperson and 163,420 online — plus approximately 4,910 children through nonformal activities
[991][999]. Key barriers to enrolment and attendance include overcrowded shelters and shared learning spaces, poor internet
connectivity, lack of devices, and limited learning space, producing significant learning loss and heightened protection risks
particularly for girls and children with disabilities [993][991][999]. Eroding household purchasing power and affordability challenges,
together with connectivity and device shortages, are further constraining families' ability to support children's continued attendance
and limiting the scale and timeliness of education interventions [991][999].
Education Needs and Current Response
Urgent needs include school rehabilitation, expanded temporary learning spaces, provision of devices and connectivity, targeted
psychosocial support, and inclusive accommodations for children with disabilities [992][993]. In response, humanitarian actors have
continued delivery of inperson and online formal and nonformal learning, with sector plans to expand TLHs ahead of the new school
year to prevent prolonged learning disruption [999][991]. Persistent funding shortfalls and access constraints are limiting the scale,
timeliness, and geographic reach of education interventions, leaving underserved areas and displaced cohorts at heightened risk
[991][1002].
Health
Availability and Access to Healthcare Services
Health facility capacity has been severely reduced by repeated attacks since 2 March: around 17 hospitals have sustained damage,
42–44 primary health care centres have closed, and at least three hospitals remain fully non-operational, with several unable to
resume secondary or maternity services [1035][1041]. Attacks on health care have continued despite ceasefires, causing direct
casualties among health workers — including 39 injured at Jabal Amel Hospital on 1 June — and contributing to workforce shortages
and reduced service availability [1035][1041]. Over 130 ambulances and emergency vehicles have also been damaged, further
hindering timely emergency response and patient transport [1039].
18
Maternal health access is acutely constrained: UNFPA estimates approximately 16,000 pregnant women among displaced populations
(roughly 1,800 births expected per month), while WHO estimated around 13,500 displaced pregnant women in shelters with
approximately 1,500 expected deliveries in the coming month, and multiple hospitals remain non-operational or restricted for
deliveries [1043][1034][1035].
Health Sector partners have delivered roughly 387,000 consultations for displaced people and provided medications to about 142,600
patients through some 197 PHCs [1048][1034][1041]. WHO and partners have distributed emergency trauma and surgical supplies —
including 120 trauma backpacks, 20 trauma/surgery kits, and 15 ICU ventilators — and shipped over 134 m³ (approximately 3.5 tonnes)
of medical supplies to frontline facilities [1048][1034][1041]. WHO is also procuring generators and UPS units (due within weeks) and
NCD supplies (expected by late June) to support facility resilience [1034][1045].
Critical shortages and stock-outs persist across medicines, EmONC, and trauma items; sexual and reproductive health services
remain limited; and specialised care is reduced, prompting urgent calls to replenish supplies and scale up emergency services
[1034][1035][1033]. Affordability and transport remain major barriers: although MoPH and partners have expanded subsidised or free
PHC services and waived emergency room fees, many patients still incur significant out-of-pocket costs, humanitarian access to
hard-to-reach areas is constrained by security concerns and cancelled convoys, and rising fuel and transport costs further limit
patients' ability to reach care [1035][1036][1048][1063]. Funding shortfalls are constraining service continuity and scale-up: the Health
Sector appealed for an additional USD 61.5 million under the Revised Flash Appeal, with only USD 12.8 million currently available, and
overall Flash Appeal coverage remains low, limiting partners' capacity to sustain lifesaving services [1033][1041][1036].
Disease Outbreaks
Communicable disease risks in Lebanon increased in early June 2026 amid massive displacement and strained WASH and health
services, with nearly one million people displaced and the majority hosted outside formal collective sites, creating overcrowded
conditions that heighten outbreak risk [1058][1053][1038]. Attacks on health care since 2 March have continued to erode system
capacity — with WHO recording around 202–203 attacks and multiple PHC and hospital closures alongside hundreds of health-worker
casualties — significantly reducing service availability for prevention, surveillance, and treatment [1034][1033][1042].
Shelter- and community-based surveillance has been scaled up but remains inadequate relative to risk. IOM reported strengthening
community-based surveillance with 141 reports submitted to the Ministry of Health, while WHO supported activation of shelter-based
monitoring and surge staffing for the national hotline to improve early detection [1053][1034][1041]. A specific exposure event of
concern involves at least 32 reported rabies exposures in recent weeks, with planned distribution of rabies awareness materials to all
shelters to reduce transmission risk [1041].
Response measures in early June included WHO provision of trauma backpacks, emergency surgery kits, and ICU ventilators to priority
hospitals, as well as logistics support for generators and NCD supplies, while IOM and partners delivered frontline health services,
border health screenings, and referrals in displacement settings [1034][1053]. The Health Sector and WHO have emphasized urgent
priorities for expanded shelter-based disease surveillance, risk communication and community engagement, replenishment of
essential medicines and trauma/EmONC supplies, and protection of health personnel, while calling for immediate flexible funding
under the revised Flash Appeal to prevent further public-health deterioration [1033][1041].
Health System Challenges
Attacks on health care have severely degraded Lebanon's health system: since 2 March, WHO recorded approximately 196–203
attacks, resulting in roughly 131–135 deaths and 379–394 injuries among health workers and patients, and driving widespread facility
closures and damage [1035][1042]. Three hospitals remain fully non-operational, 17 hospitals have sustained damage, and over 42–44
primary health-care centres have closed; the attack near Jabal Amel Hospital on 1 June damaged emergency and ICU departments
and injured 39 healthcare workers [1035][1059][1042][1043].
Workforce capacity is critically insufficient due to staff displacement, deaths and injuries, psychosocial distress among aid workers,
and reliance on volunteers. Facilities are operating at reduced capacity, with increased use of remote and mobile response modalities
[1039][1035][1041]. Essential medicines, trauma kits, and EmONC supplies are depleted or in short supply, while fuel and logistics
constraints and procurement delays have disrupted supply chains and raised costs [1033][1064][1034].
Humanitarian access constraints — including ongoing insecurity, evacuation orders, route restrictions, UXO contamination, GPS
spoofing, and cancelled missions — have delayed or prevented delivery of medical assistance, although 203 humanitarian
movements were facilitated through the Humanitarian Notification System between 2 March and 11 June [1048][1042]. Financial
shortfalls further threaten service continuity, with persistent medicine shortages and high out-of-pocket costs despite MoPH subsidies,
raising acute risks particularly for displaced pregnant women and other vulnerable groups in shelters [1035][1033][1034].

Health Needs and Current Response
Rapid health assessments conducted in May–June 2026 found a widening gap between rising health needs and declining system
capacity, driven by a high burden of non-communicable diseases, increasing mental health and respiratory conditions, constrained
sexual and reproductive health services, medicine shortages, workforce shortages, infrastructure damage, and reduced specialised
services [1033][1034][1035][1036]. Approximately 134,800–135,000 internally displaced people remain in collective shelters as of 8–11
June 2026, with shelter capacity critically stretched and communicable disease and protection risks elevated [1034][1038].
The most urgent clinical needs are trauma and mass-casualty care and emergency surgery, continuity of NCD treatment, restoration
of SRH and EmONC services for pregnant women and newborns, and strengthened communicable-disease surveillance in
overcrowded shelters [1034][1033][1043][1036][1048]. UNFPA is prioritising SRH and GBV by deploying mobile medical teams and
midwives, supplying Inter-Agency Reproductive Health kits and baby kits, and has appealed for USD 25 million to cover March–August
2026 SRH/GBV needs for targeted populations [1043][1055].
WHO's operational support includes distribution of trauma resources (120 trauma backpacks, 20 trauma/emergency surgery kits, and
15 ICU ventilators), delivery of over 134 m³ and 3.5 tonnes of emergency and trauma medical supplies, funding of hospitalisation costs
for non-Lebanese trauma patients, procurement of generators and NCD supplies, and training and psychosocial support for health
workers [1034][1041][1048][1068][1045]. Health-sector partners have delivered approximately 383,366 consultations and medications
to around 142,597 patients through PHC networks, while IOM reports supporting 18,394 individuals to access essential health services
and strengthening community-based disease surveillance with reports submitted to the Ministry of Health [1036][1048][1053].
Critical gaps remain in geographic coverage, stabilisation of supply chains (essential medicines, trauma, and EmONC items), severe
workforce shortages and burnout, and high financial barriers to care. The Health Sector urgently requests flexible funding — an
additional USD 61.5 million under the revised Flash Appeal, with only USD 12.8 million currently available — while the overall revised Flash
Appeal (June–August 2026) requested USD 639.9 million and had received USD 206.2 million (32.2%) by 11 June, constraining the ability
to sustain and scale life-saving services [1033][1036][1041][1042]. Coordination mechanisms involve MoPH, MoSA, and multiple UN
agencies and humanitarian partners (WHO, UNFPA, IOM, UNHCR), with sector and sub-national coordination and operational hotlines
(1787, 1214, 1564) to support referrals and service navigation [1058][1045][1041].

Life-saving nutrition services are being delivered through primary health care, community outreach, mobile teams, nutrition centres,
and collective shelters [1077][1076]. Partners are scaling activities to displacement-affected and hard-to-reach areas, but severe

workforce shortages—particularly in southern areas and Baalbek—are reducing outreach, stabilisation and referral capacity for
nutrition services [1076][1089]. Workforce gaps, reduced health-facility capacity, and severe funding shortfalls further constrain the
continuity and scale of nutrition services [1076][1089][1077].

- 682,433 emergency nutrition rations and micronutrient supplements distributed to cover at least one month [1077].
- 21,279 children under five and 4,312 pregnant and breastfeeding women screened for wasting, with referrals for treatment where
needed [1077].
- The national IYCF hotline received 697 calls between 1 March and 4 June, with 544 callers referred for specialised IYCF support,
and 1,107 IYCF Simple Rapid Assessments were conducted to identify feeding challenges [1077].
- Under the MoPH-led Safe Provision of Breastmilk Substitutes (BMS) initiative, approximately 313–335 infants under one received
infant formula support, with 3,161 cans of formula distributed [1076][1077].
- Approximately 15,540 caregivers of children under five received counselling and awareness sessions on IYCF, nutrition and early
childhood development, and 10 ECD/Mother-Baby Corners were established to support caregiver-child feeding and care
practices [1076][1077].
WASH
Availability of Water Sources
Oxfam's Digital Needs Assessment (April–May 2026) found water trucking was the largest water source overall (39.86%), followed by
public networks (24.64%) and bottled water (22.46%) of households. Of surveyed respondents, 52.9% reported insufficient WASH
quantity, and 35.51% rated WASH quality as "bad/not acceptable," with approximately 35.6% of displaced respondents rating water
quality as bad. Returnees were more likely to use water treatment (12%), with boiling and chlorination being common methods among
displaced households [1113]. Qualitative findings highlighted municipal service pressure in Bekaa, a disrupted main water source in
Saaide after an electrical cable to a borehole was reportedly stolen, and major drinking-water access concerns for households
outside shelters in the South and North [1113]. Public water systems have been failing in conflict-affected areas, increasing reliance on
private water trucking that is sometimes unaffordable for IDPs [1120]. Seasonal risk is elevated as Lebanon enters the dry season
(June–September): 2025 rainfall was more than 50% below average and 2026 forecasts indicate likely ~10% below-average rains,
which could compound conflict-related water access pressures [1120].
Access to Sanitation Facilities and Hygiene Practices
Oxfam's Digital Needs Assessment (April–May 2026) found severe sanitation gaps in displacement settings: 56.93% of sampled latrines
were reported non-functional, 15.33% had cleanliness issues, safety concerns affected 10.22% and privacy 8.03%, while accessibility
concerns were lower at 3.65%. Shared sanitation in collective shelters was frequently described as unclean, poorly maintained, flooded,
or lacking supplies and clear gender separation, with Kfar Habou specifically highlighted [1113][1124]. UNFPA's safety audit of 180 shelters
found that 28% lacked adequate space and 20% failed to separate female-headed households from unrelated men, with high-risk
WASH facilities frequently lacking gender segregation and accessibility, increasing protection and dignity risks for women and girls
[1130]. Overall, documented barriers to dignified sanitation and hygiene include widespread latrine non-functionality and poor
maintenance, shortages of cleaning and disinfection materials and menstrual hygiene supplies, unclear gender segregation in shared
WASH facilities, and overcrowding in shelters [1113][1130][1140]. WHO-supported hygiene activities included 17 hygiene-related health
education sessions reaching 267 participants across five shelters [1133].
Waste Management and Vector-Borne Disease Risks
UNOPS documented that the 2024 conflict in Lebanon generated approximately 14.5 million metric tonnes of debris, a volume that can
overwhelm local waste systems and complicate safe rubble management and recycling opportunities [1135]. A national digital needs
assessment found increased pressure on municipal solid waste systems in areas hosting large numbers of IDPs, notably Al Ain [1113].
Taken together, large volumes of conflict-generated debris, ongoing displacement-driven waste pressures, strained water services,
and shelter overcrowding are increasing risks of water- and vector-borne disease transmission [1135][1113]. WHO reported at least 32
recent cases of exposure to rabies and scheduled distribution of rabies awareness materials to all shelters, while initial shelter
assessments (14 visits) have been conducted to mitigate health risks in congregate sites [1133]. Humanitarian responses have focused
on emergency water supply, hygiene kit distributions, and risk communication — including rabies awareness — alongside site-level
WASH interventions to reduce these risks [1135][1113][1144][1133].
Main WASH Needs and Humanitarian Response
As of 11 June 2026, WASH Sector partners reported reaching 1,074,702 people across collective shelters, host communities, and
out-of-shelter settings [1140]. Since 2 March, partners have delivered the following at scale:
- 3,765,725 litres of potable water and approximately 110,770 m³ of water to collective shelters through water trucking [1140]
- 851,000 litres of fuel to water establishments, sustaining public water supply for an estimated 841,128 people across 296

- 114,273 hygiene kits, 68,705 menstrual hygiene kits, and 1,533 cleaning kits distributed to affected populations [1140]
IOM's March–August 2026 Flash Appeal commits to supporting critical water supply, backup generators, sanitation systems, water
trucking, bottled water distribution, household water storage solutions, and hygiene-item distributions in collective sites and host
communities [1134].

Targeted hygiene promotion has included 17 hygiene education sessions reaching 267 people in shelters [1144][1133]. However,

Shelter and Non-Food Items (NFI)
Damage to Housing and Shelter Needs
A UNDP-led rapid assessment for Beirut and Mount Lebanon found 146 buildings completely destroyed, 264 partially damaged, 3,168
housing units destroyed and 4,437 units damaged, with an estimated debris volume of about 648,942 m³ in the assessed area [1178].
Nationally, more than 61,000 housing units were reported totally or partially damaged between March and early May 2026, with earlier
CNRS/ACAPS data recording over 37,800 housing units affected between 2 March and 7 April, heavily concentrated in Nabatiye, South,
Mount Lebanon and Beirut [1172][1173]. Large sudden movements on 7 June reportedly displaced an estimated 4,000 families in
Nabatieh, 30,000 families in South governorate and some 50,000 families (~200,000 people) from Beirut's southern suburbs, many of
whom sought refuge in open areas or temporary sites [1174][1193][1173]. As of 11 June, 134,800 displaced people (35,700 families) were
registered in 642 collective shelters nationwide, with shelters in Beirut, Tyre and Saida at full capacity and North governorate identified
as the primary destination for new arrivals [1176]. The majority of displaced people remain outside formal collective sites, with
large-scale out-of-shelter hosting in overcrowded apartments and informal arrangements — estimates indicate roughly 85% of
women and girls are living outside formal shelters — while others shelter in open spaces or temporary tented sites [1181][1187]. A portion
of IDPs live in tents or informal shelters (surveyed IDP share in such shelter types ~7.44%) [1193]. Displaced populations in collective sites
include substantial numbers of vulnerable people — approximately 6,800 older persons (55+), 1,200 people with physical disabilities,
661 with mental disabilities, 386 with hearing impairments and 392 with visual impairments — underscoring needs for accessible and
inclusive shelter interventions [1193].
Rebuilding faces multiple constraints: widespread housing, land and property (HLP) issues complicate rubble removal and clearance;
structural assessments are required before safe recovery; and reconstruction costs, shortages and inflation in construction materials,
and limited national fiscal space raise major financing challenges [1205][1172]. Shelter-sector partners are prioritizing repairs and site
upgrades — 341 of 420 prioritized sites completed in one update — and scaling site management to support tens of thousands, while
appeals identify urgent shelter funding needs (UNHCR/shelter requirements cited ~USD 58.37 million and IOM planning to target some
105,000 individuals across 250 sites with USD 16 million requested) to sustain cash-for-rent, cash-for-shelter, collective-site
maintenance, and core relief item (CRI) distributions [1175][1186][1197]. Damage to community infrastructure further limits options for
return and safe re-housing: education partners reported 17 schools destroyed and over 100 damaged in initial assessments, with up to
446 schools non-operational by late May, while attacks and damage to health facilities have forced closures and reduced service
capacity across the country [1178][1173][1176].
Living Conditions in Camps or Settlements
As of 11 June 2026, 134,800 displaced people (35,700 families) were registered in 642 collective shelters, with shelters in Beirut, Tyre and
Saida at full or near capacity while North Governorate is absorbing new arrivals and authorities are activating additional sites and
tented accommodation [1176][1175]. A UNFPA safety audit of 180 collective shelters found severe protection and space shortfalls — 28%
lack adequate space and 20% fail to separate female-headed households from unrelated men — with widespread overcrowding,
absence of locks and partitions, poor lighting, high-risk WASH conditions, and shortages of women- and girl-friendly safe spaces, all of
which increase GBV risks [1196]. Women and girls account for nearly 52% of those in shelters, and protection partners report rising
psychosocial distress, particularly among children and caregivers, exacerbating protection, privacy and care burdens amid
overcrowding and mounting housing insecurity [1192][1177].
Shelter monitoring highlights the concentration of vulnerable groups inside collective shelters, including approximately 6,800 older
people, ~1,200 persons with physical disabilities, 661 with mental disabilities, 386 with hearing impairments and 392 with visual
impairments, highlighting accessibility and specialized service needs on-site [1193]. WASH partners report reaching over 1.07 million
people across collective shelters, host communities and out-of-shelter settings through fuel provision to water establishments and
water trucking to shelters, though significant gaps in sustained water and sanitation services persist [1175][1179]. Shelter-sector repairs
and site improvements are ongoing: partners prioritized repairs at 420 sites with works completed in 341 (81%), while UNHCR planned
repairs and maintenance in 76 collective shelters, completing works in 46 and continuing in 11, to improve privacy, accessibility and
safety [1175][1177].
NFI and Shelter Needs and Current Response
Digital needs assessments and community feedback report urgent NFI requirements including mattresses and pillows, kitchen utensils,
hygiene and menstrual health (MHM) kits, diapers, washing machines, food-storage items and seasonal clothing to preserve dignity
and household functioning [1174]. Inside collective shelters since 2 March, partners report distributions of 111,370 mattresses, 138,569
blankets and 74,350 pillows, with all households in collective shelters having received initial CRIs [1175]. Outside collective shelters,
partners have provided targeted in-kind support to vulnerable households, including 27,712 mattresses and 40,229 blankets [1175].
Overall, more than 87,000 displaced people in collective shelters received over 223,700 CRIs, with an additional ~14,000 displaced
people in host communities assisted with more than 48,700 CRIs [1177][1183].

Operational modalities are adapting to the scale and diversity of displacement: in-kind CRIs and shelter repairs are prioritized inside
collective sites and in hard-to-reach areas, while cash-for-rent and cash-for-shelter assistance is being scaled for households
outside shelters, including a one-off shelter cash payment of USD 250 provided to 901 vulnerable families [1175][1197][1179][1183].
Site-management scale-up includes the deployment of mobile SMC teams aiming to support approximately 80,000 people across

Critical gaps remain across the response. Shelter capacity is stretched, NFI stock and coverage are insufficient for non-Lebanese and

(including destroyed bridges isolating populations), and restricted access to some areas are further impeding timely NFI and shelter

and repair efforts, gaps remain in coverage, shelter quality, privacy and safety — particularly for non-Lebanese displaced persons
facing accommodation shortages and access restrictions — underscoring continued needs for NFIs, shelter maintenance, site
management and flexible funding [1173][1196][1175].
Protection
Impact on civilians
The humanitarian impact on civilians has been severe: at least 3,711 people were reported killed and 11,483 injured due to hostilities in
Lebanon as of 11 June 2026 [1211]. WHO's surveillance documents widespread, repeated attacks on health care, recording 203 attacks
that resulted in 135 deaths and 394 injuries among on-duty health workers, the closure of dozens of primary health centres, and three
hospitals remaining fully non-operational while 17 sustained damage, severely restricting access to care [1211][1223][1230]. Health
partners and rapid assessments warn of a widening gap between rising health needs — spanning trauma, NCDs, mental health and
SRH — and declining system capacity due to damaged facilities, workforce losses, medicine shortages and funding shortfalls,
increasing risks to civilian health outcomes and safe deliveries [1223][1236].
Nearly one million people remain internally displaced, with roughly 134,800 people (about 35,700 families) registered in collective
shelters as of 8–11 June 2026, placing severe strain on shelter capacity [1250][1211][1222]. Large new displacement movements on 7
June included preliminary estimates of roughly 50,000 families (~200,000 people) displaced from Beirut's southern suburbs and
substantial movements in South and Nabatieh governorates (approximately 30,000 families in South and 4,000 families in Nabatieh),
overwhelming local reception and prompting temporary evacuations of some collective sites [1222][1232]. Women and girls account
for about 52% of people in collective shelters, increasing gender-specific protection needs in congested sites [1232][1211].
Education has been heavily disrupted: attacks and damage to schools affected an estimated 265,000 students by April, and by 28 May
around 446 schools were non-operational, removing large parts of education infrastructure from use [1216][1229]. Displaced refugees
and migrants face restricted access to shelter and services, growing legal insecurity and loss of documentation, while UNHCR and
protection partners reached hundreds of thousands with information, case management and legal assistance and supported birth
registration and residency cases in May 2026 [1216][1209]. Economic coping and protection pressures are widespread: Oxfam's digital
needs assessment found 32.26% of households reporting debt as a negative coping mechanism (predominantly displaced
households), driving eviction risk, landlord disputes and psychosocial distress, and protection partners note constrained GBV and
specialised services amid resource shortfalls [1213][1229].
Factors Driving protection risks
According to ACAPS, intensified airstrikes, ground operations and repeated evacuation orders since early March — including the 7 June
strike on Dahiyeh that expanded the geographic scope of attacks — have driven civilian casualties and mass displacement,
underpinning most protection risks [1212]. Large-scale displacement disproportionately affects women and girls: ACAPS estimates
approximately 620,000 displaced women and girls since 2 March, with 85% living outside formal shelters, while the vast majority of all
IDPs remain outside formal sites, increasing exposure to protection harms and limiting access to services [1212][1232][1234]. This
concentration of risk is compounded for persons with disabilities, older people and those with chronic conditions, raising GBV,
psychosocial distress and access barriers [1212][1232][1234].
Severe economic stress is a key driver of protection risks: Oxfam's digital needs assessment found 32.26% of households reporting debt
as a coping mechanism, with 59% of key informants linking rent-related debt to eviction risks, fuelling exploitation, child labour and
transactional sex [1213][1217]. Humanitarian agencies report major disruptions to health, protection and education services due to
damage and attacks on infrastructure including hospitals and schools, constraining survivors' access to medical care, GBV services
and legal remedies [1228][1253][1216][1229]. Non-Lebanese populations face restricted access to shelter and services and heightened
risks of arrest or detention, while loss of civil documentation and degraded justice and protection systems limit reporting and legal
recourse for rights violations [1216][1243][1212]. Overcrowding, shelter shortages and service gaps are driving rises in GBV, trafficking
and other exploitation, with UNFPA, IOM and other protection actors warning of sharp increases in these risks unless protection and
multisectoral assistance are scaled up immediately [1216][1215][1221]. Humanitarian partners also report UXO and explosive hazard
contamination — particularly south of the Litani — impeding safe access, rubble clearance and returns and adding explosive-remnant
risks for civilians and responders [1229][1260].

Psychological and mental health impacts
Humanitarian partners report a sharp rise in mental health and psychosocial needs since the escalation began in March 2026, with
increasing presentations of anxiety, depression and conflict-related stress across displaced and host communities [1223][1216]. An
estimated 770,000 children are experiencing heightened distress, with caregivers reporting extreme fear, nightmares, sleeplessness
and feelings of hopelessness, while reported behavioural and coping manifestations include acute traumatic stress reactions among
children (including self-harm in case reports), widespread sleep and fear disturbances, and broader negative coping strategies linked
to economic strain and displacement [1212][1216][1225][1213].
The National Mental Health Programme (NMHP), co-chaired by WHO and UNICEF and involving 100+ partners, is coordinating a national
MHPSS response (snapshot period: 2 March–5 June 2026). Key service delivery figures include: 63% of PHCCs providing psychological
care and 23% providing psychiatric care; 2,734 front-line workers trained; 2,036 specialised consultations; 26,777 non-specialised
psychosocial contacts; 5,111 people receiving psychotropic medications; 52 mobile crisis team dispatches (21 resulting in
hospitalisations); and 4,832 calls to the national lifeline 1564. Digital self-help uptake included 504 new Step-by-Step users and 1,157
Self-Help Plus users [1245]. WHO additionally trained approximately 250 health workers across 29 provinces, conducted 87
peer-support sessions reaching approximately 4,700 health workers, provided individual psychological support to approximately 300
health workers, and supported training of thousands of nurses in emotional crisis management and self-care [1231][1232].
Access to MHPSS is constrained by repeated attacks on health care and disrupted services — including closures of dozens of PHCCs
and several hospitals and health-worker casualties since 2 March — while the 2026 Flash Appeal remains substantially underfunded,
limiting scale-up of MHPSS services (IOM/MHPSS line: USD 1.8M for 20,000 people) [1211][1223][1230][1250]. Displaced and other
vulnerable groups face disproportionate psychosocial impact, with shelter monitoring identifying older people, persons with
disabilities and people with mental disabilities among those with elevated barriers to services [1230][1211][1232].
Protection Needs and Current Response
Collective shelters are dangerously congested, lacking basic supplies and privacy, which increases protection risks including
gender-based violence. As of 8–11 June 2026, 134,800 people were registered across 632–642 collective sites [1221][1222][1227].
Assessments confirm protection is the largest reported need among displaced populations: Oxfam's April–May 2026 digital needs
assessment identified protection as a top concern, with eviction or lack of shelter access affecting 7.26% of households and debt as a
widespread negative coping mechanism, underscoring demand for cash, legal aid and targeted community outreach [1213].
Protection actors have reached large numbers: UNHCR and Protection Sector partners reported reaching over 242,500 individuals with
targeted protection interventions since the renewed escalation, with activities benefiting 249,505 IDPs and other affected people (62%
women and girls) as of 8 June [1209][1222]. UNHCR and partners provided emergency case management to 1,005 individuals
(including 179 children), psychosocial support to nearly 8,700 people, and emergency protection cash assistance to 13,550 displaced
refugees [1209]. IOM has delivered protection assistance to 7,019 individuals, referred over 3,327 people to multi-sector services, and
reached 4,301 with MHPSS since 2 March [1215]. The broader protection response includes legal assistance, case management,
cash-for-protection, mobile protection teams, and strengthened site-management and referral pathways [1250][1230][1215].
UNFPA is leading community outreach, GBV awareness and prevention, and dignity-kit distribution (with an estimated gap of 28,000
kits), and is hosting inter-agency PSEA and AAP coordination to strengthen collective accountability to affected people [1221]. Gaps in
GBV and specialised protection support are widening amid severe funding shortfalls, with the 2026 Flash Appeal only around 60%
funded at the initial stage, leaving major resource gaps for protection services [1229][1220][1216].
Child Protection concerns
Children in Lebanon face rising physical and psychosocial harm from the March escalation: at least 245 children were killed and 957
injured between 2 March and 4 June 2026, and an estimated 770,000 children are experiencing heightened distress from repeated
exposure to violence, loss and displacement [1216][1212]. By 7 June an estimated 1.5 million people had been displaced across Lebanon,
driving repeated displacement of children and prolonged disruption to safe routines and services [1216]. Schools have been widely
affected — nearly 265,000 students were impacted by April and about 446 schools were non-operational by 28 May — heightening
protection risks for children, as many school buildings have been repurposed as collective shelters [1216].
Negative coping mechanisms are on the rise among displaced households, with 32.26% of surveyed households reporting debt as a
primary negative coping strategy (displaced households accounting for roughly 75% of those cases), alongside increasing reports of
child labour and early marriage risks [1213][1209]. Family separation and acute protection cases are also evident: 1,005 individuals at
heightened risk received emergency case management, including 179 children requiring follow-up for serious vulnerabilities such as
separation, exploitation or violence [1209]. Documentation barriers compound these risks, though UNHCR continued support efforts —
assisting 221 refugee children with birth registration and 144 refugees to access legal residency in May 2026 [1209].
Child-focused mental health and psychosocial support has been scaled up: 28,585 children, adolescents and caregivers were
reached with child-protection awareness sessions and 58,183 children and adolescents received non-specialised and
community-based psychosocial support, while MHPSS integration in the protection sector reached 55,461 persons overall [1245].
Education-sector responses have delivered services, with partners reaching 262,384 displaced children through formal modalities
(94,073 in-person; 163,420 online) and 4,910 through non-formal education, yet large numbers remain out of school due to
infrastructure constraints and connectivity gaps [1229][1216]. A GBV safety audit of 180 shelters found 28% lack adequate space and
20% do not separate female-headed households from unrelated men, increasing risks of exploitation, abuse and GBV for children and
their caregivers [1221].

Humanitarian actors report sharply elevated GBV risks for displaced women and girls amid overcrowded, privacy-poor shelters and
disrupted protection services [1221][1216][1229]. ACAPS and related reporting estimate approximately 620,000 women and girls have

Protection partners, including UNHCR and 78 Protection Sector partners, report reaching more than 242,500 people with targeted

Elderly
Approximately 6,800 older people aged 55 years and above are residing in collective shelters, indicating a significant presence of
elderly among displaced populations [1275]. Older people and those with chronic conditions face additional barriers to accessing
health care and medications due to medicine shortages, workforce gaps, facility damage, and high out-of-pocket costs documented
by WHO and Health Sector assessments in May–June 2026 [1274][1278][1275]. Overcrowded and inadequately equipped shelters —
including poor privacy, insufficient gender-segregated WASH, and limited psychosocial support — increase protection and mobility
risks for older persons, while geographic isolation and transport barriers outside shelters further constrain access to services and
medication [1287][1275]. Non-Lebanese older displaced persons face additional movement restrictions and legal and documentation
barriers that limit their access to services, as reported in protection monitoring [1275][1267]. Shelter and protection actors are
implementing accessibility adaptations, partitioning, targeted repair and cash assistance, and UN agencies have called for
Demographic Vulnerability Assessments to identify and prioritise older persons in referrals and response planning [1284][1286][1290].
Displaced
Since the March escalation, roughly one million people remain internally displaced in Lebanon, with the vast majority hosted outside
formal collective sites and relying on informal hosting arrangements [1295][1271]. As of 11 June 2026, about 134,800 people (35,700
families) were registered in 642 collective shelters, with shelters in Beirut, Saida, Tyre and Mount Lebanon at or near full capacity and
collective sites reporting occupancy around 96 percent [1270][1271][1286]. Most displaced households reside in rented
accommodation or with host families and face rising rents, depleted savings and negative coping — including reduced spending and
increased child labour — increasing pressures on food, WASH and other basic services [1280][1287]. Cross-border movements into the
Syrian Arab Republic exceeded 418,000 by 8 June 2026, while returns are largely temporary and unsustainable due to ongoing
insecurity, infrastructure damage and limited livelihoods [1271][1294]. IOM and partners estimate over 48,000 displaced migrants and
third-country nationals who face language barriers, restricted service access, heightened protection vulnerabilities and reluctance to
use government-managed collective sites [1295][1271][1267]. Loss of civil documentation and legal insecurity are widespread among
displaced refugees and returnees; UNHCR assisted 221 refugee children with birth registration and 144 refugees to access legal
residency in May 2026 [1281][1280]. UNHCR and partners have scaled shelter repairs and site management: repairs were planned in 76
collective shelters (46 completed, 11 ongoing) and shelter actors report 341 of 420 prioritised site repairs completed (81%), while site
management scale-up aims to support approximately 80,000 people across 400 sites [1280][1286]. WASH partners reached over 1.07
million people with emergency water, sanitation and hygiene services in early–mid June, while health services are severely strained
with WHO supporting trauma care hospitalisations and emergency medical supplies [1269][1274][1267]. Cash assistance has been
scaled up, with over 137,000 vulnerable refugees reached by Emergency Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance by May and the Ministry of
Social Affairs sharing beneficiary lists for approximately 100,000 households under a blanket E-MPCA distribution as targeting
commenced in June [1280][1269]. Protection actors report escalating GBV and safety risks in displacement settings — including
overcrowding, lack of privacy, inadequate gender-segregated WASH, locks and lighting — and GBV services and specialised support
face widening gaps amid stretched resources [1290][1277][1269].
Minorities
Humanitarian reporting in early June 2026 highlights heightened exclusion and protection risks for minority and marginalised groups
in Lebanon — including migrants, persons with disabilities, older people, sexual and gender minorities (SOGIESC), and minority-origin
Syrians — amid mass displacement and strained services [1283][1271][1275][1267]. Assessments and briefs report rising
host-community tensions and perceptions that displaced groups are affiliated with armed actors, fuelling resentment, discrimination,
harassment and localised attacks against displaced populations [1267].
26
Disrupted protection and justice services have reduced victims' ability to report violations, while UNHCR and partners continue to
deliver legal residency and civil-documentation assistance and targeted protection interventions [1267][1280][1284]. Humanitarian
actors are prioritising inclusion: IOM is co-leading site management and scaling multi-sector assistance for migrants, and UNFPA and
the GBV Working Group are expanding SRH and GBV services — including mobile teams and outreach for women with disabilities —
alongside calls for demographic vulnerability assessments to map older persons and persons with disabilities, though access,
insecurity and funding shortfalls limit coverage [1271][1290][1284].
