events concealed a hardening of the violence that remained. The transitional state was the single

Monthly Overview 
June opened at the month’s highest tempo and gradually thinned, without ever becoming safer. The first 
week recorded the most incidents and the fourth the fewest, yet fatalities rose across the same span to 
peak late in the month. The arc is therefore one of consolidation rather than escalation, in which a 
declining volume of recorded events concealed a hardening of the violence that remained. Activity 
concentrated on 15, 8 and 1 June, and the geographic centre of gravity sat firmly in the south, with Dar’a 
and Quneitra together accounting for more than a quarter of everything recorded nationwide. 
That southern concentration is almost entirely a function of Israeli military activity. Israeli forces 
conducted their highest total in nine months, spread across ground incursions into villages, mobile 
checkpoints, drone overflights, artillery fire and missile strikes, and not one produced a recorded fatality. 
The absence of deaths should not be mistaken for the absence of harm. Independent monitoring places 
Israeli positions well beyond the ceasefire line established by the 1974 Disengagement Agreement, with de 
facto control over roughly 235 square kilometres, and Human Rights Watch documented home seizures,

Geographic Overview 
Dar’a and Quneitra — The Southern Occupation Zone 
The southern governorates were the most incident -dense in the country. The overwhelming majority of 
activity is attributable to Israeli military operations, which took the form of ground incursions into villages 
such as Saida al -Hanout, Abu Madhara and Al -Asha, mobile checkpoints on civilian routes, drone 
overflights, artillery fire and, on eight occasions, missile strikes. The defining characteristic is low lethality 
and high friction: no fatalities were recorded from Israeli operations during the month, yet the cumulative 
effect on civilian life is severe, with agricultural land rendered inaccessible, movement corridors

Deir ez-Zor, Ar-Raqqa and Al-Hasakah — The Insurgent Corridor 
The eastern governorates carry the country’s most serious organised armed threat. Ar -Raqqa was the site 
of the month’s most significant single attack, the 15 June suicide -belt assault on the Internal Security 
Command headquarters. Al-Hasakah saw an ambush on a Ministry of Defence transport bus between Tel 
Tamer and Ras al-Ain that wounded ten personnel on 18 June, an ambush on the Qamishli road that killed 
two security personnel on 25 June, and an explosive device detonated in a civilian vehicle in Qamishli city. 
Deir ez -Zor sustained the classic pattern of a reconstituting insurgency: an IED on a public street in Al -
Bukamal, a grenade thrown at a private residence in Al -Kharita, an explosive device at a military 
headquarters in Abu Kamal on 27 June, an RPG attack on an Internal Security checkpoint at the Al-Basirah 
bridge on 29 June, and a sequence of unattributed assassinations in outlying villages. Almost none of this 
activity carries an actor label in the source data; the signature, however, is unmistakable. 
Operational implications.  Treat the Deir ez -Zor, Ar-Raqqa and Al -Hasakah corridor as an active insurgency 
environment irrespective of the low volume of formally attributed attacks. Reintroduce explosive ordnance 
coordination, convoy hardening and threat -warning protocols. Assume the targeting of security 
infrastructure, checkpoints and military transport, and avoid co-location with those assets. 
Aleppo and Idleb — The New Front 
Aleppo has emerged, in the assessment of independent conflict monitors, as a new operational front for 
the Islamic State after being largely absent in earlier periods. The month saw an attack on an Iraqi oil 
tanker on the Aleppo to Menbij road, an explosive device in a security vehicle in Al -Bab, an IED against a 
military transport bus on the Maskanah highway, the killing of two soldiers in the Manbij countryside on 20 
June, and a motorcycle -borne assassination of a defence official in As -Safira on 28 June. Beyond the 
insurgent picture, Aleppo and Idleb carried a heavy load of fires, road -traffic accidents and explosive 
remnants of war, including UXO detonations in Al-Bab and Azaz that injured civilians. The north combined 
an emerging insurgent front with the country’s characteristic hazard burden, producing a compound risk 
environment along the M4 and Manbij corridors. 
Operational implications.  The eastward corridors of Aleppo governorate, particularly the Manbij and 
Maskanah axes, should be reclassified as elevated -threat for IED and ambush risk. Oil and commercial 
transport on these routes has been directly targeted. Combine explosive ordnance risk education with 
journey management for all movement in the governorate.  
As-Sweida — Contested Reintegration 
The defining feature of As -Sweida is not incident volume but character: armed factions maintained 
roadblocks on the main highway toward Damascus, including near Shahba city on 5 June, and inter -
factional clashes continued, with heavy fighting between two local factions in Ar -Raha on 28 June killing 
four. This is the unresolved sediment of the July 2025 crisis, in which fighting between Druze -led and 
Bedouin armed groups, compounded by government intervention and Israeli airstrikes, produced mass 
displacement and documented unlawful killings on all sides. The US -mediated ceasefire of 16 July 2025 
returned governance and security to Druze-led structures, restoring the governorate’s informal autonomy

without provisions for civilian protection, aid access or accountability. At month end As -Sweida remained 
outside effective central control, with a fragmented armed landscape and no political framework for

Operational implications.  The As -Sweida to Damascus corridor is subject to armed -actor roadblocks that 
can close without warning. Verify route status before every movement and maintain contingency for

Damascus and Rural Damascus — The Political Centre 
The capital and its rural belt were dominated by law -enforcement operations, judicial proceedings and 
protest activity. The month’s most significant security event was the 16 June improvised explosive device 
that struck the vehicle of the head of the judicial section in Babila, severing his leg, an attack claimed by 
the Islamic State and directed against the machinery of transitional justice. Protest activity was persistent 
and politically charged: a civilian sit -in outside the Parliament building on 6 June demanded equal 
employment opportunity and anti -corruption measures, and across the first half of the month protests 
demanding accountability for crimes committed under the former government erupted in several cities, 
with some escalating into mob violence against minorities and individuals associated with the previous 
administration. 
Operational implications.  Personnel associated with judicial, transitional justice or government -adjacent 
functions face a demonstrated targeted -attack risk in the Damascus periphery. Vary routines and vehicle 
profiles. Plan for sudden protest -driven mobility restriction in the capital, and note the risk of accountability 
protests escalating into communal violence. 
Homs, Hama, Lattakia and Tartous — The Central and Coastal Belt 
The central and coastal governorates recorded comparatively low volume but produced some of the 
month’s most lethal individual events. A violent dispute between two families in the village of Manjila, 
Lattakia, on 25 June left five dead. Sectarian friction in Homs produced a fatal armed assault on a shop 
owner on 8 June and the killing of an elderly shepherd in the eastern desert on 11 June, the latter in the 
Badiya corridor that remains a historic Islamic State operating area. The dominant recorded activity, 
however, was hazard -driven: fires, road -traffic accidents and structural failures, alongside a continuing 
tempo of state security operations including cordon and search raids around Salhab in Hama. 
Operational implications.  The eastern Homs desert corridor warrants continued monitoring as a residual 
Islamic State operating area. Elsewhere in the belt, the dominant risks to personnel are road safety, fire and 
communal disputes rather than organised armed violence.

Trend Analysis 
Trend 01 — The Islamic State: Attribution Collapse, Capability Intact 
The most consequential finding of the month is that the Islamic State’s operational footprint in Syria is 
substantially larger than any actor-attribution field will show. The source data named the group in a single 
record. Cross-checking against open-source reporting establishes that the group publicly claimed at least 
four distinct attacks in June, every one of which appears in the dataset under generic labels such as 
unknown or non -state armed actors, and a further three incidents match the group’s tradecraft and 
heartland geography without carrying a public claim. Beyond these seven, the eastern corridor produced 
a dense cluster of unattributed insurgent -signature attacks. Across the dataset, a large body of incidents 
carrying scores of fatalities remains unattributed. That is where this campaign lives. 
The strategic reading requires reconciling two apparently contradictory measurements. Independent 
conflict monitoring records attacks on government forces rising sharply through the first half of 2026 
against the whole of 2025, concentrated in Deir ez -Zor, Ar -Raqqa and, newly, Aleppo. Separately, other 
analysis assessed that total operations fell through the spring following the withdrawal of United States 
forces. Both are correct: total attack volume is down, while concentration onto government targets and 
tactical sophistication are sharply up. The 15 June Ar -Raqqa operation supports that thesis, employing 
suicide belts for the first time since December against a fortified facility in a provincial capital. The 
enabling conditions are structural: Ar -Raqqa’s police force is reported at roughly a fifth of required 
strength after the government’s rapid expansion into former SDF territory stretched a security apparatus 
that lacked the personnel to hold it. On current indicators the group is reconstituting rather than 
resurging. 
Operational implications.  Do not calibrate posture in the northeast to the volume of formally attributed 
attacks. Treat the Deir ez -Zor, Ar -Raqqa, Al -Hasakah and eastern Aleppo corridor as an active insurgency 
environment. Reinstate explosive ordnance risk education, convoy hardening and route survey. Avoid co -
location with security -force assets, checkpoints and military transport. Staff in transitional justice or judicial 
support functions face a specific, claimed targeting risk. 
Trend 02 — Israeli Operations as Administration, Not Campaign 
Israeli Armed Forces were associated with the highest monthly incident count in nine months and zero 
recorded fatalities. That combination is the analytical signal. The composition is telling: armed assaults 
and altercations, ground incursions, mobile checkpoints, drone overflights, artillery fire, eight missile 
strikes, and a notable number of events coded as law -enforcement operations conducted inside Syrian 
territory. A military force conducting law enforcement, running checkpoints, collecting identity data and 
detaining residents is not prosecuting a campaign; it is administering territory. Independent monitoring 
places Israeli positions beyond the 1974 ceasefire line with de facto control over approximately 235 square 
kilometres, and Human Rights Watch documented home seizures and demolitions, the razing of forest 
and agricultural land, and the transfer of Syrian detainees into Israel. The likely path is entrenchment,

with the humanitarian consequences accruing quietly through access denial rather than visibly through 
casualty figures. 
Operational implications.  Risk assessment for the south must be built on detention, property destruction 
and access denial rather than on fatality data, which will systematically understate exposure. Assume 
checkpoint interception and identity verification on all movement in Quneitra and western Dar’a. Avoid siting 
activities or assets near Israeli installations. Programme design for agricultural or livelihoods support in the 
belt should assume long-term access constraint. 
Trend 03 — The Hazard Burden: Syria’s Quiet Mass-Casualty Story 
The single largest category of recorded incidents in June was not armed conflict but hazards. Fires, road -
traffic accidents, drownings and explosive remnants of war together produced a fatality toll exceeding 
that of armed conflict. This is the characteristic epidemiology of a post -conflict transition, and it is 
routinely under-weighted in security reporting because it does not present as violence. Fifteen years of 
war degraded road infrastructure, electrical systems, building stock and water management 
simultaneously, while the institutions that would normally regulate them were hollowed out. Mass return 
compounds the exposure: displaced families re -entering damaged housing, driving degraded highways 
and swimming in unregulated waterways. The 3 June head -on collision on the Athriya to Ar -Raqqa 
highway killed six in a single event, a figure exceeded by no armed attack in the dataset. The picture is 
likely to deteriorate through the summer, as fire and drowning risk peak and returnee volumes rise against 
a humanitarian appeal funded at roughly a fifth of requirement. 
Operational implications.  Weight protection and assistance programming toward hazard exposure rather 
than conflict exposure, which is where the mortality actually sits. Duty of care should prioritise road safety, 
journey management and fire precautions, statistically the largest threats to personnel in Syria this month. 
Support to civil defence capacity is a high-return intervention. 
Trend 04 — Explosive Remnants of War as the Legacy Killer 
Explosive remnants of war detonated 27 times in June, killing at least 18 people, concentrated in Deir ez -
Zor, Aleppo, Dar’a and Ar-Raqqa, precisely the corridors into which displaced families are returning. These 
are not conflict casualties in any conventional sense; they are the delayed detonation of a war that 
formally ended eighteen months ago. The scale of contamination is exceptional even by post -conflict 
standards, with the mine-action sector estimating that more than two thousand people had been killed or 
injured by explosives by early 2026 and considering that an underestimate. The mechanism driving 
casualties is economic rather than accidental: returnees re -enter contaminated agricultural land because 
it is their livelihood, and the desperate scavenge scrap metal from ordnance. Clearance capacity is 
constrained principally by funding, with one estimate putting full remediation at 25 to 40 years at current 
resourcing. 
Operational implications.  Explosive ordnance risk education must be integrated into every returns, 
livelihoods and agricultural programme in the affected corridors, not treated as a specialist mine -action

Trend 05 — Horizontal Violence: The Dominant Lethal Dynamic 
With the former regime’s coercive apparatus dismantled and a national security architecture still forming, 
the principal driver of violent death in Syria is now community against community. Incidents centred on 
civilians as the primary actor account for the largest fatality share of any actor category. Tribal clashes, 
inter-familial disputes and sectarian friction, not organised armed groups, produced the month’s 
deadliest individual incidents: a dispute between two families in Manjila killed five; armed clashes 
between factions in Ar -Raha killed four; inter -familial clashes in the Zakyeh neighbourhood of Rural 
Damascus killed three. Disputes over land, water, honour and communal boundary are resolving through 
firearms because the mechanisms that would otherwise resolve them are absent or distrusted. The 
analytical significance is that Syria’s transition risk is not principally a counter -insurgency problem; it is a 
rule-of-law and reconciliation problem, and it is worsened by the accountability process itself, as justice 
protests escalate into mob violence against minorities. 
Operational implications.  Conflict-sensitivity analysis and do -no-harm review should be mandatory for 
programming in mixed communities, particularly in Homs, Lattakia, Hama and rural Damascus. Beneficiary 
selection and hiring practices carry heightened escalation risk where communal grievance is live. Monitor 
accountability protests as a potential trigger for communal violence rather than as routine civil unrest.  
Trend 06 — As-Sweida and the Unfinished Question of Reintegration 
As-Sweida’s material fact this month is the persistence of armed roadblocks on the highway toward 
Damascus and continuing inter-factional violence, the direct sediment of the July 2025 crisis. The 16 July 
2025 ceasefire returned governance and security to Druze -led structures, restoring the governorate’s 
informal autonomy but omitting any provision for civilian protection, aid access or accountability, and 
that omission is the operative problem. The governorate now functions with parallel authority, a 
fragmented armed landscape and contested leadership, sitting outside effective central control while 
remaining formally part of the Syrian state. Much depends on whether Damascus offers a political 
framework for reintegration that the Druze leadership can accept; none was in prospect during the 
reporting period, and the government appears content to leave the question unresolved while the Israeli 
presence to the west provides an alternative patron that further reduces the incentive to negotiate. 
Operational implications.  Access to As -Sweida cannot be secured through central government 
authorisation alone and requires acceptance negotiated with local armed structures. The Damascus corridor 
is subject to unannounced closure; verify status before each movement and maintain contingency for 
extended interruption. Treat the governorate as a distinct operating environment with its own actor mapping.  
Trend 07 — The Transitional State: High Tempo, Low Lethality, Thin Coverage 
The Syrian state security apparatus generated a high count of recorded incidents in June while associated 
with almost no deaths, and law -enforcement operations were the single most frequent event type

nationwide. This is a posture of arrests, cordon and search operations, checkpoints and judicial 
enforcement, and it represents genuine institutional consolidation, including the integration of four SDF 
brigades onto the national payroll, the release of approximately 1,300 SDF -affiliated detainees, and a 
three-month counter-terrorism campaign that produced 235 arrests. It also marks a genuine and largely 
positive departure from the coercive model of the former regime. But that softness carries its own hazard:

apparatus beyond its trained personnel base, and it is precisely in the resulting gaps that the Islamic State

insurgent signature, not in the volume of state security activity. Do not treat state presence in the rural east as 
equivalent to security provision. Maintain clear operational separation from state security forces to protect 
perceived neutrality, particularly in the northeast where those forces are the declared target of insurgent 
attack.

Humanitarian Outlook 
The humanitarian picture at month end is one of acute and worsening resource constraint colliding with a 
protection environment that is diversifying rather than improving. The 2026 humanitarian appeal, 
requiring 2.92 billion dollars to support 8.6 million people, stood at roughly 20 percent funded, and the 
World Food Programme has halved emergency food assistance. Returns continue to accelerate, with more
Syria Monthly Security Overview  June 2026 
SARI Global www.sari.global Page 12 
than 390,000 people having crossed from Lebanon since early March, placing mounting pressure on 
services that were already degraded. 
The protection risks facing returning populations are precisely those documented in this month’s data. 
Explosive remnants of war detonate in the agricultural land and damaged housing to which families 
return. Fires, road -traffic accidents and drownings kill more people than combat. Euphrates flooding at 
the end of May affected over 17,600 people and damaged farmland and critical infrastructure, with 
consequences visible in Ar -Raqqa through June. Food insecurity, halved rations and the coping 
behaviours they produce feed directly into the hazard casualty figures. 
Access constraints are geographically specific and structurally distinct. In the south, Israeli military 
administration denies access to agricultural land and interdicts movement corridors without producing 
casualties. In As-Sweida, armed roadblocks close the Damascus highway at the discretion of local factions. 
In the eastern governorates, a reconstituting insurgency targets exactly the security infrastructure that 
humanitarian convoys travel alongside, making proximity to state forces a liability rather than a 
protection. The gap between need and response capacity is widening in all three directions at once, and 
national staff, who carry the majority of field exposure, bear the consequence.

Strategic Recommendations 
1 Re-baseline the northeast on insurgency, not on attribution counts. 
The Islamic State claimed four attacks in June that the incident record labels unknown. Rebuild the 
security framework for Deir ez-Zor, Ar-Raqqa, Al-Hasakah and eastern Aleppo around an active-
insurgency assumption. Reinstate explosive ordnance coordination, convoy hardening, route survey 
and threat-warning protocols, and treat security-force assets, checkpoints and military transport as 
targets to be avoided rather than escorts to be sought. 
2 Reweight duty of care toward hazards, which are killing more people than combat. 
Fires, road traffic, drowning and explosive remnants produced the largest share of the month’s 
confirmed deaths. Journey management, defensive-driving standards, daylight-only movement on 
degraded highways and fire precautions at fixed sites are, statistically, the highest -return staff-safety 
measures available in Syria. Support to civil defence capacity is a proportionate programmatic 
response. 
3 Integrate explosive ordnance risk education into every returns and livelihoods programme. 
Contamination is concentrated in exactly the corridors receiving returnees. Treat EORE as a core 
component of agricultural, shelter and livelihoods activity rather than a specialist mine -action 
function, prohibit off-road movement in former frontline areas without clearance confirmation, and 
train all field staff in identification and reporting. 
4 Assess southern Syria on detention and access denial, not on casualty data. 
Israeli forces conducted their highest operational total in nine months without a recorded fatality 
while seizing homes, demolishing buildings, razing agricultural land and detaining residents. Any risk 
model built on fatality figures will systematically understate exposure in Quneitra and western Dar’a. 
Assume checkpoint interception, avoid siting assets near Israeli installations, and design livelihoods 
programming for long-term access constraint.

7 Protect staff associated with judicial and transitional justice functions. 
The Islamic State claimed an IED attack that severed the leg of a judicial official in Babila, and a 
defence official was assassinated in As-Safira. Personnel connected to accountability, judicial support 
or government-adjacent functions require varied routines, low-profile movement and a reassessment 
of visible institutional association. 
8 Plan for a funding shortfall that will manifest as hazard casualties. 
With the appeal at roughly a fifth of requirement and rations halved, coping behaviours will intensify: 
scavenging in contaminated ground, informal construction, unregulated travel. These are the direct 
upstream causes of the month’s largest fatality category. Prioritise life -saving modalities, model 
reduced caseload coverage explicitly, and pair any assistance reduction with intensified risk 
education.
