The Protection Sector Emergency Snapshot is produced by the Protection Analysis and 
Monitoring Task Force (PAMTF), drawing on information and secondary data from sector 
lead agencies in the context of the ongoing escalation of conflict and displacement. It  
consolidates inputs from Protection, Child Protection, and GBV partners, including 
dedicated protection monitoring actors. The snapshot reflects field observations, 
community-level insights and operational updates from frontline teams engaged in 
protection service delivery and monitoring. 
 
Situation Overview  
The humanitarian situation in Lebanon remains fragile despite the 
ceasefire in place since 17 April 2026. Hostilities continued and 
increased, predominantly in Nabatiyeh and South governorates, 
with new displacement orders and sporadic attacks being 
reported in the West Bekaa.  
 
Over 1.4 million people have been affected by the crisis, including 
an estimated 390,000 women of reproductive age, among them 
approximately 16,000 pregnant women. In a matter of weeks, 
over one million individuals have been displaced amid large-scale 
destruction, with the crisis increasingly becoming protracted and 
urban in nature. The majority of displaced populations are living 
outside the roughly 639 functioning collective shelters, making 
them harder to reach and limiting access to essential services, 
while around 32,000 people remain in hard -to-reach areas. The 
situation is further exacerbated by violations of the ceasefire  
agreements with repeated targeting of civilian areas resulting in 
casualties, ongoing displacement, and widespread damage to 
infrastructure. In addition, the presence of unexploded ordnance 
(UXO) poses a severe and ongoing threat, increasing the risk of 
injury and death while hindering safe returns and access to 
affected locations.1 The situation in Southern Lebanon , including 
El Nabatiyeh and south governorate,  remains particularly 
worrisome, with reports that around 45% of the towns and 
villages being damaged or destroyed2 
 
The protection environment continues to deteriorate amid 
intensified airstrikes, with rising civilian casualties. Since March 2, 
the Ministry of Public Health reported about 14, 685 casualties 
among civilians,3 with more than 4,000 casualties post-ceasefire.4 
 
As the situation continues , vulnerabilities become more and 
more compounded by repeated displacement, housing insecurity, 
lack of resources to cover basic needs and reliance on unsafe

continue increasing. People with vulnerabilities, including persons 
with disabilities , face insufficient support, while psychological 
distress among children and caregivers is driving growing mental 
health and psychosocial support needs.  
 
Funding shortfalls continue to exacerbate the humanitarian 
situation in Lebanon. As of 25 May 2026, only 58% of the USD 
308 million Flash Appeal had been funded. The substantial funding 
gap constrains the capacity of humanitarian actors to sustain and 
scale up the delivery of critical assistance and essential services 
amid ongoing displacement and deteriorating humanitarian 
conditions.6 Protection Sector funding remains insufficient 
relative to the identified needs across the sector. During the first 
three months of the Flash Appeal period, the sector received 
approximately 45% of required funding, with GBV interventions 
being the most underfunded at just 19%. This shortfall has led to 
significantly reduced programmatic achievements and the 
persistence of high levels of protection needs.

Analysis of Protection Risks 
and Concerns 
 
1. Housing Insecurity, Evictions and Shelter Exploitation 
remain the Dominant Protection Risk: Since the escalation, 
housing insecurity has consistently emerged as the most 
frequently reported need and contributing factor to protection 
risks. While initial concerns focused on displacement and access 
to emergency shelter, recent snapshots demonstrate a transition 
toward a prolonged housing crisis characterized by rising rental 
costs, increasing eviction threats, overcrowding, and exploitation 
within the private rental market. The near saturation of collective 
shelters, particularly in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, has forced 
increasing numbers of displaced households into informal tented 
settlements, unfinished buildings, garages, and other substandard 
accommodation. With most displaced populations residing 
outside formal shelter arrangements, exposure to arbitrary 
eviction, excessive rent increases, insecure tenure, and 
exploitative landlord practices continues to increase. This trend 
is expected to worsen as displacement beco mes more 
protracted, and humanitarian resources remain constrained. 
  
2. Repeated Displacement and Insecurity Continue to 
Drive Protection Risks: A recurring trend across all snapshots 
is the impact of ongoing hostilities, displacement orders, and 
repeated population movements. Families continue to 
experience multiple cycles of displacement, often returning to 
damaged areas only to flee again followin g renewed insecurity. 
This pattern has intensified psychological distress, depleted 
household resources, disrupted livelihoods, and reduced access 
to services. Repeated displacem ent is also contributing to 
increased reliance on unsafe coping mechanisms and exposing 
affected populations to heightened protection risks, particularly 
women, children, older persons, and persons with disabilities. 
  
3. Economic Vulnerability Increas es Exploitation Risks : 
Protection concerns linked to economic hardship have steadily 
increased throughout the reporting period. Households 
increasingly report negative coping strategies, including child 
labour, debt accumulation, reduced consumption, exploitative 
employment, and  unsafe livelihood practices. Post December 
2024 arrivals appear to face some of the highest levels of 
exploitation, including extortion, labour exploitation, degrading 
treatment, intimidation, and exposure to unsafe working 
conditions. Women and adolescent girls appear particularly 
vulnerable to exploitative labour arrangements due to limited 
livelihood opportunities, legal barriers, and financial pressures. 
  
4. Documentation and Legal Status Continue to Restrict 
Access to Rights and Services : Legal residency and 
documentation-related barriers remain a persistent protection 
concern, particularly for displaced Syrians, migrants, and newly 
displaced populations. Lack of legal status in the country as well 
as civil documentation continues to hinder access to healthcare, 
education, humanitarian assistance, freedom of movement, and 
legal remedies. Most community members, specifically post -
December 2024 arrivals from Syria shared their perception that 
lack of legal status or basic legal documentation increases  their 
risk of arrest or detention at checkpoints .   Self-restrictions on 
movement, along with administrative measures such as curfews, 
movement restrictions, and docume ntation requirements, 
continue to limit access to essential services and livelihood 
opportunities. These barriers further exacerbate vulnerabilities 
among already marginalized groups. 
 
5. Unequal access of non-Lebanese displaced Becomes 
More Pronounced: A growing trend across recent reporting 
periods is the increased reporting of unequal access to services 
among displaced Syrians and other non -Lebanese populations. 
Key Informants  increasingly report barriers to shelter, 
healthcare, livelihoods, and humanitarian assistance linked to 
nationality, documentation status, and social tensions. Syrian 
households are often perceived as having least accommodation 
options and face greater difficulties accessing assistance and safe 
housing. These concerns are occurring against a backdrop of 
increasing pressure on public services, competition over 
resources, and a maintained negative public perception  toward 
Syrians. 
  
6. Child Protection Risks Continue to Increase : Child 
labour has remained a consistently and highly reported concern 
by communities  throughout the reporting period. Reports 
indicate growing exposure of children to informal labour, highly 
risky street-based activities, including begging, particularly 
amongst economically vulnerable households. In addition, 
repeated displacement, insecurity, school disruption, and 
deteriorating household economic conditions continue to 
expose children to heightened psychosocial distress and 
protection risks. Children in conflic t-affected areas also face 
ongoing exposure to violence, destruction, and traumatic events.  
  
7. Persons with Disabilities and Individuals with Specific 
Needs Face Persistent Barriers : Across all snapshots, 
persons with disabilities, older persons, individuals with chronic 
health conditions, and children with sdisabilities have consistently 
been identified as among the most vulnerable groups. Significant 
barriers to inclusive information and referrals to relevant 
services such as  healthcare, rehabilitation services, assistive 
devices, accessible shelter, and humanitarian assistance remain 
widespread. The increasing reliance on overcrowded and 
informal shelter arrangements has further exacerbated exclusion 
risks and reduced access to specialized services.

8. Mental Health and Psychosocial Distress are 
Increasing Across Population Groups:  Psychological 
distress has become increasingly prominent throughout the 
reporting period. Repeated displacement, insecurity, loss of 
housing, uncertainty regarding the future  and protracted 
displacement situations, financial hardship, and family separation 
continue to negatively affect mental well -being. Children, 
caregivers, female -headed households, and individuals exposed 
to repeated displacement appear particularly affected. 
Humanitarian actors consistently rep ort growing mental health 
and psychosocial support needs across affected communities  
with limited ability to refer high risk cases to relevant services. 
Women and men also requested dedicated psychosocial support 
programs such as group sessions or art and recreational sessions 
for adults to process the difficult circumstances and traumatic 
incidents witnessed by displaced families. 
  
9. Gender-Based Violence Risks Remain Elevated : While 
GBV risks have been consistently present throughout the crisis, 
recent snapshots indicate that overcrowded collective shelters, 
inadequate privacy, poor lighting, insufficient lockable facilities, 
and limited safe spaces continue to create conditions  that 
increase risks for women and girls. An initial report based on 
findings from 180 collective sites (CS) indicates a predominantly 
unsafe environment, particularly for women and girls. Key 
concerns include a lack of privacy , around 50% of shelters lack  
internal locks, while approximately 30% are overcrowded and

without partitioning. W aSH facilities are similarly inadequate, 
with about 25% lacking locks or sex segregation, further 
compromising privacy and safety. Access barriers for persons 
with disabilities remain pervasive, with roughly 45% of sites 
lacking inclusive access and safe sp aces. Inadequate lighting, 
alongside unassigned and unlit rooms, further heightens 
protection risks. Additionally, there is a notable absence of PSEA

in approximately 40% of locations.  
 
Female-headed households, adolescent girls, and women with 
disabilities remain among the most vulnerable groups. Funding 
constraints also threaten the continuity of critical GBV 
prevention and response services. 
  
10. Social Tensions and Perceptions of Inequitable 
Assistance are Increasing :  In recent reports analyzing the 
social tensions across communities and regions, a marked 
increase in social tensions across Lebanon, driven by mass 
displacement, strained shelter capacity, and mounting pressure 
on host communities is reported.  Protection Monitoring 
partners highlight that the competition over scarce resources, 
perceptions of unequal assistance, political grievances, and 
frustrations linked to prolonged displacement are increasingly 
contributing to tensions between communities and instituti ons. 
Mistrust between host and displaced populations is being 
amplified through online narratives, including the circulation of 
voice notes urging residents to verify the identities of individuals 
in shelters. Public debate has also intensified around government 
decisions, such as the move to proceed with official 
Baccalaureate examinations , triggering protests and sustained 
criticism, particularly in Tripoli. Social polarization is further 
exacerbated by reactions to regional geopolitical developments 
and national infrastructure projects, which fuel existing tension 
and discourse online. Overall, an increasingly active and 
fragmented information environment is contributing to rising 
social fragmentation, heightened public mobilization, and growing 
risks of inter -communal tensions amid persistent insecurity and 
resource co nstraints. Negative perceptions toward displaced 
Syrians and concerns regarding aid distribution have become 
visible in recent reporting periods and risk undermining social 
cohesion if left unaddressed. 
 
Population Profile of Displaced  
 
The profile of IDPs remains diverse and marked by pre -existing 
vulnerabilities. While the ceasefire formally remains in effect, 
hostilities have continued, including recurring airstrikes in 
southern Lebanon. During the final week of May, Israeli military 
operations inside Lebanon intensified, with a significant increase 
in airstrikes reportedly resulting in the deadliest period since the 
ceasefire came into effect. 
 
Women and girls continue to be disproportionately affected by 
the crisis. UNFPA estimates that, in the absence of immediate 
intervention, approximately 90,000 women and girls may lose 
access to mobile GBV services, safe spaces, and reproductive 
health care in the coming month7. Non-Lebanese populations,
including displaced Syrians and migrants, remain among most 
severely impacted by the crisis. Prolonged and repeated 
displacement, deteriorating socioeconomic conditions, and 
heightened psychosocial stress continue to exacerbate 
protection risks and vulnerabilities among these populations. 8 In 
addition to female-headed households and adolescent girls, GBV 
safety audit have identified persons with disabilities as facing 
heightened protection risks and barriers to accessing services. 9

In addition, healthcare personnel continue to face heightened 
risks amid ongoing hostilities. Between 21 and 25 May, nine

Displacement Dynamics & Movement Patterns 
Between 21–24 May, nine attacks on healthcare workers killed 
eight and injured 45, including around 25 staff wounded near 
Hiram Hospital in the south on 23 May. The same day, an 
airstrike destroyed the Civil Defense Center in Nabatieh. 12 
Airstrikes across the South and Nabatieh (including Burj al -
Shmali, Kawthariyat al -Riz, Habboush, Maarakeh, and Salaa) 
reportedly killed 31 and injured 40, 13 while MSF noted hundreds 
of strikes worsening conditions.14 
 
At least 35 displacement orders were issued during the reporting 
period related to 149 localities in South, Nabatieh and Bekaa, 
triggering population movements, especially  in Tyre and its 
surroundings as well as for the first time also Nabatieh city 15, 
creating elevated levels of panic 16. According to MSF, the 
situation is alarming, with large-scale forced displacement orders 
impacting most of the south of the country.17

There is a growing pattern of families returning only to flee again, 
particularly in the southern suburbs of Beirut. As insecurity 
persists, affected populations continue to move between areas in 
search of safety , h owever, these repeated movements are 
increasingly constrained by financial pressures and worsening 
living conditions, making it harder for families to sustain 
displacement. Key informant interviews with displaced Syrians 
conducted across the country confirmed that displaced people 
face significant challenges, including overcrowded shelter 
conditions and limited ability to afford rental accommodation.18 
 
Most displaced Syrians and Palestine refugees rely on informal 
hosting with relatives and communities rather than CSs, reducing 
use of formal facilities but limiting visibility on needs and 
increasing pressure on already vulnerable hosts. Overcrowding 
is a growing concern, while CSs like Sibline Training Centre still 
host significant numbers. New population movements were 
reported from Tyre, surrounding villages, and gatherings towards 
Saida and nearby Palestine refugee camps, particularly Ein el -
Hilweh and Mieh Mieh. Additional displacement was also 
reported following evacuation orders affecti ng areas where 
Palestinian refugees reside, including Adloun and Sarafand.19 
 
Access to Shelter 
Several areas across Lebanon, including Beirut, ML (Aley, Chouf), 
the South (Saida), and North, continue to host large numbers of 
IDPs20 Just around 13% of IDPs are residing in CSs 21 while the 
majority is compelled to seek alternative arrangements, including 
staying with host families, occupying unfinished buildings, setting 
up informal shelters, or even sleeping in public spaces and open 
areas. In the first week of June, only 3% of CS s remained with 
some capacity to accommodate displaced.22  
 
The shelter crisis is acute across multiple areas. CSs in B/ML are 
full, prompting authorities to consider tented accommodation. In 
the South Governorate (Tyre and Saida), shelters are also full, 
pushing more displaced people into open spaces , Meanwhile, 
North Lebanon has become the main area receiving new 
displaced populations, while the Bekaa Governorate continues to 
absorb arrivals and expand capacity by preparing additional 
schools as shelters. 
 
The displacement patterns indicate a growing reliance on internal 
tented settlements, in particular in Akkar and Bekaa , with 
displaced families showing a preference for areas where 
humanitarian aid is concentrated, despite substandard living 
conditions.23

Restrictions to Freedom of Movement 
 
IDPs have consistently reported an inability to return to their 
areas of origin due to ongoing security incidents such as 
airstrikes.25 In addition, eviction risks inside CSs were reported 
by IDPs in Beirut, pressuring residents to return to Dahyeh  and 
make room for new IDPs to reside in the shelter. The residents 
are hesitant to return to Dahyeh for a number of reasons, 
including security concerns and damaged or destroyed homes. 
Moreover, in Baalbek, the governor issued a directive for CSs to 
be vacated, citing improved security conditions in areas of origin. 
This directive was reported despite some IDPs’ areas of origin 
being within active conflict zones. IOM reported that in the Shlifa 
school in Baalbek, families were evicted despite their homes 
being damaged and uninhabitable.26 
 
Legal Status, Documentation, and Access to 
Justice 
Lacking civil documentation has reportedly created challenges for 
IDPs who need to access basic services and rights. This impacts  
access to education and healthcare, where IDPs w ith no  valid 
documentation face challenges to enroll their children to school 
or obtain medical help . Non-Lebanese specifically face issues 
related to freedom of movement  due to absence of legal 
residency, and many fear passing through checkpoints fear ing 
arrest and deportation. Migrants also continue to face legal 
vulnerabilities, particularly those who work informally and do not 
have contracts.27

Child Protection Risks and Family Separation 
According to KIs, child labor continues to be prevalent  in many 
parts of the country . In B /ML job losses among adults led t hat 
more and more children are seen working in mechanic shops, 
delivery services and in construction across Sawfar, Borj 
Hammoud and Aalmet. According to KIIs in the North, child 
labor has reportedly become regular. In the Bekaa and Baalbek, 
KIIs indicated that children as young as six can be seen begging in 
the streets. Conversely in the South, KIIs indicated that child 
labor is not as common as it once was due to the escalating 
situation and livelihood collapse. However, KIIs in the South did 
indicate that children are constantly being exposed to extreme 
situations and mental trauma.  
 
GBV Risks  
According to observations from 180 CSs, shelters are 
significantly “protection -compromised”, with risks driven by 
structural and systemic gaps rather than isolated incidents. Key 
concerns include overcrowding, lack of internal privacy, absence 
of locks and partitions, insufficient lighting and limited availability 
of safe spaces.  Also, the qualitative data reveals a deeper and 
more complex protection environment, where GBV risks are 
shaped not only by physical conditions but also by management 
practices, social tensions, systemic exclusion, and limited 
accountability mechanisms.31

Mental Health and Psychosocial Need 
IDPs have reported trauma due to damaged and destroyed 
homes, anxiety about their children’s futures, and stress related 
to eviction. For migrants in particular, distress linked to family 
separation, chronic hardship and the inability to support 
dependents was reported.33 
 
Inter-Communal Tensions 
While no major incidents were reported regarding 
intercommunal tensions, a recent survey data indicate that 80% 
of Lebanese respondents perceive the  continued presence of 
displaced Syrians in Lebanon as placing significant pressure on 
public resources.34 
 
Intra-Lebanese tensions remained closely linked to evolving 
political and security dynamics. These tensions are particularly 
evident in discussions related to potential future pathways and 
outcomes.35 
 
Perceptions of unfair distribution of humanitarian aid were also 
cited as sources of tensions during the last week of May. The 
main concern was reportedly the inadequate or insufficient 
amount of aid being provided in comparison to the scale of the 
displacement-related needs.36 
 
 
Priority Protection Response 
Gaps and Recommendations 
Th below recommendations are  aimed at supporting sector 
members to implement relevant action to enhance the 
protection advocacy, response and cross-sectoral operations. 
 
Shelter and Overcrowding:  
• Enhance analysis of shelter-specific risks and strengthen 
protection mainstreaming across collective 
sites, in collaboration with Shelter Sector partners. 
• Work with Shelter, Cash, and Legal actors to ensure 
integrated responses combining financial 
assistance, legal aid, and safe housing solutions. 
• Present GBV Safety Audit findings  at the Shelter 
Sector Working Group and agree on concrete actions 
to address identified gaps. 
• Integrate protection risk analysis into shelter 
programming, including overcrowding, safety, 
accessibility, and privacy concerns. 
• Scale up shelter support and alternative 
accommodation options  to reduce overcrowding, 
particularly in high-displacement areas.

Legal Identity and Documentation 
• Expand mobile legal assistance and 
documentation support, including replacement and 
renewal of civil documents and residency processes. 
• Prioritize outreach to non-Lebanese populations, 
migrants, and newly displaced households , 
particularly those facing movement restrictions or 
exclusion due to documentation issues. 
• Strengthen legal awareness and counselling  to 
reduce risks of arrest, detention, and exploitation linked 
to legal status. 
• Improve cross-sector referrals (protection, 
health, education, shelter)  to address 
documentation-related barriers to services and 
assistance. 
 
  
Inter-Sectoral Coordination:   
• Strengthen coordination between Protection, 
Shelter, Health, Education, WASH, Cash, and 
Livelihoods actors  to ensure integrated, holistic 
responses. 
• Enhance information-sharing, referral pathways, 
and case management systems , particularly for 
persons with disabilities, GBV survivors, at-risk children, 
and other vulnerable groups. 
• Integrate protection considerations into all 
sectoral responses, including shelter, livelihoods, and 
service delivery. 
• Advocate collectively for increased humanitarian 
funding to sustain critical protection and multisectoral 
services amid escalating needs. 
• Promote conflict-sensitive programming and 
social cohesion approaches  to address rising 
tensions and inequalities. 
 
Access to Services:   
• Ensure sustained and equitable access to healthcare, 
MHPSS, education, child protection, and GBV 
services, particularly in displacement -affected and 
conflict-affected areas. 
• Expand mobile and community -based service 
delivery models to overcome access barriers related 
to insecurity, transportation costs, and movement 
restrictions. 
• Increase targeted support for women, girls, children, 
persons with disabilities, older persons, 
migrants, and refugees facing compounded barriers. 
• Ensure non-discriminatory, needs-based access to 
services, with particular attention to marginalized non-
Lebanese populations. 
• Integrate inclusive and accessible approaches , 
including reasonable accommodation for persons with 
disabilities. 
 
Freedom of Movement:   
• Advocate against premature, forced, or unsafe 
returns, ensuring that all returns are voluntary, safe, 
dignified, and informed. 
• Monitor and document movement restrictions, 
curfews, checkpoint -related risks, and forced 
returns, and their protection implications. 
• Provide targeted legal counselling, information 
services, and community awareness  on rights 
related to freedom of movement and documentation. 
• Advocate for reduced administrative and informal 
barriers that restrict access to services, livelihoods, 
and humanitarian assistance. 
 
Cross-Cutting Recommendations 
• Collaborate with all sectors to ensure reasonable 
accommodation measures  in shelter, WASH, 
health, and education services. 
• Integrate disability-disaggregated data collection  
into assessments and monitoring. 
• Strengthen community engagement and 
accountability systems  to ensure affected 
populations inform response priorities. 
• Enhance protection monitoring and analysis  to 
track evolving risks (especially eviction trends, 
exploitation, and discrimination). 
• Ensure gender-sensitive and survivor -centered 
approaches across all interventions. 
• Improve inter-sector coordination , particularly 
between Protection, Shelter, Cash, Livelihoods, Health, 
and Education sectors.
