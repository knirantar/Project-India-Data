PROTECTION RISK ANALYSIS  
 RENK RESPONSE TO SUDAN CRISIS (JAN – DEC 2025) 
 
SUMMARY 
Since the conflict erupted in Sudan in April 2023, Renk County has emerged as a vital entry 
point for refugees, asylum-seekers, returnees, and third-country nationals escaping violence. 
Between January and December 2025, a total of 220,044 individuals fleeing entered through 
the Wunthou/Joda official border point and various unofficial crossing points, with around 54% 
of them being South Sudanese returnees. 
Reports gathered at reception sites consistently emphasized assaults on civilians, coerced 
displacement, both physical and psychological torture, arbitrary detentions, separation of 
families, sexual violence, targeted killings of South Sudanese individuals, forced recruitment, 
property extortion, and pervasive trauma as the most common violations and dang ers that 
incoming individuals faced during their journey. 
The combined effects of these experiences have led to significant psychological distress for 
many newcomers, especially those with specific needs (PSNs) such as individuals with 
disabilities, older adults, unaccompanied and separated children, and households led by 
women. 
Upon their arrival, individuals may either stay at the Transit Center (TC) or integrate into the 
host community. However, the strain on services and decreased funding, particularly due to 
the three -month halt of onward transportation in early 2025, have resulted in ongoing 
overcrowding at the TC, which accommodated 10,949 individuals as of December 2025, three 
times its designed capacity.  
This context, combined with limited socio ‑economic opportunities and strained humanitarian 
services, has created and exacerbated multiple protection risks affecting displaced 
populations across Renk.

PROTECTION RISKS FACED IN SUDAN 
1. Loss of Property and Documentation1: Numerous refugees and returnees have lost vital 
belongings and civil documents while fleeing, whether due to extortion, forced confiscation, or 
the need to sell their possessions to meet basic needs. Significantly, nearly 50% of those 
arriving in 2025 reported having lost, misp laced, or being unable to bring their documents 
across the border. Lacking documentation, these individuals encounter challenges in adapting 
to life in the TC or host community, making them increasingly susceptible to fraud, exploitation, 
harassment, and limited access to services. 
3. Child and forced family separation: Numerous people have reported being disconnected 
from their families during times of conflict or displacement. This risk is especially pronounced 
for those who are forcibly returned without the chance to collect their belongings or notify and 
depart with family members. 
Unaccompanied and separated children 
were particularly impacted by forced returns 
from Sudan, where they were frequently 
taken against their will without any chance to 
find or communicate with their families. 
Arriving alone in South Sudan increases their 
vulnerability to neglect, exploitation, 
trafficking, abuse, and recruitment into 
dangerous or exploitative activities. Efforts for 
family tracing and reunification were initiated 
for many of these children, but progress was hindered by limited communication channels, 
poor cross-border coordination, and the ongoing instability in Sudan. 
The separation of families leads to significant emotional turmoil and puts individuals —
especially children, women, and the elderly —at risk of neglect, exploitation, trafficking, and 
abuse, along with challenges in tracing family members across borders. 
4. Psychological/ emotional abuse and inflicted distress : The risk of detention, forced 
repatriation, and the potential loss or separation from family and belongings contributes to a 
significant level of psychological distress. This issue is exacerbated by the harsh living 
conditions in Sudan and the scarcity of support services, which maintain high levels of anxiety 
and uncertainty. 
Children and teenagers enduring prolonged uncertaint y, insufficient services, and extreme 
economic challenges have increasingly turned to negative coping strategies. These include 
engaging in dangerous or exploitative labor, taking on informal and unsafe jobs, and even 
traveling to different regions in search of food or assistance—actions that put them at risk of 
arrest, abuse, trafficking, and renewed conflict. Adolescent girls are especially at risk of early 
and forced marriages, transactional relationships, and sexual exploitation due to their limited 
access to education and livelihood opportunities. Boys, particularly those who are 
unaccompanied, face increased dangers related to unsafe movement, substance abuse, peer 
pressure, and recruitment into harmful activities. For many families, these detrimental coping 
mechanisms are desperate measures driven by the need for survival, a lack of viable 
alternatives, and inadequate humanitarian assistance.

5. Gender -based Violence (GBV):  In Sudan, women and girls have reported facing 
detention, forced labor, sexual exploitation, and forced marriage by both the RSF and 
SAF, along with physical abuse, trafficking risks, and significant psychological distress. 
The ongoing conflict has greatly disrupted vital services and social support systems, 
exacerbating economic con ditions for women and girls, which severely restricts 
survivors' ability to obtain protection, seek help, and pursue justice.

6. Access to Justice 2: From January to December 2025, a total of 692 individuals (78.5% 
men and 21.5% women) sought legal assistance. Although there has been a rise in awareness 
regarding rights and grievance mechanisms, access to justice is still hindered by a shortage 
of legal service providers, protracted resolution processes, and deficiencies within the formal 
justice system. As a result, numerous survivors of violations choose not to seek justice, which 
leaves significant issues unaddressed and heightens the risks of ongoing abuse or impunity. 
7. Gender-based Violence (GBV): Gender-Based Violence continued to be a significant and 
ongoing protection issue throughout 2025 in Renk County, impacting women and girls in both 
displaced and host communities. 
Reported incidents encompassed various 
forms of violence. Psychological and 
emotional abuse was the most reported, 
making up 79% of all cases, followed by  
physical assault (41%), denial of 
resources (25%), and sexual violence, 
which included rape (14%) and sexual 
assault (8%). Furthermore, early and 
forced marriage (5%) was noted, 
emphasizing the growing pressure on families facing severe economic difficult ies and social 
instability. 
Several underlying risk factors significantly contribute to these trends. Many women and girls 
fleeing Sudan arrive with a history of violence, trauma, and family separation. Once in Renk, 
the combination of limited job opportun ities, overcrowded living conditions in the Transit 
Center, restrictive cultural norms, and stress related to displacement heightens the risk of GBV. 
Domestic violence is particularly prevalent, with women reporting experiences of emotional 
abuse, physical assault, and financial hardship within their households. These dynamics are 
often worsened by the breakdown of traditional support systems and the pressure on families 
adjusting to displacement. 
Despite the magnitude of the issue, underreporting remained a significant concern. Survivors 
often encounter stigma, shame, fear of retaliation, and a lack of trust in formal reporting 
mechanisms. These obstacles prevent many from seeking timely help, leaving them exposed 
to ongoing harm and hindering the capacity of service providers to fulfill their roles. 
8. Psychological/ emotional abuse and inflicted distress : The experiences of conflict, 
displacement, violence, and loss left a deep psychological imprint on affected populations.

Many accompanied children show 
significant psychological distress, as 
indicated by caregiver reports of 
behavioral changes such as nightmares, 
aggression, and withdrawal. These 
reactions stem from cumulative trauma 
due to conflict, displacement, violence 
exposure, and future uncertainty. The lack

psychosocial support leaves many children 
without the necessary support to process

This emotional turmoil is exacerbated by difficult conditions in Transit Centers and ho st 
communities, characterized by overcrowding, poor food supply, lack of privacy, limited 
educational opportunities, and caregiver stress due to job loss and displacement. This 
environment undermines family dynamics, increasing children's vulnerability to violence, 
neglect, and harmful social norms. Limited mental health and psychosocial support services, 
a shortage of professionals, and stigma prevent access to necessary care, leading to negative 
coping strategies like substance abuse, domestic violence, child labor, and early marriage. 
PROTECTION ENABLERS AND CHALLENGES  
 32 protection partners (including GBV, Child 
Protection, HLP , and MHPSS) supported 
displaced and host communities in 2025. 
 A functional referral pathway allowed for timely 
identification, and support for individuals with 
specific protection needs. 
 C4P, food, and non-food items were provided 
based on assessed vulnerabilities  
 Family tracing, reunification, and communication 
services facilitated reunification throughout South 
Sudan. 
 Integrated Health and Protection services in the 
eastern corridor  
 Community engagement initiatives included 
awareness sessions, group PSS, community 
dialogues, and skills training aimed at mitigating 
protection risks. 
 Capacity-building efforts for humanitarian 
partners, local authorities, and community leaders  
 Protection assessments and monitoring informing 
program design, advocacy, and prioritizing at-risk 
groups. 
 Onward transportation support allowed for the 
safe movement of over 10,000 refugees and 
returnees. 
 1,008 households were integrated in Abukadra, 
contributing to early solutions and alleviating 
congestion in temporary sites. 
 Significant funding limitations resulted in the 
downsizing of protection programs and a 
decrease in operational capacity.  
 Reduced personnel and mobility diminished field 
presence and restricted the coverage of 
prevention activities, particularly in the eastern 
and southern corridors.  
 A lack of case management staff and specialized 
services, in relation to the scale of needs, led to 
delays and unaddressed protection issues.  
 Limited facilities and overstretched services 
obstructed effective responses for individuals 
needing immediate or specialized assistance.  
 Unresolved cases and extended waiting times 
heightened vulnerability and contributed to new 
protection risks.  
 Resource shortages created obstacles to 
achieving sustainable, long-term, and durable 
protection outcomes for affected populations.
