MARKET OVERVIEW
Market Monitoring: Shelter and Household Items
22-29 June 2026 | Gaza Strip
Context & Rationale
As of June 2026, the humanitarian situation across the Gaza Strip remains critical following renewed hostilities, repeated 
displacement, and continued restrictions on the movement of goods and people. Widespread destruction of housing, 
infrastructure, and essential service networks continues to severely affect living conditions, with many households residing 
in damaged buildings, makeshift shelters, or overcrowded displacement sites. Shelter-related needs remain acute across all 
governorates, while limited recovery efforts and ongoing insecurity continue to constrain access to adequate housing and basic 
services1. 
Markets remain under severe pressure. Restrictions on commercial inflows, disruptions to supply chains, and ongoing insecurity 
have significantly reduced the availability of shelter materials, tools, and essential household items. Vendors report persistent 
shortages of key commodities, irregular market operations, and substantial price volatility, limiting households’ ability to 
meet basic shelter and household needs through local markets². These constraints are further compounded by widespread 
displacement, reduced purchasing power, and continued reliance on humanitarian assistance. 
In this context, timely and evidence-based market monitoring remains essential to inform humanitarian shelter programming. 
Conducted under the Shelter Cluster’s Technical Working Group on Cash and Markets for Shelter, this assessment provides a 
snapshot of market functionality, commodity availability, and price trends for key shelter, repair, and household items based 
on vendor perspectives. The findings aim to identify market constraints and inform evidence-based, market-sensitive shelter 
responses across the Gaza Strip.
Key Findings
• Nearly all assessed items were reported as having either no availability or low availability across all governorates 
in the Gaza Strip. The only exceptions were regular adhesive duct tape (non-construction quality) and re-used metal 
rebar, which were reported as widely available in Khan Yunis only.
• Construction materials were largely reported as unavailable, reflecting the continued constraints on recovery and 
rehabilitation activities. Key construction materials, including cement, metal rebars, and corrugated galvanized iron (CGI) 
sheets, were widely reported as having limited or no availability across assessed markets.
• The overall availability of shelter items declined compared with the February 2026 assessment3. Since the previous 
round, the availability of several key shelter items has deteriorated, reflecting continued instability in shelter-related 
markets. This decline is driven by ongoing restrictions on the import of essential materials, resulting in a growing number 
of shelter items being reported as completely unavailable.
• Market activity in June remained low across the Gaza Strip. Most vendors reported low to very low levels of activity, 
with a higher proportion indicating reduced market activity compared with the February 2026 round. Limited market 
functionality was attributed to supply shortages, high transportation costs, customs fees, reliance on a single crossing for 
commercial and humanitarian inflows, and elevated supplier prices.
• Commercial suppliers remained the primary source of shelter, household, and tools/site-level items, underscoring 
the continued role of formal market actors in supplying goods across the Gaza Strip. Individuals selling their own 
goods were reported as the second most common source of commodities, highlighting the role of secondary markets 
in complementing limited commercial supply. This pattern may reflect increased circulation of used goods and could 
indicate broader economic pressures affecting households, including potential reliance on asset sales as a coping strategy.

Item Availability and Prices
• Reported item availability is based on vendors’ perceptions during the data collection period. Vendors assessed the 
availability of item categories corresponding to the goods they sell (e.g., a fuel vendor reported on all energy-related items). Findings 
therefore reflect market conditions during the week of data collection only, as availability can change rapidly. Partners may use 
these findings as a reference when designing cash and voucher assistance approaches, while complementing them with their own 
verification and context-specific assessments in the targeted governorates.
• During the June data collection round, most energy items, shelter items, household items, and tools and site-level items were 
reported as having limited or no availability across the Gaza Strip. Ongoing economic pressures continued to strain market 
supply, while humanitarian needs for emergency shelter and household items remained extremely high, with many essential items 
requiring regular replenishment4. 
• Fuel and cooking gas were also consistently reported as having low availability. In response to shortages of gas cylinders, 
vendors reported adapting sales practices by selling cooking gas based on the quantity purchased (by kilogram), rather than through 
full cylinder exchanges. Reported prices ranged from 90–120 ILS per kg, substantially exceeding the official price of 74 ILS per kg 
set by the relevant authority at the time of data collection. Kerosene (locally known as kaz) has not entered the Gaza Strip since 
October 2023. As a result, fuel prices reported in this assessment refer to solar (the local term for diesel fuel), which is currently used 
for generators, water pumps, and, in some cases, vehicles. Imported solar was reported at a fixed price of 35 ILS per litre, while locally 
produced ranged between 15–25 ILS per litre.
• Most building materials, including cement and new concrete blocks were widely reported as completely unavailable across 
the Gaza Strip. Cement was reported as having limited availability only in Gaza Governorate. Old (salvaged) concrete blocks 
were also reported as having limited availability, with median prices ranging between 5–7.5 ILS per block. Cement available in the 
market at lower prices was described as being of poor quality and sold in 1 kg quantities. In addition, wholesalers in Gaza reported 
the availability of newly supplied locally treated cement, sold in 25 kg bags at approximately 750 ILS.
• The availability of regular and heavy adhesive duct tape declined compared to February and was reported as limited across 
markets. Newly monitored items, including summer blankets and mattresses, were also reported as having limited availability, 
with a median price of 35 ILS for summer blankets and 180 ILS for mats. Most reported mats measured approximately 3×4 m.
• Solar lights, including both household and site-level types, were reported as completely unavailable across the Gaza Strip, 
except in Khan Younis where limited availability was reported. Household solar lights recorded a median price of 345 ILS. Site-
level solar lights showed a wider price range, with prices ranging between 650–1,500 ILS.
• For construction materials, new CGI sheets were reported as having no to very low availability across the Gaza Strip, with a median 
price of 225 ILS per m². Old CGI sheets were reported at a median price of 50 ILS per m². Timber also remained low in availability 
across the Gaza Strip, consistent with the previous round, and is typically sold in standard dimensions such as 5×2.5 cm and 10×2.5 
cm, with prices varying according to size. The median price for timber measuring 5×2.5 cm was 25 ILS per piece, while timber 
measuring 10×2.5 cm had a median price of 30 ILS per piece. Items introduced newly in this market monitoring round, including 
new metal rebars, were reported as having very low to no availability. Median prices ranged between 65–75 ILS per bar, 
depending on length, with 12 m and 14 m being the most commonly available sizes. In comparison, prices for used metal rebars were 
more variable, reflecting their relatively higher, though still limited, availability across assessed markets. Plywood/timber sheeting also 
exhibited considerable price variability due to differences in availability, type, and thickness. Timber pallets recorded a median price 
of 120 ILS per unit; however, vendors also commonly sold timber pallets by weight, with individual pallets typically weighing 12–15 
kg. The reported price for timber pallets sold by weight ranged between 10–13 ILS per kg, depending on quality.
• Tools were largely reported as having limited availability across the Gaza Strip. Site-level items, including fire extinguishers, 
buckets, and solar lights, also faced significant availability constraints. Fire extinguishers were reported as completely 
unavailable, despite increased needs due to a rise in fire incidents in shelters and displacement sites5. Their unavailability was 
attributed to import restrictions, as they are classified as dual-use items. As an alternative, vendors reported that limited refilling 
services for gas-type fire extinguishers were available through a small number of suppliers, with prices ranging between 200–400 
ILS, depending on extinguisher size and service type. Similarly, items such as buckets, which were reported as widely available 
in the previous round, were reported as having limited availability in the current round. The median price increased to 30 ILS, 
compared with 20 ILS in the previous round.

Market Activity
Market activity across the Gaza Strip remained constrained and 
uneven during June. While some informal and small-scale trading 
continued, particularly for basic food items and essential household 
commodities, overall market functionality was limited by inconsistent 
supply, disrupted trade routes, and continued reliance on a single 
crossing for both humanitarian and commercial cargo during the 
assessed period and preceding weeks. This dependence contributed to 
ongoing uncertainty in market supply and operations. 
According to the Gaza Chamber of Commerce, 568 truckloads of 
shelter materials entered the Gaza Strip between 1 and 28 June, 
equivalent to approximately 20 truckloads per day. Daily inflows fluctu-
ated considerably, ranging from 9 to 57 truckloads per day for shelter 
items, with the majority carrying humanitarian cargo⁶. 
Although commercial inflows increased in June compared with 
May, supply chains remained fragile. The continued reliance on a 
single crossing, combined with fluctuating daily inflows, constrained 
market recovery and the ability of vendors to maintain consistent stock 
levels. Consequently, the availability of several commodities, particu-
larly shelter materials and durable household items, remained limit-
ed, leaving markets highly sensitive to access constraints and supply 
disruptions and reducing households’ ability to meet essential needs 
through local markets.

Type of Supplier 
Across Deir al-Balah, Khan Younis, and Gaza governorates, vendors predominantly reported re-
lying on wholesalers as their main source for shelter items, tools and site-level items. Other 
sources included larger vendors within Gaza supplying a broad client base, followed by smaller 
vendors or individual suppliers with more limited outreach. 
This reliance on wholesalers is consistent with the current structure of Gaza’s commercial supply 
chain. Continued restrictions on commercial imports, fluctuating truck entries, and dependence 
on a limited number of entry points have concentrated the distribution of goods among a rel-
atively small number of wholesalers with access to incoming commercial cargo. Consequently, 
vendors often rely on these wholesalers to replenish stock, limiting opportunities to diversify 
suppliers. Where commercial supply is constrained, vendors also reported sourcing selected com-
modities through secondary markets, including humanitarian assistance entering local markets

Reasons Limiting Vendors’ Acceptance of Mobile Money/E-wallets
Although shelter and household item vendors reported relatively high acceptance of e-wallet and 
mobile money payments during the data collection period, several operational and structural 
barriers continue to limit their use. The most commonly reported constraint was limitations 
related to transaction ceilings and the number of permitted transactions (40%)*, reflecting re-
strictions within digital payment systems and liquidity management constraints. This was followed 
by technical issues in the application used (29%), including system errors and transaction failures. 
Network coverage and internet connectivity issues (19%) also continued to affect transaction reli-
ability, particularly in areas with unstable telecommunications infrastructure. A smaller proportion 
of vendors (6%) reported lack of trust in e-wallet and mobile money systems, highlighting per-
sistent confidence-related barriers.
These findings align with broader evidence on the functionality of digital financial services in 
Gaza, where constrained financial infrastructure, liquidity shortages, and intermittent connectivity 
continue to limit the scalability of cashless payment systems. Humanitarian and market monitoring 
reports have noted that while e-wallets and mobile money platforms have expanded as coping 
mechanisms during periods of cash shortages, their effectiveness remains constrained by sys-
tem-level limitations, inconsistent network performance, and regulatory or operational transaction 
cap10. 
In this context, digital payment systems function primarily as a complementary and constraint-
driven payment modality rather than a full substitute for cash, particularly in volatile market en-
vironments where vendors rely on flexible payment options to manage stock replenishment and 
supplier payments.

Methodology
 
The Gaza Shelter and Household Items Market 
Monitoring Assessment is a quantitative key 
informant (KI) exercise targeting purposively 
sampled vendors within their respective market 
areas. The assessment was conducted under 
the Shelter Cluster’s Technical Working Group 
on Cash and Markets for Shelter and focused 
on market access, item availability, and pricing. 
The scope of the tool was expanded to include 
shelter materials, tools, site-level items, and 
core household items, in coordination with 
Shelter Cluster partners and the Site Manage-
ment Cluster. The expanded item list incorpo-
rated transitional shelter materials, including 
CGI sheets, plywood, and concrete blocks, as 
well as additional tools and site-level items 
relevant to current shelter and displacement 
site needs. 
Data collection was conducted independently, 
with partners contributing to the refinement of 
the item list but not participating directly in the 
data collection process.
Markets are selected based on field capacity 
and access, as well as field teams’ assessments 
of their relative importance within each gov-
ernorate. The most significant marketplaces 
were identified based on findings from the 
previously conducted round of the exercise11 
and previously conducted market mapping in 
October 202512.
A standardized data collection tool was used 
to collect data on the price and availabili-
ty of selected shelter, tools and household 
items, alongside key challenges reported from 
vendors’ perspectives. Prices are reported per 
specified unit, such as per kilogram or liter for 
items typically sold by weight, unless an item is 
commonly sold as an individual unit (e.g. tent) 
or by area or length, such as plastic sheeting 
measured in square meters.
Data collection
In each assessed market location, the
target was to collect at least three prices per
shelter and household item, where feasible,
from different vendors to support data quality
and consistency. However, due to limited item
availability in Gaza, this criterion was not met
for all items in some municipalities. Data collec-
tion was conducted between 22–29 June
2026 including remote and in person data col-
lection. Remote data collection leveraged a Key
Informant network comprising of 311 vendors
and a total 750 surveys in 15 municipalities
across four governorates: Khan Younis, Deir al
Balah, Gaza Governorate, with limited coverage
in Northern Gaza.
Identification of Vendors
Vendors are retailers or individuals selling 
directly to consumers. Moreover, vendors sell 
at least one category of goods included in this 
assessment and the person interviewed was the 
owner of the shop, or someone who had suffi-
cient knowledge about the business operations.
Enumerators and training
Data were collected both in-person and 
remotely by trained field staff with strong 
familiarity with local market conditions and 
dynamics. Enumerators received comprehen-
sive training from a facilitator experienced in 
the assessment methodology, data collection 
tools, and quality assurance procedures. Data 
collection was conducted using the Kobo mo-
bile application. 
To support data quality and monitoring 
throughout the exercise, field teams were also 
provided with a daily tracking to monitor the 
progress of price data collection and ensure 
adequate coverage of assessed markets and 
items.
Data cleaning and analysis
Following data collection, data was compiled 
and cleaned, normalizing prices, cross-checking 
outliers, and calculating the medians and per-
centages of collected market indicators in each 
assessed location.
The median item prices reported in this 
factsheet are ‘location medians’, designed to 
minimise the effects of outliers and differing 
amounts of data among assessed locations. The 
median prices of all assessed items are calculat-
ed within each assessed municipality.
Challenges and Limitations
• The situation in the Gaza Strip is highly 
sensitive and changing rapidly so the 
information presented in this brief only 
provides a snapshot of the situation during 
the reporting period and might become 
outdated quickly. As such, price data is only 
indicative for the time frame within which it 
was collected.  It is important to note that 
ongoing and significant disruptions to supply 
chains, including restrictions on imports and 
movement, play a critical role in shaping 
market trends and the availability of goods, 
adding further volatility to the data.
• Given current access constraints, data 
collection was primarily conducted in markets 
located in areas that remain accessible. 
Consequently, no data were collected from 
Rafah Governorate, while coverage in North 
Gaza Governorate remained limited.
• Due to shortages of shelter items, prices 
for some items were not collected in 
certain municipalities, as these items were 
unavailable in the assessed markets.
• Predefined measurement units did not 
always align with how items were available or 
commonly sold in markets, which may affect 
comparability of price and availability data 
across locations.

Annex 4: Shelter Cluster Operational Recommendations for Partners 
for Shelter Interventions through CVA modality
 
Based on the June 2026 shelter and household items’ market monitoring exercise findings, 
Shelter Cluster partners are encouraged to consider the following recommendations:
1. Prioritize market assessments before selecting assistance modalities. Partners must 
verify local market conditions immediately prior to implementation. Communities needs and 
capacities, agency capacity, along with humanitarian and market access can best inform shelter 
modality.
2. Align programme design with market findings and Shelter Cluster technical guidance. 
Partners are encouraged to align the scope of work and assistance packages or BOQs, based on 
market availability, prices, and in line with wider Shelter Cluster’s technical guidance and cash 
thresholds such as carrying out emergency repairs or improving makeshift shelters. Partners 
should monitor commodity availability, prices, and vendor capacity throughout project imple-
mentation and adapt programmes accordingly.
3. Strengthen market access for affected communities. Where cash and voucher assistance 
is implemented, partners should ensure that targeted households have access to functional 
markets and qualified vendors, especially for vulnerable groups.
4. Integrate livelihoods and Cash-for-Work opportunities where feasible. Partners are 
encouraged to incorporate cash-for-work or livelihood components alongside shelter interven-
tions where context permits to better support local recovery and enhance community resilience.
5. Promote localized approaches. Where feasible, partners should explore localized program-
ming that maximizes the use of locally available skills and resources, including the safe recov-
ery, reuse, and recycling of suitable construction materials. This approach cannot be scaled up 
due to limited material supply and market distortion risks need to be monitored.
