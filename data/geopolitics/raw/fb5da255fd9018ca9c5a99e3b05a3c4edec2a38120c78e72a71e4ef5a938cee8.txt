The Iran–United States crisis in 2026 has evolved from a high -intensity conflict into a fragile and uncertain de -escalation phase, but its 
global repercussions remain significant and ongoing. While recent pauses in military exchanges and indirect negotiations have  reduced 
immediate violence, disruptions to the Strait of Hormuz —through which around one -fifth of global  oil supply flows —have already 
triggered sharp increases in energy prices, supply chain instability, and rising inflation worldwide. These shocks are partic ularly severe 
for energy-importing and developing economies, contributing to slower global growth, higher food and commodity prices, and increased 
economic vulnerability across Asia. Myanmar faces especially deep impacts due to its heavy dependence on imported fuel (around 90–
95% of supply), limited reserves, and fragile economic conditions; the crisis has led to rising fuel prices, shortages, currency 
depreciation, and increased costs of transportation, food, and agricultural production. As a result, the conflict is not only  a geopolitical 
issue but also a major economic shock, disproportionately affecting vulnerable countries like Myanmar, where it intensifies i nflation, 
threatens livelihoods, and exacerbates existing economic instability.  
   
Summary 
• Food insecurity: WFP estimates that if the conflict continues through Q2, at least 45 million additional people globally may face 
acute food insecurity, including over 9 million in Asia. In Myanmar, a further 3.5–5 million people could become food insecure due 
to this crisis, on top of the existing 12.4 million. The crisis is intensifying vulnerabilities, especially in conflict -affected and remote 
areas, as reduced agricultural output, rising prices, and market disruptions strain household food consumption and livelihoods. 
• Fuel prices: Diesel prices have risen by an average of 45 percent nationwide, with Yangon seeing a 42 percent increase. Octane 95 
has increased by 51 percent on average, with spikes in Sittwe (up to nearly eightfold) and Myitkyina (fivefold compared to pre-crisis 
Yangon levels). Prices remain high but have gradually declined since late April. 
• Food basket cost:  As of early Ju ly, the average food basket cost is about 20 percent higher than in late February, driven mainly by 
increases in rice, palm oil, and salt prices. 
• Conflict-affected areas:  Fuel shortages and price increases of  under 60 percent since February have  rationing, and transport 
restrictions, delaying the delivery of food, medicine, and aid. Roadblocks and insecurity continue to drive up staple food prices across 
Kachin, Kayin, Kayah, Sagaing, Rakhine, Magway, and Shan. 
• Agriculture: Production costs remain high due to elevated prices of agricultural inputs, including fertilizer, fuel, seeds, and 
pesticides. Farmers in many regions continue to pay higher fertilizer prices than reference price because of transportation costs and 
supply chain constraints.  In addition, irregular weather  situation, labor shortages, limited access to affordable credit, and volatile 
crop prices continue to undermine agricultural productivity and farmers' incomes. 
  
Fertilizer Supply Risks and Food Security  
• Myanmar is highly dependent on imported fertilizers, which supply an estimated 85–95% of its chemical fertilizer needs, mainly from 
Oman, China, and the Middle East. This reliance makes the country especially vulnerable to current fertilizer supply risks c aused by 
rising fuel prices and export restrictions by major producers, which are coinciding with the main rice planting season. Given  
agriculture’s heavy dependence on fuel and imported urea, any shortages during the monsoon period could severely disrupt 
production. A 50% reduction in fertilizer use could lower national agricultural output by 10–15%, drive up rice prices, and exacerbate 
food insecurity, with fertilizer already representing the largest input cost for farmers. 
• Price outlook: The fertilizer reference prices for July have not yet been announced. As of late June, the reference prices remained at 
MMK 120,000 per 50 kg for urea and MMK 115,000 per 50 kg for DAP in Yangon. However, market prices continue to exceed these 
reference levels in many areas, driven by regional supply disparities, persistent fuel shortages, and elevated transportation costs.

Fuel prices across major townships continued to rise sharply between February (Week 4)  and April (Week 2) . Diesel recorded the 
steepest increases, with market averages in major townships such as Yangon, Mandalay, and Magway more than tripling compared to 
pre-crisis levels. By April (Week 2), diesel prices reached MMK 7,755–8,105 in Yangon and Mawlamyine, while remaining substantially 
higher at around MMK 13,500 in Sittwe and Myitkyina.  Octane 95 prices also followed an upward trend over the same period. Average 
Octane 95 prices increased from approximately MMK 2,400–2,600 in February to MMK 5,065–5,325 in April across most locations. This 
represents a 102–106 percent increase  from pre -crisis levels, confirming that prices have more than doubled nationwide. Absolute 
prices remained highest in Sittwe (MMK 26,500), although proportional increases there were more moderate (65–77 percent).  
 
 Supply chain and import  
❖ Fuel reserves cover - According to the Petroleum Products Supervision and Inspection Department 40 days of petrol and ~ 54 days 
of diesel. 
❖ Rising logistics costs are contributing to higher staple food prices, particularly rice and edible oil, with strongest impacts observed 
in fuel-dependent and remote markets such as Magway, Rakhine, Chin, Kachin and conflict affected areas. 
❖ Humanitarian supply chains remain fragmented , with access constraints driven by insecurity, multiple checkpoints, and ad -hoc 
movement restrictions, increasing delivery times and operational costs. 
❖ Border trade continues to function as a critical supply route, Although the Myanmar-Thai Friendship Bridge 2 (Myawaddy–Mae 
Sot) has reopened, trade remain only partially functional  due to Myanmar export/imports licenses issues, Restrictions on the types 
of goods permitted and limits number of cargo trucks allowed to pass. 
❖ Informal and localized cross-border trade continues to fill gaps in essential goods (fuel, food, household items), reflecting coping 
mechanisms amid constrained formal imports. 
❖ Logistics challenges and fuel mitigation:  The WFP -led Logistics Working Group continues to monitor humanitarian fuel needs. 
While fuel prices have declined from recent peaks and supply availability has improved, prices remain above pre -crisis levels, 
sustaining pressure on humanitarian logistics and transport costs.
