The waters will come in South Sudan. 
Flood resilience must come first.
Over one million people are affected by flooding in South Sudan each year. But following years of a 
response largely centered on humanitarian assistance, how are the Government, UN and partners 
adapting in 2026 in the wake of unprecedented funding cuts, to sustain progress and prevent hard-
won gains from being reversed?
Five years ago, South Sudan experienced the 
most severe flooding in 60 years, affecting 33 
out of 78 counties. Over 800,000 people were 
newly displaced while homes, farmlands and 
infrastructure were submerged under water. 
Economic damages for that year alone were 
estimated at US$671 million. 
Since then, severe flooding has become a grim 
fact of life for many communities, driven by 
intense seasonal rainfall (April to November), 
rising water levels in Lake Victoria with a 
consequent increase in Nile inflows, and the 
erratic impacts of climate change. 
In 2026, the UN and its partners are preparing 
for the worst. Rainfall already came “much 
earlier than usual, ” according to the South 
Sudan Meteorological Services, indicating high 
probability of wetter conditions. The World

For those living in conflict-affected areas, this

A humanitarian origin, a predictable 
crisis?
The flooding response in South Sudan has for 
over a decade operated through an emergency 
clockwork: prepare, assess, respond. Last 
year, humanitarian partners provided shelters, 
sanitation items, cash-for-work opportunities, 
water drainage and dyke support, among other 
assistance in response to new flood-driven 
displacements in 18 counties.
The National Flooding Taskforce, set up 
in 2023, is co-chaired by the Relief and 
Rehabilitation Commission (RRC) and 
United Nations Office for the Coordination 
of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) with 
participation extending to both humanitarian 
and development partners. Its humanitarian 
origin was a reaction to the devastating toll of 
excessive flooding at the time, where life-saving 
actions took precedence over all else. 
But today, flooding carries layers of 
predictability. Hot spots have already been 
mapped out by partners: Jonglei, Northern 
Bahr el Ghazal, Unity and Upper Nile. Risk 
assessments are regularly informed by models 
leveraging digital technology and artificial 
intelligence. Regular conflict-sensitivity 
analysis by the United Nations Mission in South 
Sudan (UNMISS) and peace partners has been 
critical to mitigate risks of communal tensions 
due to new displacements and competition 
over scarce resources.
Anchoring resilience in a volatile 
climate
As the UN and its partners continue to grapple 
with severe resource constraints in 2026, 
the shift from a response largely centered 
on humanitarian assistance to longer-term 
solutions has become increasingly urgent. 
One of the recommendations emerging from 
the 2025 flood after-action review, which 
brought together government authorities, 
aid workers and other stakeholders, calls for 
greater investment in mitigation measures and 
structural interventions to reduce the recurring 
impact of floods. A key lesson is that floods 
should not be viewed solely as a humanitarian 
challenge. With the right investments, seasonal 
water flows can also create opportunities for 
resilience, livelihoods and economic growth. 
This includes improved water management, 
flood-resilient agriculture and activities such 
as rice cultivation, which can help communities 
benefit from, rather than simply endure, 
recurring floods.
“The floods took everything 
from us: our homes, our farms, 
even our animals. We are safe 
behind the dykes, but we dream 
of the day when we can live on 
our own land again and rebuild 
our lives,” said one resident in 
the Bentiu IDP Camp.
The camp is located in Unity - one of the 
worst affected South Sudanese states 
by flooding. Its residents are protected 
from floodwater by dykes, reinforced 
with sandbags each year by the 
International Organization for Migration 
(IOM) and partners, and in coordination 
with state authorities.

Examples of this shift are already emerging. In 
Warrap, rice cultivation was introduced to flood-
prone areas by the World Food Programme 
(WFP) and partners, turning unusable 
floodplains into productive fields. In Northern 
Bahr el Ghazal, UNHCR, the United Nations 
Refugee Agency, and partners are providing 
nearly 600 climate-resilient shelters and latrines 
in three counties this year.
More systematic approaches are also taking 
shape. In Unity, flooding is increasingly 
treated not only as an emergency but also as 
a planning parameter. Integrated investments 
combining safe land, water access, roads and 
agricultural support are enabling communities 
to settle in less flood-prone areas and restart 
productive activities.
At the national level, the National Coordination 
and Operations Center (NCOC) was launched 
in April 2024, supported by the United Nations 
Development Programme (UNDP), and serves 
as a central hub for information dissemination, 
early warnings, and response coordination 
under the leadership of the Government 
of South Sudan. The Government has also 
initiated a climate resilience project aimed 
at establishing the country’s first ever multi-
hazard early warning system.
Policy and coordination frameworks are 
advancing. The Validation Workshop of the 
Revised National Disaster Risk Management 
Policy in May 2026, held by the Government 
in collaboration with IGAD and the UN (FAO, 
UNDP , UNDRR, WFP) marked an important step 
in strengthening disaster risk governance. 
During the same month, UNDP also launched 
a mapping exercise of development initiatives 
to strengthen coordination and identify 
opportunities for greater investment in 
resilience. 
Together, these efforts point towards a more 
development-oriented approach to flood 
management: one that prioritizes resilience, 
climate-informed planning and long-term 
solutions alongside emergency response. As 
resources continue to dwindle and climate 
shocks intensify, getting ahead of the waters is 
no longer simply an option - it is a necessity.

South Sudan’s food paradox
Abundant in arable land that surpasses that of many of its neighbors, South Sudan has the potential 
to become a regional breadbasket. But persistent insecurity, climate shocks, limited market access, 
and other constraints continue to undermine agricultural production. The country spends over USD 
800 million annually on food imports while millions of South Sudanese rely on emergency food aid.
FEATURE
Food insecurity in South Sudan has gradually 
climbed over the years. In ten years, the number 
of people facing crisis or emergency levels 
of food insecurity during the lean season 
jumped from 4.8 million people in 2016 to 
7.8 million in 2026 - more than half of South 
Sudan’s population. Of these, around 73,000 
people are facing “catastrophic” hunger (IPC 5), 
experiencing extreme food consumption gaps 
associated with starvation, heightened risk of 
death, and the collapse of livelihoods.
In parts of states such as Jonglei, Upper Nile, 
Lakes and Central Equatoria, repeated shocks 
have significantly undermined households’ 
access to food and nutrition services. While 
recent displacements have had a relatively 
“marginal impact” on overall crop production, 
it has exacerbated food insecurity in affected 
communities. In areas with renewed fighting 
and other emergencies, food assistance 
remains a lifeline for communities in need. 
However, outside the areas most affected 
by conflict and food insecurity, there are 
significant economic opportunities for the 
country to leverage its 33 million acres of prime 
arable land. South Sudan possesses more 
arable land than neighboring Kenya or Uganda. 
Yet, less than five per cent of this land is 
currently utilized, despite more than 80 per cent 
of the population engaging in rural subsistence 
farming. Low levels of productivity, limited 
infrastructure and market constraints continue 
to contribute to dependence on food imports 
and humanitarian assistance.

The race to agricultural resilience 
In 2025, amidst unprecedented funding cuts, 
the UN and humanitarian leadership in South 
Sudan set shared goals of contributing to a 
net increase of 30,000 metric tons of food 
production, with 10,000 hectares of land 
brought under climate-resilient agricultural 
practices, by mid-2026.
This builds on growing national efforts 
to strengthen agricultural production and 
food systems. For example, in 2024, the 
Government of South Sudan launched the Food 
and Agriculture Delivery Compact, outlining 
political, financial, and partner commitments 
to “transform agriculture and livestock into 
a thriving sector, ” with a focus on sorghum, 
rice, sesame, and fisheries. That same year, 
the United Nations Development Programme 
(UNDP) and the Ministry of Agriculture 
and Food Security launched two initiatives 
aimed at empowering rural enterprises and 
strengthening food systems: the six-year 
Rural Enterprises for Agricultural Development 
(READ) programme and the Empowering 
Women and Youth to Drive Sustainable 
Agriculture (EWYSA) project.
Early results have been encouraging. South 
Sudan’s 2025 net cereal production, estimated 
at 1.2 million tonnes, represents an eight 
per cent increase compared to the previous 
year. The area of harvested cereal land also 
expanded due to increased farming activities, 
including among returnees from Sudan and 
urban households seeking a secondary source 
of income.
However, as external funding continues to 
decline, the need for accelerated risk-informed 
investments that reduce reliance on food 
imports and strengthen the entire agrifood 
system has become increasingly urgent. 
Such investments must extend beyond food 
production to encompass storage, processing, 
transport, market access and value addition. 
This call was echoed at the 2026 National 
Agriculture Conference, hosted in May in 
Juba, under the theme “Investing in Agri-food 
Systems for Better Life in South Sudan”. The 
conference brought together government 
leaders, development partners, the private 
sector and community representatives. 
Participants emphasized the importance 
of expanding private sector-led investment 
across crop, livestock and fisheries value 
chains, supported by greater investments 
in mechanization, irrigation, infrastructure, 
market development and value addition. The 
conference also adopted a formal Communiqué 
recommending, among other measures, that 
the national government allocate at least 10 per 
cent of the national budget to agriculture in line 
with regional commitments.
Shocks will continue to affect South Sudan, 
including floods that devastate farmlands and 
volatile prices that place food beyond the reach 
of many households. Yet the country’s long-
term food security and economic resilience 
will depend on its ability to mobilize the 
investments needed to unlock its considerable 
agricultural potential.

to discuss problems in the camp, propose 
solutions and plan community activities, such 
as annual events in commemoration of the 
16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based

For Theresa, who has lived in the camp since

reliable source of affordable food, particularly

flooded and access to markets is more difficult. 
In her words, it provides a “beacon of hope for

“The opening of this restaurant 
has been my proudest moment. 
We get around 50 customers per 
day and many more during the

continues to work there to this

Theresa first fled to the Bentiu IDP Camp with 
her five children. Today, she shares her tented 
home with three of them, its walls lined with 
brightly patterned fabric, topped by a straw 
roof. Like many communities across South 
Sudan, Theresa and other residents of the 
Bentiu IDP camp are feeling the effects of 
declining humanitarian funding, even as needs 
remain high.
When asked about conditions in the camp, 
she shared: “A lot has changed and not for the 
better. We are receiving less assistance. Water 
and sanitation services have severely declined 
compared to two years ago and more people 
are resorting to open defecation because of 
poor conditions in the latrines. ”
Given the deteriorating sanitation conditions, 
Theresa is particularly concerned about 
the ongoing cholera outbreak. Earlier this 
May, suspected cholera cases in Unity State 
surpassed the alert threshold with most cases 
reported in the IDP camp. According to the 
World Health Organization (WHO), the cholera 
outbreak, which was formally declared in 
October 2024, has reached 103,956 cases and 
1,673 deaths across South Sudan, as of 22 May.

Upper Nile: Where coordination meets 
community resilience
INTERVIEW
The Upper Nile state, particularly Malakal, 
has been one of the most affected areas in 
South Sudan by conflict, displacement and 
recurrent climatic shocks. At the same time, 
durable solutions efforts are increasingly 
supporting voluntary return, local integration 
and reintegration. Today, Upper Nile hosts over 
225,000 internally displaced persons (IDPs) 
and more than 736,000 returnees, the highest 
number among all states. 
Based in Malakal, Dmytro Charskykh, Area-
Based Coordinator for Upper Nile, leads 
durable solutions coordination efforts with the 
Government and partners. In a short interview, 
he shares his experiences, highlighting 
achievements and challenges, but also 
opportunities, since the launch of the state-level 
roadmap on durable solutions in 2024.  
Could you tell us more about the Upper Nile 
Durable Solutions Roadmap and what is its 
significance?
Charskykh: The Roadmap, which runs from 2024 
to 2026, was designed to move the response 
beyond emergency assistance towards longer-
term recovery and resilience for IDPs, returnees 
and host communities. It brings together 
government, humanitarian, development, and 
peace actors around shared strategic priorities, 
including access to basic services, livelihoods, 
social cohesion, and strengthening local 
systems. Importantly, displacement-affected 
communities are also active partners in this 
process, shaping and guiding their solutions. 
Since its launch in 2024, we have seen stronger 
government leadership and coordination among 
partners, and more consistent engagement

years of displacement. Photo Credit: ABC
11
with local authorities and communities. We 
have also seen stronger linkages between 
humanitarian, peacebuilding, recovery and 
development programming.
What has been concretely achieved since the 
launch of the roadmap in 2024?
Charskykh: Through collective efforts under 
the Government, more than 3,400 formerly 
displaced people have returned or reintegrated 
across Upper Nile since 2024, including those 
who used to live in the former “Protection of 
Civilians” site in Malakal. Over 137,000 people 
were supported on Housing, Land and Property 
rights. Partners have rehabilitated water 
points, schools, and health facilities, expanded 
livelihood opportunities and community-based 
conflict resolution efforts. However, durable 
solutions are not only about infrastructure and 
services - they are fundamentally about dignity, 
inclusion and peace. 
In 2026, the Government and the UN have 
committed to extend the Roadmap by an 
additional two years, through 2028. The next 
phase will expand coverage to all counties in 
Upper Nile and incorporate solutions strategies 
for refugees in Maban and Renk.
In your view, what have been the key challenges 
in implementing durable solutions in the 
Upper Nile? And what have been key areas of 
opportunities? 
Charskykh: The key challenges remain 
insecurity, which affects counties like Nasir, 
Ulang, Longochuk and Baliet, recurrent flooding, 
displacement cycles, weak infrastructure and 
limited funding. Seasonal flooding continues to 
disrupt access in parts of the state, affecting 
livelihoods and service delivery. Limited 
economic opportunities and pressure on basic 
services make sustainable return more difficult. 
Despite these challenges, communities have 
consistently shown strong willingness to 
recover and rebuild when conditions allow. 
In Upper Nile, integrated interventions 
combining livelihoods support, rehabilitation 
of community infrastructure, and support to 
local governance structures have contributed 
to greater stability in displacement-affected 
areas, specifically in Malakal, Fashoda and 
Manyo counties, which also host significant 
numbers of refugees and returnees from Sudan. 
Community-led approaches have also helped 
strengthen trust between displaced and host 
communities, reducing tensions and supporting 
peaceful coexistence in areas where resources 
remain under pressure.
The UN and its partners have faced 
unprecedented funding cuts since last year. 
Has that affected durable solutions efforts in 
the Upper Nile, and if so, in what ways?

Charskykh: Yes, the funding cuts are having a 
significant impact. Durable solutions require 
sustained and predictable investments, but 
many partners are being forced to scale 
back activities and focus primarily on life-
saving interventions. In Upper Nile, this has 
affected field presence, coordination capacity, 
assessments, and the continuity of recovery 
and resilience programs. Some planned 
activities related to livelihoods, infrastructure 
rehabilitation, and community engagement 
have been delayed or reduced which risks 
slowing progress towards greater self-
reliance and resilience. At the same time, the 
funding environment has pushed partners to 
strengthen prioritization, improve coordination, 
and work more closely with local actors to 
maximize impact with limited resources. It has 
also reinforced the importance of targeting 
investments where they can deliver the greatest 
long-term benefits for communities.
Looking ahead, what would you say are the 
priorities for the next two years and what is the 
case for investment?
Charskykh: As the current roadmap concludes, 
the priority should be to capitalize on existing 
progress and transition toward a more 
sustainable, locally owned approach. The next 
phase should prioritize strengthening local 
institutions, expanding access to services, 
supporting livelihoods and climate resilience, 
and deepening collaboration between 
humanitarian, development, and peace actors. 
There is also a need for more flexible and 
multi-year funding that allows partners to 
move beyond life-saving activities, support 
long-term recovery and reduce aid dependency. 
Ultimately, investing in durable solutions is an 
investment in social cohesion, local stability and 
the resilience of communities. The experience 
in Upper Nile has shown that even modest but 
sustained investments can deliver tangible 
results when they are community-driven, well-
coordinated and aligned with local priorities.
On a more personal note: what does a typical 
day as an Area-Based Coordinator look like, and 
what motivates you about this role?
Charskykh: In my dual role as Area-Based 
Coordinator and UNHCR Head of Office in 
Malakal, my work involves a daily balance 
between coordination, field engagement 
and operational delivery. I work closely with 
humanitarian, development, peacebuilding 
partners, local authorities and community 
representatives to ensure that interventions are 
aligned with local priorities and that operational 
challenges are addressed collectively. In a 
context like Upper Nile, flexibility and continuous 
engagement with communities and authorities 
are essential. What motivates me most is seeing 
communities take active steps toward recovery 
despite very difficult conditions. It is rewarding 
to support processes that help people move 
toward greater stability, dignity and self-reliance.
