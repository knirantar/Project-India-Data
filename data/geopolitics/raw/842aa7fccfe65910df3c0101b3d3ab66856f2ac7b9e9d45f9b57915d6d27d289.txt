Executive Summary
The 12th iteration of the Humanitarian Networks 
and Partnerships Weeks (HNPW), co-hosted by 
OCHA and Switzerland, returned in 2026 with a re-
newed, shorter format and a strengthened strategic 
focus, with this year’s chosen theme centred on 
shaping the humanitarian reset and moving from 
global commitments to local action. The newly in-
troduced Community Day on Wednesday, 11 March 
2026 served as the anchor of the event, bringing to-
gether networks and partnerships to deepen collec-
tive reflection initiated at last year’s HNPW on how 
the humanitarian system must evolve amid shifting 
geopolitics and declining global funding, and to 
identify practical steps to drive the reset forward.
Marking one year since the launch of the humani-
tarian reset, OCHA convened a briefing on the  
humanitarian reset prior to HNPW to update 
networks and partners on progress against com-
mitments. During the Community Day, Under-Secre-
tary-General for Humanitarian Affairs and Emergen-
cy Relief Coordinator, Tom Fletcher led a keynote 
dialogue with humanitarian leaders from Yemen, 
the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Afghanistan, 
and South Sudan. The discussion offered an honest 
assessment of achievements to date, the barriers 
that hinder progress and the commitments needed 
to accelerate change.
Participants acknowledged early improvements, 
such as increased allocations of country-based 
pooled funding to local and national NGOs, stronger 
representation in country teams and examples of 
co-leadership of clusters. However, they stressed 
that key parts of the system are still falling short 
in delivering on the humanitarian reset’s localiza-
tion commitments. They called for genuine power 
sharing and more substantial local representation 
in global and regional-level decision-making bodies.
Community Day sessions, focused on Transition of 
Power, Resilience and Sustainability, Streamlined 
Coordination and Efficiency and Innovative Financ-
ing, reaffirmed several core commitments essential 
to advancing the humanitarian reset and shifting 
from global commitments to local action. These 
included: re-directing resources and authority to 
national responders without unnecessary interme-
diaries, investing in long-term system strengthening 
of local institutions rather than short-term parallel 
mechanisms; improving operational agility by 
streamlining coordination and reducing bureaucrat-
ic barriers as well as diversifying funding flows.
Together, these discussions underscored a shared 
determination to ensure the humanitarian reset 
delivers tangible improvements for people living 
through crisis. There was a clear call for humani-
tarian actors to maintain momentum and concrete 
action over the coming year to translate commit-
ments into measurable impact on the ground.

Introduction
The Humanitarian Networks and Partnerships 
Weeks (HNPW) 2026, co-hosted by OCHA and Swit-
zerland, once again proved to be a key forum for 
humanitarian practitioners to convene, exchange 
perspectives, learn from one another, collaborate, 
innovate, and shape meaningful commitments and 
forward-looking solutions. 
With close to 300 sessions, 55 exhibition stands 
organized by 78 networks, and 7,800 participants, 
including 2,000 who joined in person in Geneva, 
the strong participation underscores not only the 
relevance of HNPW, but also its growing influence 
as a global platform shaped by practitioners, for 
practitioners.
This year, the event adopted a renewed, shorter 
format and a strengthened strategic focus, guided 
by the theme: Shaping the Humanitarian Reset - 
Moving from Global Commitments to Local Action. 
Particular emphasis was placed on capturing and 
synthesizing key takeaways and recommendations 
from the keynote session, as well as from sessions 
on selected core topics of the humanitarian reset, 
namely Transition of Power, Resilience and Sus-
tainability, Streamlined Coordination and Efficiency, 
and Innovative Financing, to generate clear insights 
and practical guidance for the next phase of the 
humanitarian reset.
In addition, for the first time, the outputs and 
outcomes from sessions held during HNPW 2026 
were systematically compiled to support follow up 
on commitments made by networks and partners 
and to assess progress ahead of next year’s HNPW 
(see Annex 1).

Keynote Session: Shaping the 
Humanitarian Reset - From 
Global Commitments to Local

One Year into the Humanitarian Reset
The keynote session examined progress made one 
year into the humanitarian reset. Dominik Stillhart, 
Head of Swiss Humanitarian Aid, reiterated that the 
system must evolve toward a model in which local 
and national humanitarian actors lead by default, 
with international actors stepping in only where 
necessary - not the other way around. 
Emergency Relief Coordinator Tom Fletcher reflect-
ed on the reset’s first year and emphasized that the 
reset is fundamentally about defending core values 
and principles, refocusing on lifesaving priori-
ties, shifting power, money, and decision-making 
authority to local actors, while simplifying and 
reforming existing processes. 
While acknowledging some encouraging progress, 
he stressed that change is still moving far too 
slowly. He pointed to three core barriers imped-
ing advancement:
Power: Local actors, such as local NGOs, civil 
society, or national governing bodies doing human-
itarian work, still lack meaningful representation 
in critical decision-making spaces, with decisions 
often taken first and consultations added after-
wards, rather than placing local actors at the center 
of the process.
Money: Funding continues to be diluted through 
multiple layers of intermediaries, leaving only a frac-
tion of resources in the hands of local and national 
organizations delivering assistance on the ground. 
Process: Processes remain overly top down, with 
international actors assessing local partners rather 
than being held accountable by them.

Tom Fletcher called for a profound mindset shift 
and urged international actors to stand back where 
local colleagues are better placed to lead.
“We’ve really got to understand 
this point about creating space 
for others to take the lead. That 
doesn’t just mean telling people 
that we’re giving them power - it 
means getting out of the way.”  
- Tom Fletcher
He underscored that mindset, not technical hurdles, 
remains the biggest obstacle to achieving a truly 
locally-led system.
What Local Humanitarian Leaders 
Are Telling Us
Tom Fletcher then engaged in a candid dialogue 
with humanitarian leaders to explore what the reset 
looks like in practice. He spoke with Anna Tazita 
Samuel, Executive Director of Women for Change 
from South Sudan, Faiza Al Tamimi, Chairwoman of 
Nahda Makers Organization from Yemen, Hashim 
Amiri, Executive Director of Afghanistan Institute of 
Civil Society from Afghanistan, and Jonas Habima-
na, Executive Director at Biferd NGO from Demo-
cratic Republic of Congo. Their interventions were 
grounded, direct, and uncompromising.
“We should no longer talk about 
engaging local actors. It should 
be a humanitarian system where 
local and international actors are 
equal family members, not one 
owning the system and engaging 
the others.”  
- Faiza Al Tamimi
They noted some visible signs of progress emerg-
ing in their respective country contexts: increased 
representation in humanitarian country teams; a 
growing share of pooled funds reaching local ac-
tors; instances of national NGOs co-leading clusters 
and technical working groups, albeit often without 
the financial resources to sustain these roles; great-
er involvement of local and national organizations 
in promoting humanitarian standards; and improved 
access to global platforms.
“Handing over coordination 
responsibilities without resources 
is not localization.”  
- Jonas Habimana
However, they also underscored persistent struc-
tural barriers: fragmented and burdensome com-
pliance systems designed for large international 
organizations; funding channeled through multiple 
intermediaries that dilute resources and slow 
delivery; intermediaries often dictating terms of en-
gagement and project design; international organi-
zations handing over responsibilities under rushed 
transitions when funding runs out; limited long-term 
and flexible funding; risks being pushed downward 
to local organizations; and continued exclusion 
from key decision-making spaces where national 
and global strategies are shaped. 
Across their contributions, local leaders converged 
on a shared call for genuine power sharing, simpli-
fied processes, and sustained investment in local 
systems and existing capacities, rather than short-
term and fragmented support.
“Localization should be measured 
not by participation, but by real 
shifts in resources, leadership, 
and decision-making authority.” 
- Hashim Amiri

Tom Fletcher closed by acknowledging these chal-
lenges and committing to measurable action over 
the year until the next HNPW, signaling a shift from 
rhetoric to accountability. The session reinforced 
that the humanitarian reset will only succeed if 
local leadership is not merely recognized, but struc-
turally embedded across the humanitarian system.
“Let us finally act on what we 
discuss around tables and let us 
walk the talk.” 
- Anna Tazita Samuel

Local humanitarian leaders called for:
• Concrete action, not more policy commitments
• Shift from participation to true co ownership of the system
• Real representation and leadership in global, regional and country-level decision making
• Inclusion in needs assessment and project design, not just implementation
• Direct donor funding with fair and transparent cost-recovery provisions
• Shared risk frameworks
• Simplified, unified compliance requirements
• Mutual accountability that apply equally across the system

Core Topic Sessions
Transition of Power
Sessions under this topic explored what it takes to 
make humanitarian assistance genuinely locally 
led, including the shifts in power, resources, and 
decision-making needed to empower local actors. 
Key take-aways include:
Local leadership as the default. Empower local and 
national actors to set priorities, design programs, 
and lead coordination efforts in a way that avoids 
zero sum dynamics. Responses should be locally 
led and internationally supported.
From participation to real influence and deci-
sion-making authority. Local actors must move 
beyond being merely consulted to directly shaping 
programme designs, budgets and overall strate-
gic direction.
Funding flows re-directed and fair partnership 
principles applied. Increase direct financing, in-
cluding overheads, to local and national NGOs and 
remove layers of intermediaries. Ensure this shift 
does not create new power imbalances, particularly 
between large national NGOs and smaller organiza-
tions that may be better placed for specific roles.
Long term organizational strength. Include over-
head costs or specific budget lines for organization-
al development to allow local and national actors 
to build and strengthen systems and processes, 
invest in and retain staff, and plan beyond short 
project cycles.
Risk shared. Share responsibility rather than push-
ing risk downward. Donors should also avoid creat-
ing additional risks through short project cycles and 
rigid funding requirements.
Harmonized and simplified compliance systems. 
Donors must align requirements and ensure 
proportionate due diligence standards adapted to 
local realities. 
Trust through transparency. Commit to open com-
munication and shared data, and clearly disclose 
how much funding goes to local actors and under 
what terms—including overheads and cost recov-
ery—to reduce power imbalances that often come 
from uncertainty.
Upward flowing feedback channels. Enable local 
organizations to evaluate international partners, not 
just the other way around.

Resilience and Sustainability
Sessions under this topic explored how humanitari-
an action can better support long term recovery and 
self-sufficiency , moving beyond short-term  crisis 
response and accelerate the transition to locally-led 
systems, particularly in contexts where strong local 
capacity already exists. Key take-aways include:
Investment in systems, not just short-term pro-
jects. Strengthen local institutions, informal finance 
systems and markets and public services rather 
than creating short-term parallel mechanisms, so 
they can remain functional long after external 
actors leave.
Government leadership is fundamental for sustain-
ability. Support governments to build policy frame-
works that clarify mandates, align actors, and shift 
from reactive response to anticipatory action and 
long term solutions.
Strengthened community ownership. Listen to 
communities to understand real needs, set priori-
ties, monitor progress, and ensure accountability, 
they know their realities best.
Integrated humanitarian, development, and peace-
building approaches. Break down silos so recov-
ery efforts address root causes, not just imme-
diate symptoms.
Existing local capacity recognized and prioritized. 
Step back and support what already works instead 
of creating parallel structures.
Capability before immediate needs. Recognize 
and build on people’s skills and strengths from the 
start to avoid short-term fixes and enable long-term 
self sufficiency.

Streamlined Coordination and Efficiency
Sessions under this topic examined what it takes to 
redesign global and national coordination systems 
to be more agile, adaptive, and responsive during 
crises. They also looked at how to strengthen 
accountability and transparency in decision-mak-
ing, ensuring clear and meaningful communication 
channels with local actors. Key take-aways include:
Processes simplified and streamlined. Rethink and 
simplify partnership, funding, and operational pro-
cedures so they match the realities of small, under 
resourced local actors.
Duplication eliminated and complementarity 
strengthened. Bring the full range of actors around 
the table, including governments, humanitarian or-
ganizations, development partners, civil society and 
financial institutions to ensure coordinated action.
Reporting processes harmonized. Align reporting 
requirements and reduce repetitive processes to 
free up time and resources for actual response.
Agility built into system architecture. Replace 
overly procedural and slow systems with flexible, 
adaptive structures that can quickly respond to 
urgent and changing crisis realities.
Reduce bureaucracy. Cut unnecessary steps, 
approvals, and paperwork that delay action and 
drain capacity.
Clarify and open communication channels. Estab-
lish clear, two way communication with affected 
communities so they understand decisions and can 
influence them.
Local leadership in coordination with adequate 
resources. Ensure that funding includes the neces-
sary means for local organizations to take on and 
sustain cluster co-chair role.

Innovative Financing
Sessions under this topic focused on how to 
diversify funding streams and reduce reliance on a 
limited number of major donors, exploring ways to 
broaden partnerships, particularly with the private 
sector, and to expand the use of innovative financ-
ing mechanisms that can strengthen and sustain 
humanitarian action. Key take-aways include:
More resilient and flexible funding model. Shift 
from project restricted grants to diversified, multi 
year, core funding to enable long term planning and 
independence.
Private sector partnerships broadened. Build long 
term collaborations with businesses that offer capi-
tal, technology, expertise, and shared value opportu-
nities, not just C SR grants.
Whole of financing approaches are essential. Com-
bine humanitarian funding with national budget 
allocations and local financial institutions while 
maintaining strong local leadership.
Pooled funding increased. Expand shared financing 
mechanisms, including locally-led or area-based 
pooled funds, to enable rapid, flexible responses, 
support shared risk taking, and improve access 
to resources for local actors. Include dedicated 
funding for tailored organizational capacity 
strengthening.
Alternative financing mechanisms expanded. Use 
blended finance, social impact bonds, and digital 
crowdfunding to unlock new and more flexible 
financial resources.

What We Learned from the 
HNPW Survey
An online survey was conducted with the support of 
Sphere and Monday.com to gather insights on how 
organizations have adapted to the humanitarian 
reset since the last HNPW and where practitioners 
see the most urgent needs for reform. 
The survey gathered 194 results from a diverse 
group of participants representing over 67 coun-
tries. While the geographic representation is broad, 
respondents were mainly from Europe and notably 
from several African countries, with fewer respons-
es from the Americas and Asia. Respondents also 
varied in seniority levels, reflecting perspectives 
from both emerging and established humanitarian 
practitioners. 
Organizational 
Adaptations to the Reset
Respondents reported several adaptations to their 
usual ways of working since the last HNPW and 
following the launch of the humanitarian reset. The 
three most frequently mentioned elements were: 
• Increased partnership with local actors
• Organizational restructuring
• Internal learning and reflection
With over 75% of respondents indicating increased 
collaboration with local and national actors, there 
appears to be a clear and sustained commitment to 
advancing long-standing localization commitments. 
Beyond establishing new partnerships, respondents 
also reported concrete actions such as allocating 
quota-based representation for local and national 
actors in key country level decision making fora,

engaging affected communities throughout the en-
tire programme cycle to leverage local knowledge 
and foster ownership, working through existing 
local governance structures and community com-
mittees rather than establishing parallel structures, 
and establishing platforms for capacity building, 
knowledge transfer and resource mobilization to 
support locally-led action. 
Perceived Barriers to 
Local Leadership
Respondents were also asked to identify the main 
barriers to local leadership in humanitarian action. 
Three main obstacles that stood out were: 
• Limited funding  
• Donor risk aversion and compliance 
requirements 
• Exclusion from coordination mechanisms
Despite widespread organizational commitments to 
locally led action, over 95% of respondents iden-
tified limited funding opportunities as a primary 
constraint to local leadership, while 70% pointed to 
restrictive donor requirements that favour tradition-
al top-down partnership models, often channeled 
through intermediaries. Coordination mechanisms 
also remain largely dominated by international 
actors. These approaches limit the development 
of equitable, trust-based collaborations in which 
local and national actors can move beyond the role 
of mere implementers and assume leadership and 
decision-making responsibilities. These findings 
highlight a major gap between stated commitments 
and the enabling conditions and structures needed 
to translate them into action and call for systemic 
change in the way international actors, and donors 
in particular, share power and resources.
“If communities and local actors 
are the first to respond and the 
last to leave, they must also 
be the first to lead and the first 
to be funded.” 
- Survey respondent
Priority Areas for Change
When asked to identify key actions to improve 
humanitarian action in future, respondents over-
whelmingly prioritized two areas:
• Increase flexible and multi-year funding
• Build local technical, managerial, and institu-
tional capacity 
This ranking reflects again a strong demand for 
structural and financial reforms that would reduce 
strict earmarking by donors to enable more rapid, 
efficient, and locally driven humanitarian responses, 
with almost 90% of respondents asking for greater 
access to flexible and multi-year funding.
A common thread across the findings is a clear 
willingness to commit to a shift towards locally led 
action by organizations that is hindered by the cur-
rent lack of funding and insufficient flexibility within 
prevailing funding modalities, which if remained 
unaddressed severely restrict the scope for  
meaningful change.

Conclusion and Way Forward 
This year’s HNPW demonstrated once again that 
the event is a key forum not only for humanitarian 
practitioners to exchange perspectives and collab-
orate, but also to challenge one another to shape 
impactful commitments. The key-take aways from 
the four core topics of the humanitarian reset: 
Transition of Power; Resilience and Sustainability; 
Streamlined Coordination and Efficiency; and Inno-
vative Financing — point to concrete and interlinked 
priorities for the coming year. Although the keynote 
session highlighted visible progress on the human-
itarian reset in the past year, it underscored that 
much work is to be done to shift from rhetoric to 
accountability and genuinely realize localization. 
Looking ahead to the next iteration of the HNPW, 
the challenge is to ensure that commitments made 
during the two weeks are translated into action. 
Tangible outputs and action points coming from 
the sessions have been recorded and will be used 
to track progress on its implementation in the year 
ahead. Accordingly, the next HNPW will be inviting 
humanitarian leaders and practitioners to use the 
event as a platform to hold each other into account.
