ESCALATION IN THE MIDDLE EAST AND BEYOND — MOBILITY REPORT
Data collection period: 09 - 15 June 2026 | Publication date: 18 June 2026
OVERVIEW
On 15 June 2026, the United States of America and the Islamic Republic of Iran reached an agreement 
on a Memorandum of Understanding concerning the cessation of hostilities. The escalation in the Middle 
East and beyond had triggered a humanitarian crisis affecting multiple regions in an already fragile context, 
with significant impact to population mobility. Since the announcement of the agreement, its impact on 
mobility dynamics across the region and beyond remains uncertain. The situation continues to be highly 
fragile, requiring sustained monitoring and operational preparedness.
In the Islamic Republic of Iran, some families have begun returning to their homes amid signs of de-escalation. 
However, thousands continue to shelter in hotels due to damage to housing and civilian infrastructure. As 
of 01 June, approximately 9,000 individuals remain displaced, unable to return to their homes due to 
the extent of the damage. Overall, population movements remain localised, temporary, and economically 
driven, with no signs of mass displacement.
In Lebanon, the humanitarian situation remains fragile, with needs both immense and severe despite the 
15 June announcement of a peace deal. Limited and cautious movements of displaced families towards 
areas of origin have been reported, but the majority of the nearly one million people displaced within 
Lebanon since 02 March remained in areas of reception due to continued security concerns and ongoing 
military activities in southern Lebanon.1 Authorities advised against returns to high-risk locations, and many 
displaced families are awaiting further assurances before making decisions regarding return. As of 11 June 
2026, 134,807 people remain displaced across 642 crowded collective shelters, with thousands more 
families sheltering in open spaces.2 Meanwhile, cross-border flows into the Syrian Arab Republic 
are continuing, with a total of 441,453 movements recorded as of 15 June 2026,3 placing additional 
pressure on an already overstretched humanitarian system.
IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) is on the ground, actively tracking displacement and cross-
border mobility while scaling operations in coordination with local authorities and partners to better 
understand how the crisis is impacting human mobility in the region.

IMPACT ON NEIGHBOURING COUNTRIES  
OF THE ISLAMIC REPUBLIC OF IRAN
The conflict is affecting mobility along shared borders between the Islamic Republic of Iran and its neighbouring countries, driving precautionary movements within and across borders. Since the start of the crisis, flows from the 
Islamic Republic of Iran to neighbouring countries have fluctuated over time, varying significantly between countries. Afghanistan receives the highest number of movements with substantial dips and spikes while Pakistan and 
Iraq see much smaller and more occasional movements. Overall, cross-border flows remain moderate and variable rather than steadily increasing.
AFGHANISTAN 
Between 01 March and 15 June 2026, a total of 467,248 individuals 
entered Afghanistan (4,367 daily average) from the Islamic Republic 
of Iran, while 187,746 individuals departed Afghanistan to the Islamic 
Republic of Iran over the same reporting period (1,755 daily average).5 
Headcount figures for Islam Qala BCP currently reflect only returnees 
and Iranian nationals. Data on individuals with valid passports was not 
collected this week due to temporary access restrictions and should be 
considered when interpreting the figures.
During the reporting period of 09 - 15 June 2026, cross-border 
movements at Milak, Islam Qala, and Abu Nasr Farahi BCPs remained 
stable, with all points operational. Economic pressures, limited legal 
pathways, and enforcement measures continued to shape movement 
patterns.
At Milak BCP, forced returns from the Islamic Republic of Iran increased 
and spontaneous returns continued due to limited jobs and low income. 
Outflows remained relatively high, mainly among truck drivers and 
passport holders, with restricted access to new visas.
At Islam Qala BCP, spontaneous returns decreased slightly, while forced 
returns increased, including cases linked to Iraq via the Islamic Republic 
of Iran. Returnees reported mistreatment and high transport costs amid 
intensified enforcement. Inflows of passport holders remained stable, 
while outflows increased slightly, mainly involving families. 
At Abu Nasr Farahi BCP, inflows and outflows remained steady with no 
disruptions. Limited taxi crossings continued, and one Iranian businessman 
exited Afghanistan. 
Flow Monitoring Survey (FMS) findings show Iranian inflows 
remained limited, short-term, and mainly work-related, while 
outflows were largely family-driven. Afghan inflows from the Islamic 
Republic of Iran were mainly due to forced returns, while outflows 
were economically driven and supported by valid documentation. 
PAKISTAN
Between 01 March and 15 June 2026, a total of 28,305 individuals 
crossed from the Islamic Republic of Iran into Pakistan, representing 
a daily average of 265 flows. Movements occurred primarily through 
the Taftan–Mirjaveh BCP (23,911 individuals) and the Gabd– Kumb– 
Rimdan BCP (4,394 individuals). 6 Movements in and out of Pakistan 
have slowed following the Eid holiday, high temperatures, and security 
incidents in the country  along the route of the Taftan BCP. 
Cross-border movement from the Islamic Republic of Iran to Pakistan 
through the Gabd and Taftan BCPs remains restricted. Currently, only 
students, drivers, pilgrims, and tourists with valid passports and visas are 
permitted entry. Many travellers are using Pakistan solely as a transit 
route due to the closure of Iranian airports amid the conflict, before 
continuing to onward destinations.  Those traveling irregularly to the 
Islamic Republic of Iran are detained by Iranian authorities and handed 
over to Pakistan’s Federal Investigation Agency (FIA) for processing.
Border authorities reported no influx of Iranian nationals other than 
those with valid passports and visas in Pakistan. The majority of Iranian 
arrivals subsequently returned to the Islamic Republic of Iran, primarily 
as their visits were trade related.
Outbound movement from Pakistan to the Islamic Republic of Iran 
continues on a limited basis, restricted to Pakistani students and drivers. 
Although many students initially returned to Pakistan at the onset of 
the conflict, some are now traveling back to the Islamic Republic of 
Iran to resume studies.
As of 10 June 2026, a total of 82 Third Country Nationals (TCNs) 
were recorded in Pakistan.  According to authorities, TCNs entering 
through the Taftan and Gabd– Kumb– Rimdan BCPs were mostly tourists 
or businessmen. Many had been stranded in the Islamic Republic of 
Iran and, due to the unavailability of flights, transited through Pakistan 
before returning to their countries of origin.

IRAQ
CROSS-BORDER MOVEMENT
As of 15 June 2026, border conditions remained broadly stable with 
all crossing points opened and no major new restrictions reported. All 
Border Crossing Points (BCPs) continue to register both incoming and 
outgoing movements. 
The Iraqi Border Point Commission reported that between 01 and 
30 April, a total of 271,751 individuals entered Iraq from the Islamic 
Republic of Iran while 270,142 departed, resulting in a net flow of 1,609 
individuals.7 During the month of May, there was a significant increase 
in both arrivals and departures, with arrivals rising to 535,428 (a 
97% increase compared to April) and departures reaching 517,160 
(a 91% increase). 
This sharp rise in cross-border movements is likely linked to a religious 
pilgrimage period coinciding with the Eid holiday (26–30 May), which 
typically drives temporary, high-volume travel flows between the Islamic 
Republic of Iran and Iraq. As a result, despite the substantial increase in 
overall movement, the net inflow remained relatively moderate at 18,268 
individuals, indicating that most movements were short-term in nature.
DTM FLOW MONITORING ACTIVITIES AT KEY BORDER 
CROSSING POINTS (BCPs) 
Between 02 and 15 June, data from the DTM Flow Monitoring 
Registry, which gathers information on individuals’ demographic 
profiles, reasons for movement and intentions, reported that Iranian 
nationals accounted for 67 per cent of arrivals. 
Among incoming movements of Iranian nationals, the primary purposes 
were family visits (37%), employment (31%), and tourism (15%), with 
no movements linked to conflict or insecurity reported. All incoming 
movements of Iraqi nationals were returns after long-term residence 
in the Islamic Republic of Iran (100%).
Outgoing movements were mainly driven by returns to the Islamic 
Republic of Iran following long-term stays (65%, over three months) 
and short-term stays (28%, less than three months). Outgoing Iraqi 
nationals primarily travelled for family visits (39%), tourism (29%), and 
shopping (13%). Iraqi returnees were predominantly destined for Al 
Sulaymaniyah (97%), with smaller proportions traveling to Erbil (2%) 
and a minimal share to Baghdad and Kirkuk

Between 28 February and 10 March 2026, a total of 4,930 individuals entered

Between 28 February and 29 April 2026, a total of 3,564 individuals crossed

REPUBLIC OF TÜRKİYE
As of 15 June, the situation along Türkiye’s border with the Islamic 
Republic of Iran remains calm, with no indications of irregular movements 
or increased pressure.
The latest available data on entries at the main Border Crossing Points 
(BCPs) indicate that arrivals in April averaged 4,200 per day. 8 These figures 
include both Iranian nationals and Third-Country Nationals (TCNs) and are 
consistent with regular cross-border mobility patterns.
TURKMENISTAN

citizens evacuated earlier through Turkmenistan.
According to the EU Delegation in Turkmenistan, additional evacuations of 
TCNs have taken place. These reportedly included one citizen each from 
Finland, Austria, Sweden, and France, as well as two citizens from the 
Netherlands.

INTERNAL DISPLACEMENT
With the Memorandum of Understanding agreement between the United States of America and the 
Islamic Republic of Iran on 15 June 2026, the number of families cautiously returning to their locations 
of origin continues to increase. However, the majority of the nearly one million people displaced within 
Lebanon since 02 March remained in areas of reception due to continued security concerns and ongoing 
hostilities in southern Lebanon.10 As of 11 June 2026, 134,807  people remain displaced across 642 
crowded collective shelters, with thousands more families sheltering in open spaces.11
Authorities continued to advise against returns to high-risk locations, and many displaced families are 
awaiting further assurances before making decisions regarding return.12 Many households returning to 
their home areas appear to be assessing housing conditions and basic service availability while maintaining 
existing displacement arrangements. As a result, humanitarian needs remain high, shelter capacity 
remains under pressure, and displacement levels are expected to remain elevated in the near term.

SYRIAN ARAB REPUBLIC
As of 15 June, a total of 441,453 movements had 
been recorded along the four points of entry (PoEs) 
at the Syrian-Lebanese border where DTM Syria tracks 
population movements into the Syrian Arab Republic. 
Syrian nationals constituted 97 per cent of all recorded 
movements, with Lebanese nationals accounting for the 
remaining 3 per cent. DTM also reported the transit 
of 2,606 Lebanese nationals through Syrian territory 
towards Jordan. 
In addition to cross-border movements into the Syrian 
Arab Republic, DTM Syria implemented Emergency 
Mobility Tracking (EMT) to capture arrivals into the 
country. Between 02 March and 08 June 2026, DTM 
Syria recorded 121,757 individuals arriving across 1,211 
locations in all 14 governorates in the Syrian Arab 
Republic. 
Between 09 and 15 June 2026, Key Informants (KIs) 
continued to report significant reintegration challenges 
among newly arrived households. Key informants (KIs) 
reported increasing intentions among returnees to either 
return to Lebanon or move within the Syrian Arab Republic 
due to a lack of sustainable livelihoods in areas of origin. 
Despite some improvements in basic services, economic 
hardship remains the main driver of instability, with scarce 
job opportunities, low wages, and widespread reliance on 
savings, remittances, or family support to meet basic needs. 
KIs also noted rising use of negative coping strategies. This 
pattern is most evident in Aleppo, Ar-Raqqa, Al-Hasakeh, 
and Idleb, where returnees are reconsidering their decision 
to stay due to unemployment and poor living conditions. 
These findings emphasize the need for income-generating 
support, cash assistance, and economic recovery measures 
to reduce the risk of further secondary displacement.
Most arrivals were men (31%), followed by women (25%), 
girls (23%), and boys (21%). This distribution likely reflects 
employment-related drivers, including both access to 
livelihoods and job losses. Among the arrivals currently 
employed (49% of locations), the majority relied on daily 
wage labour (77%), followed by salaried work (45%) and 
agriculture or livestock activities (42%). While some work is 
available, it is generally irregular, informal, and insufficient to 
provide stable income, leaving many returnees dependent 
on low-security, short-term employment. As a result, 
households continue to rely on coping strategies such 
as savings, remittances, borrowing, and support from 
relatives, though KIs report these mechanisms are 
becoming increasingly unsustainable as resources and 
social support networks are depleted.
More than half of KIs (55%) reported that returnee families 
were unable to cover essential expenditures through 
available income alone. A further 44 per cent indicated 
that earnings met only part of household requirements, 
while only 1 per cent considered income levels adequate 
to fully meet basic expenses.
Consequently, economic reintegration remains a major 
challenge for households returning from Lebanon, with 
KIs highlighting persistent barriers to financial stability. Local 
economic conditions are described as weak, with limited 
labour demand, rising living costs, and declining purchasing 
power restricting recovery from displacement-related losses.

METHODOLOGY
This analysis is based on information compiled weekly from IOM’s 
country offices. DTM monitors population mobility and displacement 
dynamics across countries in Afghanistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Iraq, 
Lebanon, Pakistan, the Republic of Türkiye, Turkmenistan, and the 
Syrian Arab Republic.
Data is drawn from primary DTM flow monitoring and mobility tracking 
activities, alongside secondary sources, such as government data, with all 
secondary information validated by our field teams. This includes data on 
cross-border movements, internal displacement, and qualitative insights 
on emerging mobility trends and humanitarian needs. While efforts 
are made to align reporting to the same reference week, collection 
periods may vary across countries.
