In The Gambia, tensions between farmers and herders in Central River Region South (CRR-S), Central River Region North (CRR-N), North Bank Region (NBR), and Lower River Region (LRR) have intensified 
due to climate change, environmental degradation, and rising socio-economic pressures, increasing competition over land and water resources.
Transhumance remains a key livelihood strategy but increasing resource scarcity and cross-border livestock movements between The Gambia and Senegal continue to heighten pressure on host communities.
Through its Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), IOM developed the Transhumance Tracking Tool (TTT) to monitor transhumance movements and related vulnerabilities in the four regions and to support 
peaceful transhumance practices.
Over the five-month period from November 2025 to March 2026, the TTT early warning system captured 151 alerts through trained enumerators, key informants, community representatives, and local 
authorities. These included movement-related alerts (66%) and sudden events (34%), helping stakeholders identify risks, inform timely responses, and support conflict prevention.

Over the five-month period from November 2025 to March 2026, movement alerts indicated that 58 per cent of reported movements were categorized as mass movements, 27 per cent as late movements, 
14 per cent as early movements, and 1 per cent as other types of movement, such as seasonal movements. Reported transhumance movements took place across regions in The Gambia and Senegal.
All key informants (100%) indicated that the reported movement alerts had the potential to result in agro-pastoral conflict. In addition, 75 per cent of respondents assessed the probability of risk 
materialization as likely, 21 per cent as very likely, and 4 per cent as unlikely.
Event alerts showed that 27 per cent related to agro-pastoral conflict, 29 per cent to farmer–herder conflicts, 27 per cent to disasters, and 10 per cent to inter-community tensions. The main reported causes 
of agro-pastoral conflict were grazing area encroachment (27%), farmland encroachment (17%), damage to surrounding fields by pastoral groups (13%), and the early or late movement of pastoral groups 
(6%), with other causes accounting for 10 per cent. The main actors involved in event alerts were national and/or local authorities (29%), local farmers and breeders (29%), pastoral groups in transhumance 
(23%), and others (12%).
Key actors involved in conflict management included community leaders (89%), village committees (72%), local and/or national authorities (61%), religious leaders (50%), farmers (44%), other actors (44%), 
herders (28%), and pastoral organizations (22%). In terms of outcomes, 35 per cent of reported events were resolved, 62 per cent remained unresolved, and 4 per cent were reported as unknown.

alerts. Depending on the nature of the alert, some measures were taken to prevent, mitigate, or resolve tensions linked to transhumance movements. Other reported incidents, not directly related to 
transhumance, also prompted local responses, including farmer–herder clashes and property damage. The examples highlighted below illustrate some of the actions taken to document and respond to

In The Gambia, community leaders and Village Communities played an active role in resolving conflicts reported through alerts. Pastoralist groups and farming communities, in coordination with local authorities 
and Religion leaders, also contributed to addressing reported disputes and managing anticipated livestock movements. Their involvement supported community-based solutions aimed at reducing tensions and 
promoting peaceful transhumance.
