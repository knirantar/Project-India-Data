 Larysa  Magdyuk  presented  the  findings  outlined  in  the  analytical  report  on  the  monitoring  of  
women’s
 
rights
 
for
 
2022–2025.
 
During
 
the
 
monitoring
 
process,
 
more
 
than
 
50
 
female
 
respondents
 
participated
 
in
 
surveys
 
and
 
focus
 
groups.
 
The
 
key
 
objective
 
of
 
the
 
monitoring
 
was
 
to
 
assess
 
how
 
Ukraine’s
 
obligations
 
under
 
the
 
CEDAW
 
Convention
 
are
 
being
 
fulfilled
 
during
 
the
 
war.
 
Nine
 
articles
 
of
 
the
 
Convention
 
were
 
selected
 
for
 
monitoring.
 
The
 
main
 
point
 
highlighted
 
in
 
the
 
report
 
is
 
that
 
women’s
 
rights
 
cannot
 
be
 
viewed
 
in
 
isolation
 
from
 
the
 
broader
 
context
 
of
 
the
 
challenges
 
associated
 
with
 
the
 
war
 
in
 
Ukraine.
  The  first  set  of  challenges  identified  by  the  monitoring  concerns  the  care  crisis.  Women  
continue
 
to
 
bear
 
the
 
primary
 
burden
 
of
 
caring
 
for
 
their
 
families,
 
children,
 
elderly
 
relatives,
 
veterans,
 
and
 
the
 
wounded.
 
As
 
a
 
result,
 
many
 
of
 
them
 
are
 
excluded
 
from
 
the
 
labor
 
market.
 
The
 
second
 
set
 
of
 
challenges
 
is
 
unequal
 
access
 
to
 
services.
 
In
 
many
 
regions
 
of
 
Ukraine,
access  to  services  -  especially  medical  services  -  and  infrastructure  is  limited.  ID  women  
often
 
remain
 
isolated
 
in
 
the
 
communities
 
to
 
which
 
they
 
have
 
moved,
 
despite
 
the
 
existence
 
of
 
support
 
programs.
 
The
 
third
 
set
 
of
 
challenges
 
is
 
economic
 
isolation
 
or
 
even
 
exclusion.
 
These
 
are
 
circumstances
 
that
 
force
 
women
 
to
 
take
 
lower-paying
 
jobs
 
that
 
may
 
not
 
even
 
be
 
related
 
to
 
their
 
education
 
or
 
previous
 
experience.
 
The
 
fourth
 
set
 
of
 
challenges
 
is
 
invisible
 
vulnerability.
 
These
 
are
 
cases
 
of
 
domestic
 
and
 
GBV
 
that
 
often
 
remain
 
unseen.
 
  The  first  area  addressed  in  the  analytical  report  is  the  realization  of  women’s  rights  in  
political
 
life.
 
The
 
report
 
notes
 
the
 
purely
 
formal
 
nature
 
of
 
gender
 
quotas,
 
the
 
inability
 
of
 
women
 
to
 
participate
 
in
 
the
 
country’s
 
political
 
life
 
due
 
to
 
their
 
experiences
 
of
 
displacement,
 
the
 
insufficient
 
participation
 
of
 
female
 
military
 
personnel,
 
and
 
barriers
 
related
 
to
 
discrimination.
 
The
 
next
 
area
 
is
 
civic
 
activism,
 
where
 
women
 
were
 
the
 
most
 
active
 
volunteers
 
at
 
the
 
start
 
of
 
the
 
full-scale
 
war.
 
However,
 
as
 
government
 
and
 
tax
 
controls
 
increased,
 
the
 
level
 
of
 
volunteering
 
declined.
 
Legislation
 
on
 
volunteering
 
is
 
not
 
geared
 
toward
 
supporting
 
women’s
 
volunteering.
 
The
 
instability
 
of
 
funding
 
for
 
women’s
 
organizations
 
and
 
the
 
overburdening
 
of
 
women
 
also
 
pose
 
challenges.
 
A
 
new
 
issue
 
that
 
has
 
emerged
 
is
 
physical
 
and
 
emotional
 
exhaustion.
 
In
 
the
 
field
 
of
 
education,
 
the
 
main
 
challenge
 
is
 
online
 
education
 
and
 
learning
 
losses.
 
In
 
the
 
health
 
sector,
 
the
 
biggest
 
challenge
 
is
 
the
 
lack
 
of
 
information
 
for
 
women
 
about
 
their
 
medical
 
rights
 
and
 
options,
 
as
 
well
 
as
 
the
 
lack
 
of
 
systematic
 
attention
 
to
 
women’s
 
mental
 
health
 
issues.
 
In
 
the
 
socio-economic
 
sphere,
 
the
 
problem
 
is
 
a
 
lack
 
of
 
jobs
 
and
 
resulting
 
long-term
 
unemployment
 
among
 
women,
 
particularly
 
for
 
IDPs,
 
as
 
well
 
as
 
insufficient
 
opportunities
 
for
 
retraining
 
and
 
flexible
 
employment.
  Another  section  of  the  report  addresses  temporary  special  measures  and  protection  during  
maternity.
 
Damage
 
to
 
medical
 
infrastructure
 
is
 
affecting
 
women’s
 
access
 
to
 
health
 
care
 
services.
 
A
 
new
 
and
 
significant
 
issue
 
is
 
the
 
lack
 
of
 
legal
 
options
 
for
 
civil
 
partnerships.
   The  issue  of  human  trafficking  receives  special  attention  in  the  monitoring.  Due  to  the  war,  
migration,
 
and
 
economic
 
vulnerability,
 
the
 
risk
 
of
 
human
 
trafficking
 
for
 
women
 
and
 
children
 
is
 
increasing.
  Regarding  issues  related  to  gender-based  violence  against  women,  there  are  gaps  in  
legislation,
 
and
 
insufficient
 
coordination
 
among
 
all
 
responders
 
and
 
available
 
specialists.
 
There
 
is
 
stigma
 
and
 
mistrust
 
of
 
institutions
 
on
 
the
 
part
 
of
 
victims.
 
Society
 
exhibits
 
tolerance
 
toward
 
GBV
 
and
 
discrimination.
 
The
 
number
 
of
 
cyberviolence
 
cases
 
is
 
rising,
 
and
 
there
 
is
 
a
 
lack
 
of
 
response
 
mechanisms.
 
  2.  Key  Takeaways  from  the  Women  Deliver  Global  Conference  in  Naarm  (Melbourne),  
Milena
 
Goryachkovska
  Milena  Goryachkovska  shared  her  experience  of  attending  the  Women  Deliver  conference.    The  conference  brings  together  up  to  12,000  participants  from  around  the  world  and  has  a  
less
 
formal
 
atmosphere.
 
It
 
tends
 
to
 
address
 
the
 
pressing
 
issues
 
highlighted
 
by
 
the
 
women’s
 
movement.
 
This
 
year,
 
there
 
were
 
6,000
 
participants,
 
3%
 
of
 
whom
 
were
 
from
 
Europe.
 
There
 
were
 
three
 
representatives
 
from
 
Ukraine.
 
A
 
major
 
focus
 
of
 
this
 
year’s
 
conference
 
was
 
on
 
the
rights  of  indigenous  peoples,  decolonization,  and  climate  justice  with  an  intersectional  
approach.
 
  Feminist  approaches  to  the  use  of  artificial  intelligence  were  also  discussed  at  the  conference.   Somewhat  less  attention  was  paid  to  the  topics  of  women’s  economic  empowerment,  
combating
 
gender-based
 
violence,
 
and
 
the
 
rights
 
of
 
women
 
with
 
disabilities.
  There  was  almost  no  coverage  of  Agenda  1325  Women,  Peace,  and  Security  or  the  UN  
Security
 
Council,
 
which
 
is
 
very
 
different
 
from
 
the
 
discussions
 
taking
 
place
 
in
 
Ukraine.
 
According
 
to
 
Milena’s
 
observations,
 
the
 
international
 
community
 
is
 
aware
 
of
 
events
 
in
 
Ukraine,
 
but
 
they
 
remain
 
on
 
the
 
periphery
 
of
 
the
 
discourse.
 
Much
 
more
 
attention
 
was
 
paid
 
to
 
events
 
in
 
Gaza.
 
In
 
addition,
 
other
 
wars
 
were
 
also
 
discussed.
  At  this  conference,  the  so-called  Melbourne  Declaration  on  Gender  Equality  was  approved.  It  
is
 
a
 
visionary
 
document
 
preceded
 
by
 
lengthy
 
interregional
 
consultations.

  Vitaliy  Matveev  shared  insights  into  the  work  of  the  NGO  PROJECTOR  in  documenting  and  
investigating
 
war
 
crimes
 
against
 
LGBTQI+
 
individuals.
 
The
 
organization
 
has
 
recorded
 
over
 
250
 
cases
 
in
 
which
 
members
 
of
 
the
 
LGBTQI+
 
community
 
have
 
served
 
as
 
witnesses,
 
eyewitnesses,
 
or
 
victims
 
since
 
2023.
 
The
 
community
 
faces
 
war
 
crimes
 
directly
 
linked
 
to
 
SOGI
 
or
 
people’s
 
physical
 
appearance
 
or
 
representation
 
in
 
society.
 
Russian
 
military
 
personnel
 
single
 
out
 
such
 
individuals
 
and
 
subject
 
them
 
to
 
additional
 
criminal
 
acts.
 
  The  community  rarely  turns  to  the  Ukrainian  law  enforcement  system  because  there  were  
already
 
issues
 
with
 
the
 
system’s
 
response
 
to
 
reports
 
even
 
before
 
the
 
full-scale
 
invasion.
 
Since
 
reports
 
from
 
the
 
LGBTQI+
 
community
 
were
 
not
 
singled
 
out
 
in
 
any
 
way,
 
it
 
is
 
impossible
 
to
 
collect
 
statistical
 
data
 
on
 
their
 
number
 
or
 
the
 
number
 
of
 
victims.
 
The
 
number
 
of
 
cases
 
of
 
war
 
crimes
 
has
 
increased
 
significantly.
 
To
 
date,
 
there
 
are
 
only
 
9
 
officially
 
recognized
 
cases.
 
These
are  9  people  who  agreed  to  speak  publicly  about  having  been  victims  of  war  crimes  
committed
 
by
 
Russian
 
military
 
personnel.
 
  The  NGO  PROJECTOR  has  developed  a  guide  containing  recommendations  on  the  specific  
aspects
 
of
 
investigating
 
crimes
 
against
 
LGBTQI+
 
individuals
 
in
 
situations
 
of
 
armed
 
conflict.
