Summary
Sudan is facing one of the world’s largest humanitarian and displacement crises as the conflict between the Sudanese Armed Forces
(SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which began in April 2023, enters its fourth year in 2026. The conflict has triggered
widespread civilian suffering, economic collapse, mass displacement, and severe regional instability, with humanitarian conditions
continuing to deteriorate across much of the country.
An estimated 19.5 million people are currently experiencing acute food insecurity, making Sudan the world’s largest hunger crisis.
Famine conditions have been confirmed in el-Fasher (North Darfur) and Kadugli (South Kordofan), while multiple areas across Darfur
and Kordofan remain at high risk of famine amid ongoing conflict, siege tactics, market disruption, and severe restrictions on
humanitarian access. Children continue to bear a disproportionate burden of the crisis, with millions exposed to acute malnutrition,
disease outbreaks, and heightened mortality risks.
Fatality estimates remain highly contested due to limited access, communication blackouts, and verification constraints. The Armed
Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) recorded nearly 30,000 reported deaths by late 2024, while independent analyses and
international media estimates suggest the true death toll may exceed 150,000 people. Civilians continue to face widespread violence,
including indiscriminate shelling, aerial bombardments, drone attacks, and ethnically targeted violence, particularly in Darfur and
parts of Kordofan.
Sudan’s health system remains severely degraded, especially in active conflict zones where many facilities are non-operational or
functioning only partially. Although the nationwide cholera outbreak that spread across all 18 states between 2024 and early 2026 was
declared contained in March 2026, underlying conditions—including mass displacement, damaged health infrastructure, and poor
sanitation—continue to create a high risk of renewed outbreaks and other communicable diseases.
Displacement continues at an unprecedented scale. More than 14 million people have been displaced since the start of the conflict,
including approximately 9 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and over 4 million refugees in neighboring countries, making
Sudan the world’s largest displacement crisis. At the same time, limited and fragile returns to parts of Khartoum and Al Jazirah have
been reported since early 2026 following shifting frontlines, though returnees face widespread destruction, insecurity, and lack of basic
services and livelihoods.
Militarily, the conflict has continued to fragment since late 2025, with intensified fighting in Darfur and Kordofan driving further civilian
displacement and humanitarian access constraints. The RSF maintains significant influence across much of Darfur and continues
offensive operations in Kordofan, while the SAF retains control over key eastern and northern urban centers. The growing use of drones,
aerial strikes, and long-range attacks has increasingly impacted civilian infrastructure, including hospitals, schools, markets, and
displacement sites, further compounding humanitarian needs.
Ethnic violence and sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) continue to escalate, particularly in Darfur. Human rights organizations
and monitoring networks have documented widespread abuses, including conflict-related sexual violence, forced displacement,
arbitrary detention, and targeted attacks on ethnic communities. Despite sustained international pressure, including sanctions
imposed by the United States and United Kingdom on RSF leaders and affiliated networks, diplomatic and mediation efforts have yet to
achieve a durable ceasefire or political settlement.
The conflict is also generating increasing regional spillover, with large refugee flows, cross-border insecurity, and growing pressure on
already overstretched humanitarian systems in neighboring countries. Humanitarian operations inside Sudan remain critically
underfunded, with the World Food Programme warning in 2026 that severe funding gaps are threatening emergency food pipelines,
ration levels, and the continuity of lifesaving assistance.
As of mid-2026, Sudan remains one of the world’s most severe and complex humanitarian emergencies, characterized by prolonged
armed conflict, fragmented territorial control, mass displacement, and shrinking humanitarian space. Without urgent improvements in
humanitarian access, sustained financing, and renewed diplomatic engagement, the crisis is likely to deepen further, with serious
implications for civilian populations and broader regional stability.

Operational Environment
Economy
Current economic situation
Sudan's macroeconomy has collapsed into a severe crisis, with the Sudanese pound trading at record lows on the parallel market
(around 4,400 SDG per USD) amid wartime economic collapse and rampant black-market foreign exchange activity [10][5].
Authorities have pursued emergency measures — including Central Bank and government efforts to boost gold production and
exports and emergency fiscal measures — to stabilise the pound, even as core financial services, broker networks and remittance
flows have collapsed and trade and transport disruptions persist [5][6][10]. The government reports a 73% poverty rate (including
diaspora) and has announced a 2026–2030 five-year livelihoods plan alongside claims of partial service restoration (e.g., electricity
restored to up to 80% of Khartoum), while acknowledging ministries are operating far below capacity [9]. FEWS NET and related
humanitarian analyses warn that Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes are expanding across Greater Darfur, Greater Kordofan and Blue
Nile, and that two in five people in Sudan face crisis or worse levels of food insecurity, with nearly 200,000 in catastrophic (IPC Phase 5)
conditions in some areas [6][41][11]. Market assessments show markets remain functioning in many localities, but affordability —
driven by loss of purchasing power — is the binding constraint, with cash-based assistance recommended where markets operate
and continued market fragility noted across monitored sites [42][6].
Impact of the conflict on the economy
The conflict has severely distorted trade routes, market cycles and economic output. Competing territorial control — SAF holding Port
Sudan and central export infrastructure while RSF controls much of Darfur and Kordofan's gold and livestock areas — has fragmented
export chains, incentivised smuggling (notably gold flows to the UAE) and shrunk legitimate foreign-exchange earnings [27][14][31].
Gold revenues and illicit mineral flows have materially financed the war economy: the RSF produced an estimated 10 tonnes of gold in
2024 (approximately US$860 million) while gold prices rose around 150% since 2024, briefly breaking US$5,000/oz in March 2026 [14][7].
Food and fuel inflation spiked sharply in April 2026: sorghum rose +23% month-on-month and over 170% above the five-year average,
wheat +17.5% month-on-month and approximately 187% above average, millet +14% and approximately 134% above average, while
diesel rose 55% month-on-month on the official market and fuel costs increased 83–144% year-on-year — raising transport costs and
compressing household purchasing power [6][11]. April 2026 market monitoring found over half of localities had generally functional
markets, but almost two-thirds scored below average in affordability and resilience, with Dilling, As-Sunut, Nyala Shimal and Kereneik
performing poorly across availability, access and infrastructure indicators [6].
Targeted strikes and infrastructure damage have severed key trade and aid corridors — most notably the Ardamata bridge in El
Geneina (damaged in early June 2026) and bridges on the Kadugli–Dilling road — reducing commercial traffic and constraining
humanitarian access ahead of the rainy season [5][8][1]. Regional shocks linked to Strait of Hormuz disruptions have fractured
humanitarian and commercial logistics, raising freight and insurance costs, delaying critical consignments to Sudan (including a
two-month delay of medical supplies) and inflating operational costs as of early June 2026 [18][41][55]. Cross-border population and
return movements also reshape market cycles: DTM recorded 766,910 people crossing back into Sudan between January 2024 and
April 2026 (82% from Egypt), affecting labour, remittances and local demand patterns [20]. Together, currency depreciation, record
food and fuel inflation, disrupted routes and port operations, and war-financing via illicit exports are increasing the risk of market
collapse and famine in parts of North Darfur and South Kordofan during the 2026 lean season [6][14][11].
Stability of the currency and banking systems
The conflict has severely disrupted financial infrastructure, producing high inflation, currency depreciation, liquidity shortages,
fragmentation of the financial system and the collapse of formal banking in nearly half of the country [24][33]. The Sudanese pound
plunged on the black market to about 4,400 per US dollar; airlines began requiring ticket purchases in US dollars and traders rely on
black-market foreign exchange, while the Egyptian pound traded at around 87 Sudanese pounds — all signalling a sharp loss of
confidence in the national currency and formal foreign-exchange channels [5][10]. Authorities in the army-controlled government,
together with the Central Bank, have pursued emergency measures to boost gold production and exports to artificially stabilise the
pound, measures that analysts describe as stopgap and insufficient to address core problems [5]. A large rise in global gold prices
(approximately +150% since 2024, briefly above US$5,000/oz in March 2026) has increased revenue flows tied to mining, strengthening
incentives for armed groups to control mining and transit routes and complicating monetary stabilisation efforts [14][27]. The Finance
Minister chaired a June 8, 2026 meeting with the African Development Bank, WFP and other partners to secure urgent financing for the
2026–27 agricultural season and to follow up on the Port Sudan water project, indicating continued engagement with international
financial institutions to support food-security financing despite macroeconomic instability [30].
Employment
Sudan's labour market has been severely disrupted by the conflict, with high inflation, currency depreciation, liquidity shortages and
the collapse of formal banking in nearly half the country undermining formal employment and market functioning [33][5]. Poverty is
reported at 73% (covering Sudanese inside and outside the country), over 11 million people are internally displaced, and millions more
have lost income-earning opportunities — driving heavy reliance on cash and voucher assistance, with over 90% of affected
households preferring cash, while local mutual-aid networks and the Collective Sudan platform deliver coordinated CVA [9][33][24].
The Ministry of Human Resources announced a five-year livelihoods plan (2026–2030) to support over three million small-scale
projects, prioritising in 2026 the implementation of 500,000 projects for youth and women and agricultural inputs programmes,
backed by partnerships and resumed financing windows via banks, the Zakat Chamber and the Anti-Poverty Commission [9]. Minister
Mua'tassim Ahmed Salih stated the ministry is operating at roughly 10% capacity, that over 10,000 projects are ready for beneficiaries,

Market and value-chain assessments in April–May 2026 found markets largely functional at the state level but with low affordability
and resilience, with supply-chain disruptions and the loss of financial services constraining farmers, traders and informal employment
opportunities [6][33]. Authorities have pursued emergency measures — including boosting gold production and exports and an
emergency 2026 budget with very high tax assumptions — but analysts warn these stopgap policies are unlikely to restore sustainable

Politics
Overall political landscape
The conflict has produced de facto parallel administrations: the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) re-established a government in Port
Sudan (including the appointment of Kamil Idris as prime minister) while the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) created a separate
administration centered in Nyala under Mohamed Hamdan "Hemedti," consolidating rival control over different regions [132][92]. Major
international and regional actors have coalesced around a civilian-led, time-bound dialogue and roadmap calling for an inclusive
Sudanese civilian transition, with explicit support for broad participation of women, youth, and civil society, and warnings of measures
against those who undermine it [119][114][111]. The Nairobi roadmap and related processes announced in May–June 2026 seek a
renewable, internationally monitored ceasefire and a political track that notably debates excluding leaders of the Islamist movement
from political participation — a point identified as a core obstacle to a comprehensive settlement [94]. On the ground, the RSF
tightened security and redeployed forces across Darfur and Kordofan following the defections of senior field commanders in early
June 2026, withdrawing units from some frontlines and reinforcing others from Nyala and El Fasher [133]. Local governance remains
partly functional in some areas: in West Kordofan's Ghubaysh, local command structures, ministries, and administrative authorities
continued to operate and facilitated access for responders despite heavy militarization and looting of health infrastructure earlier in
May–June 2026 [159]. The rule of law is severely weakened by entrenched "deep state" networks and a politicized judiciary that have
historically undermined judicial independence and accountability [101]. Persistent calls for accountability continue from rights groups
and victims: Human Rights Watch and other actors have urged investigations into alleged abuses by commanders who switched
sides, and victims have filed war-crimes complaints against RSF members in regional jurisdictions for atrocities dating from April 2023
through March 2025 [137][157].
Political stability
Western governments and regional and international organizations issued a joint statement after Quintet consultations in Addis
Ababa (3–5 June 2026) calling for an urgent humanitarian truce, a permanent ceasefire, and an inclusive, civilian-led political process
to restore democratic civilian rule; signatories included the United States, Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Norway, the United
Kingdom, the African Union, the EU, IGAD, the League of Arab States, and the UN [114][119][113]. Those international partners pledged
support to complete a broad Sudanese dialogue within six months and warned that measures could be taken against individuals or
groups that obstruct the civilian transition process [119][113]. The Samud final communiqué (7–9 June 2026) urged the SAF and RSF to
accept a humanitarian truce, guarantee unconditional humanitarian access, release detainees, remove armed formations from major
cities, open civic space, and begin a political process toward an independent civilian government with accountability and transitional
justice measures [124]. Sudan's de facto political fragmentation — with parallel administrations and competing authorities across
regions — continues to undermine checks and balances and complicate prospects for a unified civilian transition [132][93]. Attacks on
key civilian infrastructure, notably the drone strike that damaged the Ardamata Bridge in El Geneina and forced suspension of traffic in
mid-June, have been condemned by external actors and directly threaten humanitarian access and the viability of political
confidence-building measures [98][87]. International pressure that conditions ceasefire and humanitarian access on a civilian-led
transition increases external influence on domestic political competition, intensifying leverage on armed actors while also raising risks
of politicized implementation of transition benchmarks [113][114].
Elections
Media reporting between 11–12 June 2026 indicated that the RSF-aligned "Ta'sis" (Founding) coalition organised parallel secondary
school certificate exams in Darfur and parts of Kordofan, with around 10,000 students sitting tests in some 83 centres; female
examinees made up about 74 percent of participants, and the events were publicly marked by Lieutenant General Mohamed Hamdan
Dagalo and Prime Minister Mohamed Hassan al-Ta'aishi [147][140][118]. UNICEF on 10 June 2026 called for all Sudanese students to be
allowed to sit the same nationally recognised examinations across conflict lines, warning that fragmented certification risks long-term
inequalities and that education must remain neutral [127]. Observers and press warned that the parallel exams are unprecedented
and risk deepening political and institutional divisions by demonstrating a non-state-aligned authority performing state-like functions
and politicising education [140][147][118]. Together, these developments signal increased influence of a powerful non-state actor over
public institutions and raise concerns about the erosion of neutral civic spaces that underpin fair access to state credentials and
future opportunities [127][140][147].
Political stakeholders
On the ground, power is divided between the SAF under General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, which operates from Port Sudan and retains
central-state institutions, and the RSF under General Mohamed Hamdan "Hemedti" Dagalo, which controls much of Greater Darfur and
has established a self-proclaimed civilian administration in Nyala that issues decrees, collects informal taxes, and prints identification
documents [132][92]. Armed movements and Juba-signatory groups — most prominently the Sudan Liberation Movement (SLM) and
the Justice and Equality Movement (JEM) — remain influential regional stakeholders; the SLM under Mona Arko Minawi fields an
estimated 7,000–10,000 fighters, giving it significant leverage in Darfur [97][94]. The Nairobi roadmap (announced May 2026) proposes
a three-track process combining humanitarian measures, an internationally monitored renewable ceasefire, and a political transition

civilian-led roadmap and tasked the Quintet to prepare an inclusive, time-bound Sudanese civilian dialogue intended to produce a

accountability, and implement a civilian-led transition [94][93][101].
Historical barriers to power
Sudan's exclusionary access to political power is rooted in colonial-era centralization that prioritized urban centres over rural
peripheries, producing long-standing structural imbalances between Khartoum and outlying regions [109][86]. Post-independence
governance reproduced that centralizing pattern — formal decentralization often lacked real autonomy or resources — leaving Darfur,
South Kordofan, Blue Nile, and eastern regions chronically under-represented and fueling recurrent armed grievances [86][93].
Repeated coups, the entrenchment of a "deep state," and the militarization of politics have blocked genuine civilian rule and
institutionalized patronage networks that limit marginalized groups' meaningful participation [99][101][132]. The 2023 war hardened
these patterns by producing rival de facto administrations and parallel governance structures, further fragmenting political access for
communities outside centres of control [132][92]. Political initiatives since 2025–May 2026 have reflected contested approaches to
inclusion — some proposals, such as the Nairobi roadmap, seek to bar former regime and certain armed or Islamist actors from
post-conflict politics, underscoring an ongoing debate over exclusionary versus inclusive settlement terms [94]. Sudanese civic and
political actors continued to call in early June 2026 for a national project that counters hate speech, counters efforts to revive
dissolved regime networks, and expands participation for marginalized regions as a precondition for sustainable access to power
[124].
Trust in institutions
Public trust in Sudanese state institutions is low and has been eroded by decades of exclusion, weakened institutions, and the
prolonged war that began in April 2023 [100][132][97]. Trust in health authorities and health-related messaging has further declined,
driven by attacks on healthcare, the emergence of parallel humanitarian service delivery, and polarising social media narratives that
undermine perceived protection [169][109]. Political legitimacy is fragmented: Islamist networks retain influence within parts of the
state and have broadly aligned with the SAF, while the RSF has established parallel administrative structures and allied coalitions,
further reducing confidence in unified governance [94][132]. Key sources of organised support for governing authorities include
international and regional backers pressing for an inclusive civilian transition and providing diplomatic leverage (including the US, EU,
AU, IGAD, League of Arab States, and the UN), domestic military and paramilitary networks (including Islamist-linked cadres), and
entrenched "deep state" institutional and economic patronage networks [115][114][94][101].
Security & Stability
Security overview
Combat and aerial/drone strikes have intensified across multiple fronts in June 2026, with repeated strikes on markets, villages, and
civilian infrastructure causing significant civilian deaths and disrupting humanitarian access [236][202]. Active frontlines persisted in
northwest North Darfur, Greater Kordofan, and Blue Nile in May–June 2026, with ground clashes and aerial strikes reported in Dilling,
Kauda, Heiban, Kurmuk, Bau, and Geisan [202][229]. A drone strike on Ghubaysh market in May 2026 reportedly killed at least 28
civilians; Ghubaysh hosts large IDP populations (DTM 12 Apr 2026: ~22,153; local authorities: 68,000; SI assessment ~30,000) and has
become a recurring humanitarian and security hotspot due to militarization and critically reduced water, food, and health services
[221]. A reported arson campaign and intercommunal fighting in Kauda from 2–16 May caused at least 61 civilian deaths and burned
the central market and surrounding settlements [265]. Drone attacks and aerial bombardment in May also targeted markets and
trade routes, with strikes reported across Kordofan and Nyala, disrupting commerce and humanitarian access [202][190].
Renewed intercommunal violence between the Beni Halba and Salamat in South Darfur triggered deadly clashes and the
displacement of over 13,000 people within a week (4–6 June 2026) [202][238][210]. High-profile defections in April–May
2026—including RSF and SPLM-N Agar commanders—have altered cohesion and local control dynamics, fragmented command
structures, and prompted calls by Human Rights Watch for accountability for commanders linked to violence in Darfur [232][202].
Humanitarian access and basic services remain constrained by shifting frontlines, aerial attacks, sieges, and opportunistic criminality
[202][236]. International pressure rose in early June 2026: Quintet actors called for a humanitarian truce and an inclusive civilian
transition after consultations (3–5 June), and the Sudanese executive's 7–9 June 2026 communiqué called for acceptance of a
Quartet humanitarian truce, unconditional humanitarian access, releases of detainees, and removal of armed formations from cities
[239][242][212]. US senators introduced legislation on 11 June 2026 to sanction individuals and entities accused of supplying weapons
or obstructing efforts to end the war and humanitarian access [242]. These dynamics—escalating drone/aerial strikes, defections,
intercommunal clashes, and continued arms and resource flows—are driving population displacement, elevating civilian protection
risks, and constraining humanitarian operations across Sudan [236][202][232][242].

Human Rights Watch and other rights actors report that neither main party has taken credible steps to investigate or prosecute
members of their forces for serious abuses, and have warned against amnesties that would entrench impunity while calling for
defecting RSF commanders to be held accountable [232][217][211]. The ICC's prosecutorial work remains focused on Darfur, with
investigations reported to be accelerating as of January 2026, and regional and extra-judicial accountability avenues are emerging,
including a universal-jurisdiction case filed in Kenya and calls from rights groups for expansion of international jurisdiction to address
alleged war crimes across Sudan [223][232].
The ICRC has expanded logistical links—including the first Port Sudan–Khartoum flight—to deploy specialized personnel and has urged
all parties to facilitate safe, unimpeded humanitarian access and to spare civilians and civilian infrastructure in line with international
humanitarian law [216]. Security incidents and the involvement of armed actors have disrupted health services and constrained
outbreak and humanitarian responses, complicating access for response teams in conflict-affected areas [257][224]. Assistant
Secretary-General Martha Pobee reported on 9 June 2026 that the Sudan war's regional spillover includes repeated cross-border
incursions and drone strikes affecting Chad, placing major strain on host-state resources [200].
Risks affecting social stability
FEWS NET reported in May 2026 that Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes are expanding and some populations face Catastrophe (IPC
Phase 5), with a credible risk of Famine in parts of North Darfur and South Kordofan—notably Dilling, western Nuba Mountains, Kadugli,
Um Baru, Kernoi, and At-Tine—through May–September 2026 [202]. Several RSF drone strikes killed civilians in At-Tine and Kernoi,
further disrupting commerce and compounding food insecurity in these high-risk areas [202][190].
Large refugee flows continue to strain neighbouring states: UNHCR recorded 927,915 Sudanese refugees in eastern Chad as of 17 May
2026, while UNOCA reported an overall influx of over 900,000 Sudanese refugees and 300,000 Chadian returnees into Chad since April
2023, exacerbating pressure on host-community resources, markets, and social cohesion [191][200]. Heavy militarization, rising
intra-group clashes, and the fragmentation of command structures are undermining localized stability and aid access, while negative
coping strategies and protection risks are amplifying social tensions in humanitarian hotspots [202][221].
Legal & Policy
Legal and policy framework
The international legal framework governing accountability for the Sudan conflict rests on several overlapping obligations and
mechanisms. Human Rights Watch stated that Sudanese authorities are obligated under international law to investigate, prosecute
and punish atrocity crimes, and that using amnesty to shield perpetrators from accountability is incompatible with those obligations
[305][289]. The Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Sudan concluded in February 2026 that the RSF committed crimes
against humanity, war crimes and genocide in and around El Fasher, North Darfur, and highlighted that its findings are essential to
accountability efforts [296]. The ICC has been investigating crimes in Darfur since 2002 and, according to ICC Deputy Prosecutor
Nazhat Shameem Khan, was intensifying investigations in January 2026 into alleged crimes in Al Geneina and El Fasher, while its
mandate remains limited to Darfur by the 2005 Security Council referral [305][297][293]. Human Rights Watch urged Security Council
members and the Coalition for Atrocity Prevention and Justice — formed in February 2026 by the UK, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands
and Norway — to push for expansion of ICC jurisdiction beyond Darfur and to oppose amnesties that would grant impunity
[305][297][293]. In early June 2026, Legal Action Worldwide and partner organisations filed a complaint in Kenya invoking universal
jurisdiction and seeking investigations into alleged RSF war crimes and crimes against humanity committed between April 2023 and
March 2025, asking Kenyan prosecutors to consider charges under domestic law [324][322][328]. Human-rights monitors
documented escalating drone strikes that killed at least 33 civilians in North Kordofan in the seven days before 8 June 2026 and called
for independent investigations and adherence to IHL [335]. The ICRC has called on all parties to facilitate safe and unimpeded
humanitarian access and to spare civilians and civilian infrastructure in accordance with international humanitarian law, and has
expanded logistical capacity inside Sudan — including a resumed Port Sudan–Khartoum ICRC flight — to support response operations
[309][306]. The Sudan Humanitarian Fund's 2026 First Standard Allocation committed USD 40 million to restore integrated life-saving
health, WASH and nutrition services in high-severity and hard-to-reach localities, while prioritising national and local partners to
support humanitarian continuity and localisation efforts [290][301].
Humanitarian impact of legislation
National and international legal and policy environments have direct consequences for humanitarian operations on the ground.
Attacks on key transport infrastructure and intensified drone strikes have sharply constrained humanitarian access in Darfur and
Kordofan, including a drone strike that damaged the Ardamata Bridge in El Geneina in mid-June 2026 and reported destruction of
bridges between Kadugli and Dilling in South Kordofan, threatening primary aid corridors into the region [332][310][336]. Monitoring
bodies continue to document persistent attacks on health care and civilian infrastructure throughout the conflict [309][306].
Humanitarian cash programming faces legal and administrative barriers: practitioners report that authorities' approval processes,
financial regulations and other administrative impediments delay or limit Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance, while the fragmentation of
the formal banking system, liquidity shortages and collapsed services further complicate CVA delivery and reach [273][321][288].
Between January 2024 and April 2026, DTM recorded 766,910 individuals crossing back into Sudan from neighbouring countries,
generating large-scale return dynamics that complicate protection, documentation and service delivery planning [294]. Cross-border
monitoring and IOM findings show documentation gaps among returnees — about 12% reported crossing without any form of
documentation, with strong variation by crossing point — limiting access to legal rights and assistance for a notable share of returnees
[314]. A U.S.
9
Senate bill introduced on 11 June 2026 (the PEACE in Sudan Act) would expand sanctions authorities and require assessments for
terrorist designations, including sanctioning actors accused of obstructing humanitarian assistance, creating potential legal and
compliance risks for aid delivery and funding channels [331].
International agreements impacting the crisis
Several multilateral processes and agreements have shaped the international response to the Sudan crisis. Following Quintet
consultations in Addis Ababa (3–5 June 2026), the United States, EU members, the African Union, IGAD, the League of Arab States and
the UN issued a joint statement backing a rapid, inclusive civilian-led political process, urging a humanitarian truce leading to a
permanent ceasefire, endorsing the Berlin Principles adopted at the 15 April Berlin conference, and calling for a comprehensive civilian
dialogue to conclude within roughly six months [276][347]. A final communiqué issued on 7–9 June 2026 urged the SAF and RSF to
accept a Quartet-proposed humanitarian truce, allow unconditional humanitarian access, release detainees, remove armed
formations from major cities and adopt accountability measures — including mechanisms to address war crimes, crimes against
humanity and genocide [274]. The Independent International Fact-Finding Mission's February 2026 findings were scheduled for
discussion in an Extraordinary Institutional Debate on 15 June 2026, and stakeholders urged the UN Security Council to translate those
findings into concrete protection and accountability actions [296]. The UN system has repeatedly called on parties to protect civilians
and facilitate rapid, safe, unhindered and sustained humanitarian access, while UN agencies warned of spill-over effects on
neighbouring Chad and severe strain on health services from attacks on infrastructure and key aid routes [278][310].
Limitations of current policies
Existing legal and policy frameworks leave significant gaps that undermine humanitarian access, human rights protection and crisis
resolution. Humanitarian operations face pervasive administrative and operational barriers — including access restrictions,
checkpoints, movement limitations, logistical bottlenecks, fuel shortages and administrative interference — that repeatedly disrupt
supply chains and constrain delivery of in-kind and cash assistance [273]. Markets at state level remain largely functional while
purchasing power is the primary constraint for households, and affected populations show a strong preference for cash assistance,
underscoring the mismatch between needs and restrictive modality policies [273][321]. Large information and coordination gaps —
notably the absence of regularly updated mapping of gathering sites and IDP locations — complicate needs assessments and risk
excluding populations in unofficial sites from assistance [282][277][279][284]. Humanitarian coverage is patchy, with weak
coordination networks and a near-total absence of sustained response in localities such as West Kordofan, East Darfur and Ghubaysh,
aggravating protection, health and WASH crises on the ground [277][279][284]. The health system suffers deep governance, funding
and workforce gaps — including collapsed oversight, near-total reliance on external funding and severe staff shortages — which limit
recovery, service continuity and the ability to absorb humanitarian inputs [275]. Donor funding cuts are already forcing service
reductions: the IRC warned that cuts could close 34 IRC-supported health facilities by July 2026 and reported that water trucking has
ceased for 100,000 people in Central Darfur, increasing outbreak risks and service loss [337]. Serious accountability shortfalls persist:
human rights monitors report a lack of credible investigations and the use of amnesties or impunity that undermine obligations to
prosecute atrocity crimes, while international jurisdictional mandates such as the ICC remain geographically limited to Darfur, leaving
accountability gaps across the country [305][289][297]. Published analyses and funding strategies call for removing administrative
barriers to cash, expanding MPCA, improving mapping and coordination, restoring health governance and financing, prioritising
national and local partners with capacity building, and pursuing expanded accountability mechanisms to address impunity
[273][275][301][337][305].
Demography
Demographic overview
As of 31 May 2026, Sudan hosted 868,075 refugees, of whom Ethiopian refugees numbered 67,062 (approximately 8% of the total)
[377][356]. Key registered statistics for Ethiopian refugees (UNHCR, 31 May 2026):
- Total individuals: 67,062; total households: 20,322; registration coverage ~92%; 4,610 households registered at household level
(unregistered ~7%).
- Age/sex breakdown (registered): adult males 33%, adult females 26%, male children 18%, female children 17%; persons with
disabilities 1,009 (~2%).
- Camp vs. out-of-camp: 72% in camps, 28% out of camps [356][377].
Between January 2024 and April 2026, IOM DTM recorded 766,910 individuals crossing back into Sudan from neighbouring countries,
with the majority returning from Egypt (82%) and smaller proportions from Chad (18%), Ethiopia and Eritrea (<1% each) [363]. From
August 2025 to April 2026, 135,179 individuals were recorded returning from Chad, with 66% using Um Dukhun and 34% using
Adekonk-Ag Geneina as crossing points [360]. Among those in the Chad corridor, 58% were Sudanese and 42% non-Sudanese (of
whom approximately 77% Chadian and 18% Central African); children under 18 comprised approximately 56% of travellers while those
aged 18–59 comprised approximately 44%, with a higher share of females among adults (32% female vs. 12% male in the 18–59 group)
[360]. DTM found that 64% of individuals crossing into Sudan intended to return to their places of origin within three months, 24%
planned to move elsewhere within Sudan, and 12% planned onward movement outside Sudan [363].
Household structure among recent returns and refugee populations is marked by high shares of female-headed households: 91% of
households among returnees at Chad border entry points in May 2026 were female-headed (IOM), and UNHCR data indicate
approximately 38% women-headed households among registered Ethiopian refugees in Sudan [367][356].
10
Internal displacement sites show a demographic profile dominated by women and children with marked male absence: Al Nahil IDP
camp (established 5 March 2025) hosted an estimated 18,020 people (3,260 households) and was reported to be 85–90% women, with
the remainder mainly children and few men [359]. In West Kordofan (Ghubaysh) gathering sites, assessments reported a high
proportion of women and children among IDPs; local authorities provided an unverified estimate of approximately 68,000 IDPs in the
area, while six gathering sites officially hosted more than 2,000 households (~10,000 people) at the time of assessment [370].
Sudan's population is characterised by broad ethnic, cultural and religious pluralism — including Arab, Nubian, Darfurian, Nuba, and
multiple Nilotic and other African groups — contributing to significant subnational demographic variation relevant for humanitarian
response planning [381].
Third country nationals
According to IOM, 1,329,732 people crossed into Chad since the onset of the Sudan crisis, including 400,914 Chadian returnees as of 31
May 2026 (projected to reach 405,000 by June 2026); these returnees lack resources and have urgent needs for food, WASH, shelter,
NFIs, healthcare and protection [367][354]. By April 2026, Chad was hosting approximately 1.2 million Sudanese refugees, including
927,000 arrivals since April 2023, creating sustained pressure on host resources and services [375]. IOM biometric registration data in
Chad captures returnee demographics of 58% female, 69% children, 4,809 pregnant women and 14,012 lactating women, highlighting
heightened needs for maternal and child health, protection and WASH services [367].
UNHCR reported nearly 989,200 Sudanese in refugee-like situations in 2025, with major host countries being Libya (388,100), Egypt
(264,700), Chad (181,700), South Sudan (76,000) and Uganda (27,600) [365]. Sudanese in Libya face arrests, hate speech and
heightened fear, with estimates of hundreds of thousands — some reports citing more than half a million — prompting calls for legal
and humanitarian protection and voluntary repatriation support [358].
Socio Cultural
Socio cultural overview
Sudan is characterized by broad ethnic, cultural and religious pluralism—including Arabs, Nubians, Darfurians and diverse Nuba/Blue
Nile groups—and long-standing marginalization of peripheral regions (Darfur, South Kordofan, Blue Nile, Eastern Sudan) that has
helped transform cultural differences into political divides [424][407]. The 2023–26 war has produced a de facto division of power, with
rival authorities imposing influence over different regions and hardening a territorial split by early 2026 [407][442]. The Islamist
movement remains a significant political and social force, with networks inside state institutions and broad alignment with the
Sudanese Armed Forces, making its inclusion in future political settlements highly contested [410]. On 10 June 2026, the Civil
Democratic Alliance (Sumud) called for a humanitarian truce and confidence-building measures, recommending the exclusion of the
dissolved National Congress Party and the Islamist movement from future political arrangements [446]. Civil society actors have
urged community leaders, religious figures and media to confront hate speech and strengthen social cohesion [446].
Local social structures and a tradition of mutual aid have become primary frontline providers of humanitarian assistance, with mutual
aid groups and platforms such as the Collective Sudan coordinating cash and voucher assistance across affected areas [416][428].
Religious community leaders (sheikhs) play influential roles in representing households, resolving disputes and coordinating with
authorities and humanitarian actors [428]. Women-led organizations continue to lead humanitarian response, protection and
resilience efforts while calling for meaningful participation of women and girls in decision-making processes [406]. Community-based
health care, local initiatives and professional networks have partially sustained basic health services amid institutional breakdown,
relying on social capital but remaining limited in resources and sustainability [411]. Assessment teams in West Kordofan in early June
2026 engaged community leaders and women's representatives to capture population dynamics, service gaps and protection
concerns in IDP-hosting localities such as Ghubaysh [434].
Gender dynamics
On 9 June 2026, the UN Special Representative on Sexual Violence in Conflict highlighted that women in Sudan have been subjected to
public rape as a tactic of terror and remain largely invisible in high-level negotiations despite leading local peace initiatives [426]. The
Regional Platform for Support to Women-led Organizations, established in 2025 with UNHCR and UN-Women, calls for women's full,
equal and meaningful representation in humanitarian coordination, peace negotiations and political processes, and for direct,
sustainable financing to strengthen women-led responses [406]. After four years of conflict, women and girls continue to face rising
risks of forced displacement, conflict-related sexual violence, deteriorating livelihoods and limited access to basic services [406].
In the Abyei area, UNISFA reported women's direct participation in a biannual migration conference rose from 19% in 2023 to 41% in
2025, yet women remain underrepresented in local security mechanisms (7–16%) and are almost absent from traditional courts [404].
Assessments in West Kordofan document harassment, looting and incidents of physical and sexual abuse during transit to safer areas,
indicating heightened protection risks for women and girls on the move [434]. Cross-border monitoring found pregnant or lactating
women constituted 42% of returnees from Chad, reflecting high care and service needs among mobile populations [431].
Women-headed households face specific economic barriers in covering food and basic costs, increasing their vulnerability and
shaping prioritization of assistance modalities [423]. Cash and voucher assistance is widely preferred—over 90% of affected
households identify cash as the most dignified form of support—with local responders including mutual aid groups and women-led
organizations serving as primary frontline providers [428].
Health sector monitoring shows Sudan emerged in 2025 as a context with recorded arrests of health workers, undermining service
delivery; gender data are incomplete but men are more frequently recorded among detainees where reported [448]. UNHCR reporting
(31 May 2026) shows refugee populations hosted in Sudan include substantial female shares (Ethiopian refugees approximately 45%
female), underscoring the need for gender-sensitive protection and services in refugee and host communities [433].

Impact of socio cultural drivers on aid
Aid delivery in Sudan is significantly shaped by social structures and cultural dynamics. Recipients reported limited and mostly indirect
engagement with aid providers, often mediated through community committees or sheikhs, and many said they were not consulted
on programme design, modalities or distribution arrangements [428][416]. Authorities imposed regulatory restrictions on delivery
mechanisms—often permitting only digital transfers—which constrained choice of modality and affected accessibility for some groups
[428]. Centralised registration and distribution points were described as stressful and inaccessible for people with disabilities, with
recipients recommending door-to-door approaches and tailored outreach for vulnerable groups [428]. Field assessments that used
consultations with women representatives and gender-disaggregated focus groups demonstrated that meaningful community
engagement increases trust and programme relevance [439].
A West Kordofan assessment (03/06/2026) documented SGBV incidents during displacement, a lack of safe and dignified facilities for
women and girls, and recommended safe spaces, confidential case management, psychosocial support and distribution of menstrual
kits [434]. The same assessment found women primarily engaged in small, informal daily work (tea selling, laundry), and noted that
IDPs in Ghubaysh are closely integrated with host communities, implying assistance should prioritise shared services and avoid
interventions that could fuel host–displaced tensions [434].
Community and informal support networks—including community health care initiatives and mutual aid groups—have been central to
maintaining basic services and local responses, reducing but not replacing the need for formal humanitarian support [411][428].
Regional civil society statements continue to call for women's full, equal and meaningful representation in humanitarian coordination,
direct financing for women-led organisations, and strengthened GBV services and accountability, reflecting local advocacy priorities
that directly affect the acceptance and design of aid interventions [406].
Environment
Environmental conditions
Sudan is experiencing a long-term decline in rainfall alongside rising temperatures and increasingly irregular rainy seasons,
heightening the country's combined drought and flood risk [469]. Climate risks are concentrated in the central agricultural and
pastoral belt between roughly 10°–16°N, which is also the heart of rain-fed agriculture and grazing livelihoods, with historical major
floods recorded in 1946, 1988 and 2019 [469]. Forecasts in mid-2026 signal a high likelihood of El Niño developing into 2026–27, raising
the probability of below-normal Sahel rainfall during June–September 2026 with adverse implications for soil moisture and cropping
seasons [498][533]. Extreme heat and associated water stress are already worsening food security and production, with assessments
indicating nearly 25 million people in Sudan are food-insecure [514]. Conflict and institutional weakening — including the erosion of
capacity at the Ministry of Irrigation and Water Resources, staff migration and loss of expertise — have degraded water management
and elevated water as a national security concern, as noted on 11 June 2026 [516]. Post-conflict damage and neglect of irrigation and
drainage infrastructure (including systems linked to the Sennar reservoir), together with looted agricultural machinery, further reduce
irrigation effectiveness, increase salinity risks and constrain agricultural recovery [506][469]. Gaps in climate monitoring and the need
to modernize observing networks and build integrated early-warning systems have been identified as priorities to manage drought
and flood hazards more effectively [469].
Environmental challenges
Access to safe water is severely compromised in multiple locations. A West Kordofan (Ghubaysh) assessment in early June 2026
reported limited supplies, 2–3 hour queues, water contamination confirmed by testing, and some households walking up to four hours
to fetch water [484]. An assessment of Al Nahil IDP camp (site visit 6 May 2026) found a single manual handpump serving about 18,020
people that yields poor-quality, high-salinity water; previous water trucking of approximately 150 barrels/day (~30 m³) covered only
one jerrycan per household before operations stopped, leaving residents reliant on purchased water at reported prices of 4,000 SDG
per barrel or 200 SDG per jerrycan [483]. Municipal waste services have halted in some areas since the start of the conflict, leading to
open dumping on city outskirts and increased vector and environmental pollution risks, as observed in Ghubaysh on 23 May 2026
[484]. Waterborne disease risk is elevated and prioritized in humanitarian planning: the Sudan Humanitarian Fund 2026 WASH strategy
allocates $13,000,000 for cholera/AWD/Hepatitis E preparedness and response, including water quality monitoring, chlorination across
the water chain, WASH kit distribution and disinfection activities [512]. Accelerating desertification and degraded pastures further
compound pressure on water resources across the country [469].
Agricultural calendar
Sudan's main unimodal rainy season runs June–September and underpins rainfed cereal and pastoral production across the country
[464]. Seasonal forecasts issued in June 2026 indicate a likely delayed onset and below-average rainfall across parts of Sudan
associated with a developing El Niño, raising the risk of moisture stress during critical crop growth stages, while short-term outlooks for
11–17 June 2026 forecast suppressed rain and abnormally hot temperatures (maximums exceeding 35°C) that may exacerbate crop
and pasture stress [464][465][500][474]. Humanitarian analyses note the May–September lean season has begun and that
Emergency outcomes are expanding, with a credible risk of Famine in parts of North Darfur and South Kordofan if conflict, market
disruption and access constraints cut off food and assistance [477]. On 8 June 2026, Finance Minister Dr. Gebreil Ibrahim chaired a Port
Sudan meeting with AfDB, WFP and other partners to secure urgent financing for the 2026–2027 summer agricultural season —
prioritizing improved seeds, fertilisers, fuel and pesticides — and to review AfDB-supported wheat projects and the Port Sudan Water
Project implemented by UNICEF [479]. The African Development Bank and WFP launched the four-year BOOST project (22 May 2026) to
strengthen rain-fed production in Sennar and Blue Nile, aiming to support more than 230,000 farming households and produce nearly
one million metric tons of cereals and pulses over four years [497][491].
12
Large irrigation initiatives described as the "island" project have released water from the Sennar reservoir to start the season and aim
to expand irrigated area (claims up to 1.2 million acres), while observers caution that success depends on urgent canal and drainage
maintenance and substantial, timely financing to farmers [506].
Environmental risks and mitigation
Seasonal outlooks from WMO/GHACOF indicate a high probability of below-average June–September (JJAS) rains across many areas
of Sudan and above-normal temperatures in northern Sudan, increasing the risk of moisture stress, crop heat stress and reduced
fodder availability during JJAS 2026 [533]. Short-term weather data for 11–17 June 2026 showed rainfall surpluses over the previous 30
days in parts of Sudan, while abnormally hot maximum temperatures exceeding 35°C were forecast for the outlook period alongside
lighter near-term rain [474][470]. A June 2026 Sudan climate memorandum recommends prioritizing climate observing networks,
early-warning monitoring (including orbital separation/monsoon timing) and use of seasonal forecasts to guide agricultural and
pastoral investments and planning [469]. UN guidance on post-conflict rubble management urges people-centred circular
approaches during reconstruction — prioritizing damage and hazard assessments, segregation and safe handling of hazardous
materials (e.g., asbestos, UXO), occupational safety, and maximizing reuse/recycling to reduce environmental harm and emissions
from disposal and transport [532]. Taken together, practical mitigation priorities for Sudan in mid-2026 include strengthening climate
observing and early-warning systems, applying seasonal forecasts to agricultural and pastoral planning, and adopting sustainable
rubble management and hazardous-material controls during reconstruction to limit environmental damage and health risks
[469][533][532].
Conflict
Overview of the ongoing armed conflict
The current war began on 15 April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) and has
hardened into a de facto territorial partition that shifted from the capital region to Darfur and then to Kordofan by early 2026 [563].
Fighting is high-intensity and increasingly characterised by drone strikes, air raids, rocket fire, shelling, bombardments and heavy
artillery in populated areas, with allied armed groups further fragmenting the conflict [628][660][652]. Drone and airstrike warfare has
intensified civilian harm and compounded access restrictions for humanitarian agencies attempting to deliver aid [652][660]. Reports
also document widespread sexual and gender-based violence used as a tactic in the conflict [628].
Key recent facts and figures:
- More than 12 million people displaced and credible estimates of up to 400,000 dead since the conflict's outbreak [563].
- Independent conflict analyses and UCDP/PRIO assessments recorded a dramatic rise in one-sided violence in 2025, with sieges
and massacres in El Fasher estimated at around 60,000 deaths and driving much of the 2025 surge in civilian fatalities
[569][586].
- UN and local monitors reported at least 880 civilians killed by drone strikes between January and April 2026 [664][589].
- A Darfur victims' rights group documented 470 deaths and 187 injured from air raids and drone attacks between March 2024 and
January 2026, and reported use of foreign-made drones (including Iranian Mohajer/Ababil models and Turkish Bayraktar
systems) in strikes that have destroyed infrastructure and disrupted farming and trade [660].
- Between 8–15 June 2026, local reporting documented renewed strikes and civilian casualties: drone strikes in El Obeid (North
Kordofan) on 12 June killed at least 19 civilians [651], and a shooting at a social event in South Darfur on 13 June killed at least
three people [650].
Conflict stakeholders
The conflict is principally between the SAF and the paramilitary RSF, with allied armed movements and regional militias contesting
territory across Khartoum, Darfur, Kordofan and other states [560][581]. In Darfur and other fronts, multiple non-state groups are
active, including the Sudan Liberation Movement, the Justice and Equality Movement (JEM), and components of the Sudan
Revolutionary Front (JEM, SLA-AW, SLA-MM, SPLM-N), as well as janjaweed and various community militias [560][592].
External state sponsorship is central to the conflict's dynamics: the UAE is identified as a principal backer of the RSF, while Egypt and
Saudi Arabia broadly support the SAF, with Türkiye, Qatar and Russia moving between sides [563][578][574]. A UN Panel of Experts
estimated the RSF produced roughly 10 tonnes of gold in 2024, creating lucrative self-financing incentives and cross-border illicit trade
that sustain its operations [563]. Human Rights Watch and other monitors documented the recruitment and deployment of hundreds
of Colombian private military contractors allegedly hired via an Abu Dhabi-based security company to fight alongside the RSF
[574][575]. Conflict monitors (ACLED) have also reported defections and intra-coalition tensions within RSF ranks, indicating growing
fragmentation and violent competition over remaining war spoils [574][575].
On the accountability and legislative front, US senators introduced bipartisan legislation on 11 June 2026 to expand sanctions against
individuals and entities supplying weapons, recruiting fighters, smuggling resources or obstructing humanitarian assistance in Sudan
[661]. Human Rights Watch and other accountability advocates caution that defections and political arrangements risk impunity for
alleged perpetrators and have urged investigations and prosecutions of commanders accused of abuses [619][641]. International
partners issued a joint statement in Addis Ababa on 9 June 2026 backing a civilian-led, time-bound national dialogue and roadmap
toward a civilian transition [607][581][626].

Impact of the conflict on the population
By early June 2026, large-scale displacement and acute humanitarian needs persisted across Sudan. Key displacement figures
include:
- 33.3 million people required humanitarian assistance [561].
- 8.9 million people remained internally displaced in May 2026 (a 23% decline since January 2025), while return movements rose to
4.2 million, with most returns reported to Khartoum (nearly 2 million) and Al-Jazirah (1.2 million) [546][545].
- Cumulative crossings from Sudan to neighbouring countries since April 2023 exceeded 3,018,640, with Chad and Egypt among
the largest recipients [549][550]. IOM and UNHCR data show 1,329,732 individuals crossed into Chad since the crisis began, of
whom 400,914 were Chadian returnees as of 31 May 2026, with IOM projecting Chadian returnees to reach about 405,000 by June
2026 [554].
Displacement sites and host communities face severe multisectoral gaps — food, cash, shelter, WASH, primary health care and
protection — with concentrated needs in assessed sites such as Khazan Jedeed, where three camps host roughly 45,500 people living
in precarious shelter with extremely limited safe water, non-functional sanitation, medicine shortages and almost no sustained
humanitarian presence [634].
Acute food insecurity is expanding: FEWS NET projects 40–45% of the population will need food assistance by December 2026, and
national analyses warn Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes are widening with some areas at risk of Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5),
notably in North Darfur and parts of Greater Kordofan and South Kordofan [544][546]. Health and WASH risks are elevated — cholera
outbreaks have been reported in displacement sites and suspected large-scale cholera was noted in parts of West Kordofan — while
fragile markets, rising food and fuel prices, and degraded transport and storage constrain both commercial and humanitarian access
[634][546].
Ongoing hostilities, including drone strikes and attacks on markets and key routes, have caused civilian casualties, damaged
infrastructure and impeded humanitarian access, prompting international calls for a humanitarian truce to enable safe, sustained
assistance [557][567][553][626].
Peace negotiations
Since fighting broke out in April 2023, international diplomacy in June 2026 has focused on securing a humanitarian truce as a
stepping stone to a civilian-led political transition [626][607][581]. Announced in Nairobi in May 2026, a Nairobi roadmap proposes a
three-track process combining humanitarian measures, a renewable internationally monitored ceasefire, and a political transition
focused on state reform, championed by a civilian anti-war bloc that includes the Somoud coalition and the Sudan Liberation
Movement [564][593]. The Nairobi text seeks to exclude SAF and RSF from the political track while involving them in ceasefire talks, and
proposes barring leaders of the Islamist movement (including the former National Congress Party) from political participation — a
contentious approach that analysts warn could complicate prospects for a durable settlement [564].
The Quintet (UN, African Union, European Union, League of Arab States, IGAD), backed by Western states, held consultations in Addis
Ababa in early June 2026 and issued joint statements supporting a time-bound, inclusive civilian-led dialogue intended to produce a
roadmap toward civilian rule within roughly six months [607][580][626]. Regional and international mediation tracks active alongside
Nairobi include the Quad (United States, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt), the Quint mechanism, and outcomes from the April 2026 Berlin
conference, while states such as the UAE have publicly urged a humanitarian truce and the launch of a civilian political track
[564][596][580]. Sudanese civilian actors, including the Civil Democratic Alliance (Sumud), publicly called on the SAF and RSF to
accept a proposed humanitarian truce, release detainees, implement prisoner exchanges, withdraw forces from major cities, and
reopen civic space as confidence-building measures ahead of negotiations (Nairobi meetings, 7–9 June 2026) [593].
Major obstacles highlighted across initiatives include the unresolved debate over Islamist inclusion, the enduring influence of former
regime networks in state and security institutions, the risk that a ceasefire without a broader political settlement will institutionalize
military fragmentation, and the potential need for international measures against actors who seek to undermine a civilian transition
[564][607][580].
Conflict outlook
The Sudanese conflict remains fragmented, with substantial RSF territorial control reported in country-level assessments, and the war
has entered its fourth year with continued high-intensity dynamics as of early June 2026 [589][677]. Local fighting has turned areas
such as Khazan Jedeed (East Darfur) into major displacement hubs, and tens of thousands fled following the capture of El Fasher in
October 2025 [634]. Samud's Executive Office (meeting 7–9 June 2026) described ongoing escalation and called for an end to the April
15, 2023 hostilities [677].
Sudan's crisis is embedded in a tightly interlinked Horn of Africa conflict system — characterised by refugee flows, gold-smuggling
routes and external sponsorship — which raises the risk of further spillover and makes unilateral de-escalation less likely without
regional diplomatic action and tighter arms-embargo enforcement [563][589]. Humanitarian impacts are acute: national data
indicate roughly two in five people face crisis-or-worse food insecurity, with nearly 200,000 in catastrophic hunger, and forecasts warn
food insecurity will worsen from mid-2026 as El Niño affects rainfall patterns, while humanitarian supply chains are already fracturing
under regional shocks [646][638]. Overall, the outlook is high risk for further intensification and regional entanglement; a negotiated
settlement appears unlikely in the near term absent coordinated regional diplomacy, enforcement of embargoes, and measures to
restore humanitarian access [563][589][677].

Displacement
Displacement overview
As of May 2026, an estimated 8.9 million people remained internally displaced inside Sudan, a 23% decline from January 2025,
reflecting large ongoing returns throughout 2025 with around 3.6 million people reported returning to Sudan that year
[732][736][728][749]. DTM recorded 766,910 individuals crossing back into Sudan from neighbouring countries between January 2024
and April 2026, with 82% returning from Egypt and 18% from Chad (Ethiopia and Eritrea each under 1%) [683].
Recent displacement dynamics remain concentrated in frontline areas: nearly 60,000 people were displaced in Blue Nile since January
2026, with multiple localized displacements in late May 2026 in Kordofan and South Darfur [732]. Movements from Chad largely reflect
protracted mobility rather than new flight — 72% of arrivals reported no recent displacement and only 26% reported displacement after
April 2023 — with origins concentrated in Central Darfur (41%), West Darfur (34%), and South Darfur (17%); Khartoum is the single largest
state of origin for cross-border returnees overall (32%) [681].
Movement intentions remain highly uncertain: among those intending to return, 70% did not know how long they would stay, and
primary reported drivers for crossing back included improved security (approximately 50%), livelihood or business opportunities (24%),
family visits (24%), and legal difficulties abroad (13%) [681][683]. Many returns recorded in 2025 occurred under pressure and into
precarious conditions, while global resettlement and complementary pathways contracted, reducing available durable solutions for
refugees [728][749][712]. Cross-border and internal return movements have increased pressure on host areas and services — for
example, arrivals into White Nile State from West Kordofan since October 2025 are straining limited capacities [704].
Living conditions of the displaced population
Assessments in Khazan Jedeed (Sha'ria locality, East Darfur) in early May 2026 found severe humanitarian conditions among newly
arriving displaced populations, critical gaps in basic services, and a limited humanitarian presence amid continued population influx
[685][693]. The three assessed IDP camps — Al Nahil, Al Salam, and Al Wahida — together host approximately 8,000 households
(around 45,500 people), with most displaced households having arrived after April 2025; broader estimates indicate more than 80,000
IDPs concentrated in the area and several hundred thousand displaced across the wider Khazan Jedeed administrative unit [693].
Shelter conditions are extremely precarious, with most shelters makeshift (branches, straw, fabric) and insufficient to provide
protection from climatic conditions or basic privacy [693].
Major multisectoral needs were identified across WASH, health, shelter, food security, and nutrition. Cholera outbreaks inside
displacement sites in 2025 caused confirmed deaths linked to lack of medicines, IV fluids, and sanitation infrastructure [693]. Key
WASH indicators from community-level assessments show 56.93% of latrines reported as non-functional, 15.33% of facilities with
cleanliness problems, 10.22% with safety concerns, 8.03% with privacy concerns, and 3.65% with accessibility issues [753]. Protection
emerged as the largest need among the displaced (16.38%), compared with 8.4% among returnees and 8.6% among host
communities; documented protection concerns include family separation, unaccompanied and separated children, child-headed
households, sexual violence against girls, and lack of assistive devices for people with disabilities [693][753]. Non-food items linked to
dignity — mattresses, hygiene and MHM kits, diapers, cooking and food-storage items — were repeatedly cited as priority needs [753].
In West Kordofan (Ghubaysh), a 3 June 2026 assessment reported lack of updated IDP mapping, high local NFI prices limiting access,
non-existent or inadequate shelter exposing IDPs to rain and heat, reliance on private clinics for healthcare, widespread child
malnutrition, and limited, largely informal livelihood opportunities [680]. Across border areas, humanitarian actors including CNARR,
UNHCR, IOM, and partners provided WASH, NFIs, shelter support, and protection services in May–June 2026, with UNHCR and CNARR
assisting an estimated 67% of refugees to move from spontaneous sites into established camps in Chad as of 9 June 2026 [688][686].
Overall, assessments highlight rapidly growing needs across protection, WASH, shelter, health, and food and nutrition while
humanitarian presence and response capacity remain severely limited relative to demand [685][693][680].
Population movements
Between January 2024 and April 2026, DTM recorded 766,910 individuals crossing back into Sudan from neighbouring countries [683].
Of those, 64% intended to return to their place of origin within three months, 24% planned to move elsewhere within Sudan, and 12%
planned onward movement outside Sudan — with intended destinations including Chad (49%), Central African Republic (38%), and
Egypt (4%) [683]. Individuals crossing from Ethiopia into Sudan mainly originated from Gedaref (36%), Khartoum (24%), and Kassala
(20%), with only 24% reporting displacement after April 2023 [681].
Internal returns within Sudan increased to 4.2 million (a 6% month-on-month rise), with most returns to calmer areas such as
Khartoum (nearly 2 million) and Al-Jazirah (1.2 million); 83% of these returns were from locations of displacement within Sudan
[732][736].
By April–May 2026, Chad hosted approximately 1.2 million Sudanese refugees, with roughly 927,000 arrivals since April 2023. A total of
23,800 arrivals were recorded in the first four months of 2026, compared with 55,600 in the same period in 2025, indicating a significant
slowdown in new arrivals [690][689]. IOM and UNHCR data show cumulative crossings into Chad since the crisis began at
approximately 1,329,732, including roughly 400,914 Chadian returnees as of 31 May 2026 [695][700]. IOM monitoring at the Adré and
Tiné entry points recorded 845 arrivals in May 2026 (780 at Adré, 65 at Tiné), a 39% decrease compared with April 2026 counts at those
two points [686]. UNHCR reports 67,062 Ethiopian refugees in Sudan as of 31 May 2026, representing approximately 8% of Sudan's total
refugee population of 868,075 [711][727].

Drivers of displacement
Armed conflict and targeted violence have been the principal driver of displacement. The Rapid Support Forces (RSF) were found by
the Independent International Fact-Finding Mission to have committed crimes against humanity, war crimes, and genocide against
non-Arab communities during the October 2025 siege and takeover of El Fasher, fueling mass displacement and severe protection
threats [761]. By the end of 2025, Sudan remained the world's largest internal displacement crisis, with approximately 9.1 million
internally displaced persons and large cross-border refugee outflows of around 952,700, even as some 3.6 million returns were
recorded — many occurring under pressure and into fragile conditions [735][728][716]. Neighbouring Chad had received nearly
920,000 refugees from Sudan since April 2023, reflecting sustained cross-border movements and significant host-country strain [690].
Environmental hazards compounded conflict-driven displacement: flooding and heavy rains between April and November 2025
displaced nearly 30,200 people and repeatedly re-displaced conflict-affected populations, while drought and climatic stress have
exacerbated resource scarcity and community tensions [704][698]. Severe food insecurity and economic collapse further increased
displacement risk, with two in five people in Sudan facing crisis or worse levels of food insecurity and nearly 200,000 people in
catastrophic hunger, deepening drivers of flight and reducing absorptive capacity for returnees [738]. Returns often occurred to areas
with damaged infrastructure, limited basic services, and continued insecurity, raising risks of secondary displacement and failed
reintegration [708][716].
Integration
Assessments in Ghubaysh (West Kordofan, 3 June 2026) and Khazan Jedeed (Sha'ria, 5 May 2026) report that IDPs are closely
integrated into urban host communities — often hosted within households and relying on markets and informal work — while formal
employment opportunities remain limited, with women engaged in small daily activities and many men inactive [680][693]. Schools
reopened in Ghubaysh in May 2026, but many displaced children have dropped out because families cannot afford annual
school-related costs estimated at approximately 7,000 SDG per year [680]. Access to healthcare is largely market-based through
private clinics; nutrition services are absent (no malnutrition treatment centre and no measles vaccination campaign reported in
Ghubaysh); and cholera outbreaks in Khazan Jedeed in 2025 caused confirmed deaths, highlighting weak public health provision for
both displaced and host populations [680][693].
Water and sanitation services are inadequate: households depend on purchased water and donkey carts, face 2–3 hour queues,
water quality testing indicated contamination, sanitation coverage is low, and municipal waste management has ceased, increasing
pressure on shared services [680]. Food insecurity is acute — Ghubaysh communities report a catastrophic food situation with no WFP
distributions and limited communal kitchen coverage — while displaced households often prioritise children's meals and reduce
dietary diversity [680].
Despite these service gaps, assessments note positive social cohesion in urban sites where family links and proximity to markets
contribute to perceptions of safety and a preference to remain in situ rather than move to formal camps, suggesting interventions
should prioritise shared infrastructure and services benefiting both displaced and host communities [680][693]. Regionally, UNHCR
and partners are working to expand refugee inclusion in national systems: a World Bank-funded project is extending social protection
coverage to approximately 50,000 Sudanese refugees in eastern Central African Republic, and World Bank-supported biometric ID
pilots in Chad have issued 25,000 identity cards to facilitate inclusion in unified social registries [689][724]. IOM is coordinating
multisector assistance in Chad — covering WASH, food, protection, health, shelter, NFIs, and education — alongside 928
social-cohesion caravans and coexistence trainings, indicating significant operational support but continued pressure on host
systems [700][686]. Key integration gaps include limited formal employment and vocational pathways, unaffordable education costs,
weak and overloaded health and nutrition services, inadequate WASH and waste management, and the need for stronger registries,
documentation, and expanded safety nets [680][689][724][686].
Humanitarian Access
Humanitarian access overview
Humanitarian access in Sudan remains severely constrained by insecurity, attacks on civilian infrastructure, administrative
impediments, and repeated supply-chain disruptions that limit movements across Darfur and Kordofan, increasing logistical costs
and delays [795][796]. In April 2026, WFP reached 3.1 million people with food, cash, and nutrition assistance—including 1.4 million in
Greater Darfur—but drone strikes, insecurity along key routes, funding shortfalls, and supply disruptions are severely constraining
operations and threaten the planned target of 4.4 million people between June and September 2026 [795]. A drone strike on the
Ardamata Bridge in El Geneina in mid-June 2026 caused extensive damage, forced suspension of traffic on a critical Darfur corridor,
and prompted a U.S. State Department warning that the attack jeopardises deliveries of emergency food and medical supplies to
Zalingei and other areas of West and Central Darfur [821].
Funding and operational pressures are forcing service reductions, with reported funding cuts requiring the closure of 34 IRC-supported
health facilities in Sudan by July 2026, risking loss of healthcare access for nearly half a million people [785][799]. Health service
delivery is sustained largely through community care, local initiatives, and humanitarian actors rather than a functioning national
system, with urgent priorities to ensure continuity of primary health care, rehabilitate supply chains for essential medicines, and
protect and support remaining health personnel [799].
Cash and voucher assistance is widely preferred and effective where markets function—over 90% of affected households report
preferring cash—and is presented as a key modality to bypass commodity supply constraints, though administrative requirements
and financial regulations continue to limit cash scale-up in some areas [804][796]. OCHA's Sudan humanitarian financing
architecture is responding with pooled funding priorities in 2026, including the Sudan Humanitarian Fund's indicative US$40 million
envelope to support integrated, life-saving services and protection-centred responses for IDPs, host communities, and returnees in the
16
highest-severity locations [798].
Barriers to humanitarian access
Fighting since April 2023 has produced a massive crisis in Sudan: in 2026, an estimated 33.3 million people require humanitarian
assistance and more than 11 million are internally displaced [804]. Access constraints are most acute across Darfur and Kordofan and
in high-severity locations prioritised by the Sudan Humanitarian Fund, affecting IDPs, host communities, and returnees across 13+
states [796][798].
Humanitarian access is severely constrained by active insecurity, movement restrictions and checkpoints, damaged infrastructure,
fuel shortages, and repeated logistical bottlenecks that impede the transport and delivery of goods and personnel [796]. Regulatory
and administrative obstacles compound these challenges: authorities frequently restrict approved delivery modalities (often
permitting only digital transfers) and impose approval processes, customs rules, and financial regulations that delay or limit both cash
and commodity programming [789][796]. Assessment reports from Khazan Jedeed, East Darfur (May 2026) and Ghubaysh, West
Kordofan (June 2026) documented critically limited humanitarian presence, severe service gaps, and the absence of updated
population mapping—raising the risk that non-official sites and recently arrived IDPs will be excluded from assistance [800][786].
Protection and social barriers further compound access. Recent assessments recorded widespread WASH deficits (open defecation,
unaffordable soap), lack of safe and gender-segregated facilities, and elevated GBV risks that restrict women's and girls' safe access
to services and dignity [786]. IOM cross-border monitoring also identifies movement-related protection risks at transit
points—including loss or damage of belongings, general insecurity, language barriers, bribery and extortion, and long processing
times—that impede safe movement and access to services [812].
Humanitarian access gaps
Access gaps are most acute in East Darfur and parts of West Kordofan. Assessed camp populations in Khazan Jedeed include:
- Al Salam: 9,555 individuals (1,911 HH)
- Al Nahil: 18,020 individuals (3,260 HH)
- Al Wahida: 18,000 individuals (3,000 HH)
- Total assessed population: 45,575 (8,171 HH) [788]
The displaced population profile is heavily weighted toward women and children while many men are absent (killed or away),
increasing protection and livelihood vulnerabilities for female-headed households [788]. Critical sector shortfalls across assessed sites
include water supplies critically below emergency thresholds with almost no functioning water source inside camps, sanitation at
roughly one latrine per 87 households, food consumption reduced to about one meal per day, and food distributions that are
irregular—reaching less than half of camp populations and systematically excluding recent arrivals [788]. Health access is severely
limited by medicine shortages and active outbreaks of cholera and measles within camps [788]. A real danger persists that
populations in non-official gathering sites are excluded from aid due to movement restrictions and inadequate mapping [788][786].
At the system level, Sudan's health sector shows major information, funding, and workforce gaps—collapsed health information
systems, near-total dependence on external humanitarian funding, and severe staff shortages producing regional imbalances and
reduced service quality [799]. Security constraints and attacks against health personnel further restrict access: arrests and detentions
of health workers were reported in 2025, and serious alleged atrocities by armed actors in Darfur—including a large-scale October
2025 assault in el-Fasher—produced mass casualties and impeded humanitarian operations [814][834].
Some alternative delivery measures have been implemented—humanitarian actors began water trucking and handpump
rehabilitation in Khazan Jedeed with operations starting around May 2026—but these remain insufficient to close gaps and often
operate as parallel, donor-dependent service channels rather than integrated system recovery measures [788][799]. Assessments
recommend regular, comprehensive mapping of all camps and gathering sites and sustained advocacy to ensure NGOs can access
and assist populations regardless of a site's official status [786][788].
Information & Communication
Information landscape
Humanitarian information systems in Sudan show significant gaps in population mapping, needs data, and coordinated reporting.
West Kordofan assessments in early June 2026 note weak or non-existent coordination and the absence of updated, accurate
mapping and population figures for gathering sites, complicating humanitarian planning and risking exclusion of people in unofficial
sites [845][846]. Reporting reliability is further constrained by access, political restrictions, and funding shortfalls; institutional
monitoring is vulnerable to shifts in authority and reduced field presence, while open-source reporting has expanded but diverges
from institutional datasets, complicating validation and comparability [858]. Funding cuts and operational constraints are already
undermining response and data coverage — the IRC reported planned closure of 34 health facilities in Sudan by July 2026 and that
drone strikes accounted for over 80% of civilian deaths between January–April 2026, both affecting monitoring capacity and service
continuity [855].
The Sudan Humanitarian Fund (SHF) has documented prioritised response planning and funding processes for 2026, including a First
Standard Allocation of approximately USD 40 million to sustain integrated life-saving health, WASH, and nutrition services and to
prioritise national and local partners, indicating active donor-facing reporting and allocation mechanisms [869][867][842]. OCHA
guidance and SHF procedures emphasise data protection, informed consent, and careful visibility practices, with SHF operational rules
requiring documented proposal review and balanced expert participation, reflecting institutional attention to ethical data-sharing and
17
governance, though implementation gaps remain [877][842][869].
Qualitative studies of cash programmes highlight persistent information and feedback weaknesses. A December 2025 user-journey
study by Ground Truth Solutions and partners found decentralised local responders and strong beneficiary preference for cash, while
Collective Sudan (CCS) workshop recommendations (February 2026) call for systematic public information about cash programmes,
diversified communication channels, real-time feedback mechanisms, and presence of partner focal points at registration and
distribution points to improve two-way information flows [856][841]. Key data points include:
- 33.3 million people requiring humanitarian assistance in 2026 and over 11 million internally displaced [856]
- Over 90% of affected households prefer cash assistance (user-journey study, December 2025) [856]
- 69.3% of households unaware of how to file a complaint; WhatsApp preferred by 78.98% (digital needs assessment) [839]
- SHF First Standard Allocation 2026 approximately USD 40 million to sustain integrated life-saving services [869][867]
Barriers to information
Assessments and qualitative studies found multiple compounding barriers preventing Sudanese populations from accessing aid
details and feedback mechanisms [841]. Recipients often lacked basic details — including who the provider was, eligibility criteria,
transfer value, and timing — and many first learned about cash assistance via community representatives such as sheikhs and
neighbourhood committees, with limited direct engagement with aid providers and minimal post-distribution follow-up [841]. Data
were collected in December 2025–January 2026 in Khartoum and North Darfur and findings were validated in February–March 2026
[841]. Centralised distribution points were reported as stressful and hinder access for persons with disabilities [841].
A collapsed financial infrastructure — characterised by high inflation, liquidity shortages, and collapse of formal banking in nearly half
the country — combined with administrative and regulatory restrictions on modalities (often permitting only digital transfers)
constrains both information flow and modality choice [841][864]. Digital channels and network access are uneven: hotlines and
messaging work only where network access permits, and digital delivery may exclude women, older people, and persons with
disabilities unless specifically adapted [841]. Language barriers compound these challenges — 21% of Ethiopia-to-Sudan arrivals cited
language difficulties and 7% at Eritrea–Sudan crossings — indicating an additional constraint to accessing information and services
[886].
Recommended mitigation measures include standardised community information packages, real-time feedback mechanisms
(hotlines and staff presence at registration), staggered distributions, and in-person outreach [841].
Communication channels
Primary information channels include WhatsApp, SMS, call-centre systems, mass media, community platforms (community dialogues,
religious institutions, schools), household visits, and in-person registration and distribution events, with hotlines and messaging
services used where network access permits [839][881][862][841]. Mobile-dependent channels such as bank transfers and mobile
wallets are used for cash and voucher delivery and have expanded inclusion where networks and financial services remain functional,
but their use is contingent on local network and banking functionality [856][864][841]. Social media and digital platforms are widely
used for outreach but also pose misinformation and trust risks that can undermine public health and humanitarian legitimacy [858].
Face-to-face channels and community dialogues are routinely employed for behaviour-change outreach and accountability. In
March–April 2026, 10,274 frontline workers, volunteers, and community leaders were trained in interpersonal communication, risk
communication, and community engagement to support outreach and feedback collection [881]. Yet awareness of complaint
mechanisms remains low across many surveyed households, meaning in-person channels are necessary but not sufficient [881][839].
Key figures on channel preferences among surveyed households:
- 78.98% reported WhatsApp as their preferred feedback channel [839]
- 11.98% preferred face-to-face reporting [839]
- SMS and other mechanisms accounted for under 9% [839]
User-level recommendations from cash-recipient research include systematically informing communities about programmes
(provider names, eligibility, transfer values, timelines), diversifying online and offline channels, ensuring staff presence at registration
and distribution for real-time feedback, and deploying hotlines and messaging where networks permit to improve timeliness and
clarity of information [841].
Misinformation and rumors
Cash recipients in Khartoum and North Darfur reported receiving misinformation from other community members about expected
transfer values, eligibility criteria, types of assistance, and payment timelines (interviews conducted in December 2025) [841]. Because
recipients commonly first learned about assistance through community representatives such as sheikhs and neighbourhood
committees, with limited direct engagement with aid providers, information gaps created mistaken expectations about selection and
payments [841][856]. These gaps inhibited household planning and contributed to the likelihood that eligible people remained
unaware and excluded from programmes [841].
Coordination and health assessments in 2026 note that community rumours and misinformation are hindering case detection,
reporting, and care-seeking in outbreak and health responses, and that information gaps persist across the health system [862][863].
18
Open-source monitoring and protection analyses show that social media and local commentary can amplify harmful narratives that
erode trust in health and humanitarian actors and, in some contexts including Sudan, contribute to hostility or threats against health
services and workers [858]. Reported impacts include declining trust, disruption of vaccination and public-health campaigns, and
heightened protection risks for health workers and facilities [858].
Recommended mitigation measures include systematic public communication of provider names, eligibility criteria, transfer values,
and timelines; diversifying channels and equipping community liaisons with accurate information; real-time feedback and complaint
mechanisms such as hotlines and staff at registration and distribution points; and scaling up risk communication and community
engagement (RCCE), social listening, and community feedback systems to detect and counter damaging narratives [841][862].
Communities' reliance on local leaders and peer networks for information underscores the need to ensure those representatives
receive accurate, timely information to prevent rumour-driven exclusion or misuse of resources [841][858].
Preferred sources of information
WhatsApp is the overwhelmingly preferred feedback channel among affected households (78.98% overall; 59.89% among displaced
households), with face-to-face reporting at 11.98% and SMS and other mechanisms accounting for under 9% [839]. Community
representatives — including sheikhs, neighbourhood committee members, mutual aid groups, and local responder networks — are
primary and trusted sources of information about assistance and registration in Sudan [841][856].
Access to and preference for specific channels varies significantly across population groups. Many women and girls may lack
personal phones, share devices, or lack the privacy needed to report sensitive issues, while older persons and people with disabilities
often prefer verbal or face-to-face channels [839]. The majority of households (69.3%) do not know how to file a complaint,
underscoring the need for repeated, accessible, and trusted communication on available feedback mechanisms [839].
Programmes should therefore diversify and combine channels — including hotlines and messaging services where networks permit,
in-person outreach and staff presence at registration and distribution sites, and information materials in local languages adapted for
differing literacy levels — to reach different groups effectively [841][881].

Sectoral Analysis
Health & Nutrition
Damage to health infrastructure
The Saudi Maternity Hospital in El Fasher was struck multiple times in October 2025 (3 and 7 October), with the 7 October shelling killing
at least 13 people, injuring health workers and severely damaging the hospital and its equipment, followed by further attacks on 26–28
October that involved killings, mass patient deaths and abductions of health staff during the city's siege [1011]. Since April 2023, the
Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have been linked to the highest number of reported attacks on health care in Sudan, with over 400
recorded cases following the conflict outbreak; monitoring records cite 88 attacks in Darfur between April 2023 and December 2025
and 487 documented attacks on health facilities across Sudan since 2016, as well as 502 reported incidents of military occupation of
health facilities [1011]. In early June 2026, intense drone strikes on El Obeid killed dozens of civilians (rights groups reporting at least 23
deaths) and struck residential areas, a funeral gathering and supply trucks, while local hospitals received waves of casualties and
medical services were described as increasingly strained [961][966]. In Ghubaysh locality, assessments in June 2026 found the rural
hospital — the only facility within a 200 km radius — operating at roughly 10% capacity or less, with 17 PHCs of which two were
non-functional and 15 only partially operational, chronic medicine shortages, interrupted cold chain and blood bank services, looted
water infrastructure, and staff reduced from 123 pre-conflict to about 70 unpaid or irregularly working personnel, leaving over 500,000
people without free access to nearby healthcare [963]. Khazan Jedeed Hospital's staffing has also collapsed — overall staff reduced
from approximately 20 to 4, doctors from 1 to 0, nurses from 3 to 1, laboratory technicians from 2 to 0, and pharmacists from 2 to 0 —
with the facility receiving approximately 200 patients per day despite extremely limited medicines and only very basic items available
on site [944].
Attacks on health centres
Attacks on health facilities and personnel form part of a wider, documented pattern of violence since the 2023 outbreak of war. On 7
June 2026, a drone strike in Sudri locality (Adeid Raha area) hit two civilian vehicles carrying people travelling to seek medical
treatment, killing three and injuring another, illustrating how attacks on transport routes are directly impeding access to health
services [933][961]. Separately, drone strikes on 7–8 June 2026 in El Obeid, North Kordofan, killed up to 23 civilians and hit residential
neighbourhoods, a funeral gathering, and vehicles including a food truck and a fuel station, further disrupting civilian access to care
[961][966][933]. These incidents reflect a broader, systematic pattern: health facilities have been repeatedly raided, occupied or
shelled since the conflict began, including the repeated assaults on the Saudi Maternity Hospital in El Fasher in October 2025 that
caused multiple deaths, injuries, facility destruction and abductions of health workers [1011].
Availability and cost of medicines and health nutrition services
Across assessed areas, availability of medicines is critically limited. At many facilities, stocks are restricted to basic items such as
malaria RDTs, paracetamol and limited Coartem; patients are routinely asked to purchase drugs from local markets; and therapeutic
nutrition supplies are scarce, with one pharmacist reporting only approximately 20 boxes of Plumpy'Nut on site [944]. The lone
functioning clinic in Sirba lacks diagnostic toolkits, protective gear and basic antibiotics, prompting some families to cross into Chad to
purchase medicines [941]. UNICEF-supplied vaccines to Al Fula have not arrived for the past three years, and WHO's most recent
delivery to Ghubaysh arrived approximately one month before 24 May 2026 after a six-month gap [963]. High costs are further
blocking access: soap costs 1,500–1,600 SDG, placing hygiene items out of reach for many households [963][944]. These shortages
stem from major drug-supply disruptions — road closures, destroyed warehouses and import constraints — creating near-total
dependence on external humanitarian supply lines [965]. Logistics disruptions at regional hubs have further delayed shipments, with
the International Rescue Committee reporting one essential medical supply shipment held for two months in Dubai [993]. To address
these gaps, the Sudan Humanitarian Fund (SHF) First Standard Allocation 2026 explicitly prioritises uninterrupted medical supply
chains and allocates USD 20.5 million for health interventions and USD 6.5 million for nutrition, including procurement and last-mile
distribution of essential medicines, RH kits and SAM kits [945][950].
Impact of the conflict on doctors and health staff
The conflict has had a severe and sustained impact on health personnel across Sudan. Between 2016 and 2025, more than 3,600
health workers were reported killed, 1,558 kidnapped, and 2,575 arrested, contributing to long-term loss of medical personnel and
services [1011]. Security incidents have continued into 2026: in early June, RSF arrest campaigns in West Darfur reportedly included
employees of international organisations — including two staff from Doctors Without Borders and CRS among detainees reportedly
transferred to Nyala — constraining staff safety and humanitarian operations [1010]. At the facility level, staffing has collapsed at key
hospitals. Ghubaysh Rural Hospital, the only facility within a 200 km radius serving over 500,000 people, had 123 staff before the conflict
and retains approximately 70, many of whom are unpaid and not working regularly [963]. The cholera outbreak in West Kordofan has
further strained remaining health workers, who are managing emergency CTCs with minimal resources [963].
Disease outbreaks
Recent assessments in early–mid June 2026 report multiple concurrent outbreaks across western and central Sudan amid severe
WASH failures and near-collapse of local health services [930][963][928]. In AnNuhud (West Kordofan), a suspected cholera outbreak
had rapidly escalated to over 300 cumulative cases and more than 70 deaths by late May 2026, with an alert issued 19 May 2026 and
345 cases and 96 deaths (CFR 27.8%) reported by 2 June 2026 [930][931][963]. No rapid diagnostic tests were available domestically,
so samples had to be sent to Abyei and Juba for confirmation, and emergency cholera treatment centres were stood up by partners
[963]. A rapid multisector assessment in Ghubaysh (West Kordofan) conducted 19–25 May 2026 found ongoing AWD/cholera and
measles outbreaks, with the main Ghubaysh hospital operating at minimal capacity and severe shortages of medicines and supplies
20
[928][963]. In Riteiret (Ghubaysh locality), the MoH and assessment teams confirmed 40 measles cases on the day of visit;
communities reported 15 deaths in the preceding two weeks, mostly children aged 5–10, with approximately 90% of measles cases
severely acutely malnourished and no children in the locality having received measles vaccination due to undelivered stocks.
Solidarités International teams began immediate response actions including water chlorination, establishment of an isolation tukul,
PPE provision and community awareness [963]. Across assessed IDP sites and camps, teams reported cholera and measles
transmission, neartotal absence or dysfunction of latrines (observed 1 latrine per 87 households), open defecation, unaffordability of
soap, and widespread diarrhoeal disease, malaria and malnutrition driven by WASH and service gaps [944][963]. In Central Darfur
(Jebel Marrah), a suspected mpox outbreak was estimated at about 80 cases as of midMay 2026 [930][931]. In Sirba locality (West
Darfur), a meningitis epidemic reportedly killed more than 90 people, mainly children, in an area described as effectively cut off from
international assistance [941]. Humanitarian analyses warn these outbreaks are likely to escalate through the rainy season given very
poor WASH access, overcrowded IDP sites, interrupted vaccination and weakened health system capacity, raising acute malnutrition
and mortality risks [930][931][963].
Health and nutrition needs and priorities
Rapid multisector assessments conducted in May–June 2026 across Khazan Jedeed (East Darfur) and Ghubaysh (West Kordofan)
documented severe gaps in basic services, critically degraded health and WASH infrastructure, near-minimal hospital capacity, and
urgent needs spanning health, nutrition, WASH, shelter and food security [929][928][963][944]. Communities consistently ranked
health, food, water and shelter among their top priorities, reporting very limited access to medicines, widespread reduction in meal
frequency (often one meal per day), high acute malnutrition including multiple SAM referrals, and child deaths linked to severe
malnutrition [944]. No malnutrition treatment centre or nutrition supplies were present in assessed areas of West Kordofan, and
measles vaccination campaigns had not taken place [928][963]. In response, Solidarités International implemented water trucking,
handpump rehabilitation and emergency SNFI distributions (including approximately 1,100 households) in Khazan Jedeed in May 2026,
while ICRC expanded air links between Port Sudan and Khartoum to accelerate deployment of specialised personnel and sustain
operations closer to affected communities [944][984]. The Sudan Humanitarian Fund (SHF) First Standard Allocation 2026 has
allocated USD 40 million to restore and sustain integrated, life-saving health, WASH and nutrition services in the highest-severity and
hardest-to-reach localities, with a strong emphasis on national and local partners as primary recipients [967][950]. SHF guidance
specifies maintenance of a minimum PHC/SRH package, emergency and trauma care, disease surveillance and outbreak response
(cholera, measles, hepatitis E, dengue, malaria), infection prevention and control, MHPSS integration, and intersectoral referral
pathways [945]. The combined operational priority is to keep health facilities open and functioning by integrating WASH and nutrition
support into facility-based and community health service delivery, strengthening outbreak preparedness and response, and scaling
localised partner capacity and referral systems [967][950][945].
Education
Impact of the crisis on school infrastructure and personnel
Field assessments report that education services in many conflict-affected and displacement settings are largely non-functional due
to absent teacher incentives, shortages of teaching materials, lack of seating and basic infrastructure [1047][1068][1046]. Schools in
Ghubaysh only reopened in May 2026, while many gathering and IDP sites occupy former schools that stopped functioning since the
start of the conflict [1046]. On 8 June 2026, roughly 10,000 students sat Sudanese Certificate examinations in Darfur and parts of
Kordofan under arrangements by the RSF-aligned Founding Alliance, conducted across approximately 84 centres in eight states
[1041][1055]. Female students constituted about 74% of examinees in those locations, and the opening day in Nyala was marked by
heightened security measures, including federal police vehicles deployed around examination centres [1055][1041]. Authorities in areas
under the Founding Alliance have also announced plans to rehabilitate universities and expand access to higher education [1041].
UNICEF and other observers warned that the emergence of parallel examinations risks deepening political and institutional divisions
and may jeopardise recognition of qualifications [1034][1041].
Access to education
Conflict, displacement, and damage to infrastructure have left many schools closed or non-functional across Sudan. Large numbers
of IDP children have dropped out because families cannot afford school-related costs, estimated at approximately 7,000 SDG per year
in some locations and reported at 6,000 SDG per month in others [1046][1047]. Movement restrictions, ambushes, drone strikes,
scarcity of vehicles, and high transport costs have made travel to services and evacuation dangerous and costly, further constraining
students' ability to reach schools or examination centres [1055][1064]. The wider displacement context adds significant pressure on
education: the Sudan crisis is associated with over 1.2 million refugees and asylum-seekers, and Sudan hosts significant refugee
caseloads including 67,062 Ethiopian refugees as of 31 May 2026, increasing demand on already limited education services
[1074][1073]. Displacement-affected populations are disproportionately women and children, heightening risks of school dropout and
barriers to attendance for these groups [1046].
Education needs and priorities
Assessment missions in East Darfur (Khazan Jedeed) and West Kordofan documented major education needs, including lack of school
infrastructure, high school fees, and closed or non-functional schools, with a call to re-establish school facilities to enable safe return
to learning [1036][1047][1046]. The same assessments highlighted critical gaps: limited humanitarian presence and response capacity,
rapidly growing displacement increasing pressure on local infrastructure, and outdated or incomplete mapping of gathering sites that
risks excluding children in non-official sites from education assistance [1036][1047][1046].

On 10 June 2026, UNICEF publicly urged that all Sudanese students be allowed to sit the same nationally recognised examinations,

WASH
Damage to water infrastructure
A rapid assessment in Ghubaysh (19–25 May 2026) found a single private water yard (50 m³ tank, drilled 2018, 5.5 kW pump) with an
underperforming solar system (32 panels, 8 malfunctioning), frequent pump failures, broken collection points, multi-hour queues, and
high user fees (approximately 2,000 SDG per barrel) [1083][1084]. In Khazan Jedeed, assessments in early May documented ongoing
water trucking and handpump rehabilitation needs for three IDP camps, noting insufficient clean water, active water trucking
operations as of 6 May 2026, and prior cholera outbreaks in 2025 linked to degraded WASH infrastructure [1082][1085]. Explosive
weapons and drone strikes have damaged civilian infrastructure and water systems across Sudan; a drone strike on the Ardamata
Bridge on 10 June 2026 halted a key aid corridor into West Darfur, impeding delivery of relief items including water supplies
[1130][1094][1099]. On 11 June 2026, experts warned that weakening institutional capacity at the Ministry of Irrigation and Water
Resources threatens the long-term protection, operation, and rehabilitation of Sudan's water infrastructure, compounding immediate
damage and recovery challenges [1088]. Widespread degradation of water and sanitation systems across Sudan has resulted in
heavy reliance on water trucking and point sources, contributing to severe shortages that place an estimated 1.1 million people in
Southern Darfur at heightened risk of cholera and other waterborne diseases [1105][1084].
Access to clean water
In Al Nahil IDP camp, a Solidarités International (SI) assessment on 6 May 2026 found approximately 18,020 people relying on a single
manual handpump as the only permanent water source, with high salinity and suspected contamination leading households to avoid
using it for drinking or cooking and prompting collection of a water sample for analysis [1082]. Water trucking had previously supplied
the camp via two bladders at approximately 150 barrels/day (≈30 m³) for roughly two months before operations stopped, after which
households became dependent on purchased water at reported prices of 4,000 SDG per barrel and 200 SDG per jerrycan [1082]. In
Khazan Jedeed, water supply is largely privately managed with roughly 45 handpumps in town (31 functional, 14 needing
rehabilitation) and additional functionality gaps in the wider locality (13 non-functional, 10 functional), constraining access for
displaced and host populations [1082]. A rapid SI assessment in Ghubaysh (19–25 May 2026) documented 12 water yards (five
government, remainder private) operating 12–16 hours a day but overused and facing storage and leakage losses, with water drawn
from boreholes at depths of 700–800 feet that is generally slightly saline and not chlorinated, and people queuing 2–3 hours or
travelling up to 6 hours round trip to fetch water [1083][1084]. Households in Ghubaysh relied on purchased water at reported prices of
1,500 SDG per drum and 150 SDG per 20L jerrycan, while water trucking was deemed an inappropriate long-term response to avoid
dependency [1083]. At the national response level, a Port Sudan meeting on 8 June 2026 reviewed the Port Sudan Water
Project—implemented by UNICEF and funded at $51 million by the African Development Bank—to enhance drinking water services in
Port Sudan [1089]. The SHF First Standard Allocation 2026 (USD 40 million) prioritises WASH interventions, allocating funding for WASH in
health-care facilities, cholera/AWD preparedness and response, chlorination and water-quality monitoring, and short-term water
trucking to bridge gaps to semi-permanent solutions, with a WASH envelope of $13,000,000 [1092][1103]. UNICEF continues to deliver
safe drinking water to locations including Kosti, Sindalti and the Mountain (approximately 15,500 people reached), while an estimated
1.1 million people in Southern Darfur face severe water and sanitation shortages and heightened cholera risk [1105].
Contamination of water sources
Municipal solid-waste services have largely stopped in conflict-affected areas such as Ghubaysh (West Kordofan), with waste
commonly disposed of at open sites and SI water-quality testing confirming contamination that requires chlorination [1083]. No
desludging activities have taken place for years, latrines need rehabilitation, medical waste management is inadequate due to a
non-functional hospital incinerator, and Ghubaysh Rural Hospital lacks chlorine and PPE, worsening infection risks [1083]. The hospital's
borehole and water tank are non-functional, and the main pipeline fills the tank only approximately two hours per week—insufficient
for health-care needs and continuity of safe water supply [1083]. Similar gaps in solid-waste management were documented for El
Fasher, with recommendations to rehabilitate final disposal sites and train municipal staff, noting increased vector proliferation and
pollution risks where municipal systems have collapsed [1082]. Environmental surveillance detected vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2
(cVDPV2) in sewage in 2026 (three sewage samples from Sudan), and one cVDPV2 case was reported in Sudan in 2026, indicating
ongoing fecal–oral transmission risk linked to sewage contamination and poor sanitation [1149]. Sudan experienced a large
cholera/AWD outbreak in 2025, with 71,825 suspected cases and 1,194 deaths across 55 of 80 counties, underscoring the severe
public-health consequences of WASH system failures [1128]. Humanitarian planning in 2026 (SHF) prioritizes water-quality monitoring
and chlorination across the water chain, functional sanitation and health-care waste management, and targeted
cholera/AWD/Hepatitis E response activities to address these contamination and disease risks [1103]. Limited community outreach has
been reported locally—such as Friday-prayer mosque awareness sessions in Ghubaysh—but overall risk communication, community
engagement, and vector-control measures remain insufficient in affected areas [1083].

Water needs and priorities
Rapid assessments and site visits report severe deficits in water quantity and quality, widespread open defecation, and almost
non-existent sanitation and bathing facilities across multiple displacement sites [1082][1083]. In Al Nahil IDP camp, assessors found
roughly 60 latrines for over 18,000 people (approximately 1 latrine per 300 people), no bathing facilities, and only a single hygiene-kit
distribution targeting approximately 120 households in early 2026, leaving coverage extremely limited [1082]. High market prices make
hygiene items unaffordable (soap 1,500–1,600 SDG), and limited water access forces communal sharing of jerrycans (costs of
approximately 500 SDG per jerrycan), undermining handwashing and safe storage practices [1082][1083]. A suspected cholera
outbreak in An-Nuhud (West Kordofan) recorded over 300 cumulative cases and more than 70 deaths by late May 2026, with poor
WASH access identified as a key driver [1096]. Assessment teams in Ghubaysh (West Kordofan) identified ongoing AWD/cholera and
measles outbreaks and recommended emergency water trucking, repair and rehabilitation of handpumps and water systems,
provision of household water treatment chemicals, desludging, latrine construction, and community hygiene promotion including
menstrual kits [1084][1083]. The Sudan Humanitarian Fund's 2026 allocation strategy prioritises sustained water trucking as an
immediate measure and medium-term investments in boreholes, water yards and distribution networks, construction and
rehabilitation of latrines, health-facility water supply improvements, chlorination, and targeted WASH NFI distributions for cholera
response, with a dedicated WASH cholera/HCF package and an identified ~$13 million facility-focused WASH line [1103][1092]. IOM
reported that regional response activities for Sudan-affected returnees in Chad delivered improved water and sanitation to 223,969
people through 17 boreholes, 7,836 WASH kits, and 630 latrines, demonstrating scalable interventions in neighbouring areas [1113]. Key
operational constraints hampering scale-up include limited humanitarian presence in hard-to-reach localities, logistical and fuel
constraints, insufficient storage and treatment points, and funding shortfalls that have forced the cessation or reduction of water
trucking in some areas [1083][1092][1107].
Sanitation & Hygiene
Access to adequate sanitation facitlies
A community-level digital needs assessment found 56.93% of latrines reported as not functional, with cleanliness (15.33%), privacy
(8.03%), safety (10.22%), and limited accessibility (3.65%) also noted as major issues, indicating widespread unusable sanitation
infrastructure rather than pure lack of coverage [1158]. In Al Nahil IDP camp, roughly 60 latrines were serving over 18,000 people
(approximately 1 latrine per 300 people), well below the SPHERE minimum of 1:20 and linked to widespread open defecation and high
public-health risk; only 13 latrines had been constructed by World Vision in the camp [1157]. Assessments in West Kordofan (Ghubaysh)
conducted 19–25 May 2026 observed near-total absence or severely degraded latrines in displacement sites — Al Itilad had only four
latrines (requiring desludging, rehabilitation, and doors) and Al Khanza had one latrine observed — with open defecation reported as
common practice [1156][1177]. Severe water and sanitation shortages are estimated to affect 1.1 million people in Southern Darfur,
exacerbating cholera and other disease risks [1173]. As part of cross-border interventions in the Chad response to the Sudan crisis, IOM
reported the construction of 630 latrines and related WASH assistance that improved sanitation access for 223,969 returnees and
host-community members [1195].
Prevalence of WASH coping mechanisms
Women and girls in West Kordofan displacement sites reported waiting until early morning or after dark to relieve themselves due to
the near-total absence of functional latrines and lack of privacy [1156]. In Al Nahil IDP camp, the severe latrine shortage (approximately
1 per 300 people) drives widespread open defecation, use of ashes or sand instead of soap, and infrequent bathing (about once every
three days) [1157]. Households across assessed sites reported being unable to afford soap at market prices of 1,500–1,600 SDG, further
limiting hygiene options [1157]. Displaced households rely heavily on emergency and costly water sources, including water trucking
(39.86%) and bottled water (22.46%), while public networks serve only 24.64% of households [1158].
Availability and cost of hygiene items
Assessments in early June 2026 found hygiene items extremely limited in displacement sites, with soap generally only available for
purchase at approximately 1,500–1,600 SDG — unaffordable for most households [1157][1156]. Humanitarian hygiene support has been
minimal: one hygiene-kit distribution targeting roughly 120 households was reported at the beginning of 2026, while other sites
reported never receiving hygiene kits or soap distributions [1157]. As a consequence, residents commonly wash without soap or use
ashes and sand as substitutes, and bathing is infrequent (about once every three days or once–twice weekly) due to limited water
and lack of dedicated bathing facilities [1157]. Voucher and market restrictions also limited effective access to hygiene items for some
recipients, and community assessments highlighted unmet needs for menstrual hygiene materials, dignity kits, and other hygiene NFIs
for women and girls [1217][1158].
WASH driven disease risks
Extremely poor access to WASH services is exacerbating multiple disease outbreaks and likely worsening already-high acute
malnutrition across Sudan [1163][1165]. UNICEF issued an alert on 19 May about suspected cholera in Fuja (Al Fula locality); an
assessment conducted on 3 June 2026 reported cases had rapidly risen from initial reports of 96 cases and 27 deaths to 345 cases
and 96 confirmed deaths (CFR 27.8%) by 2 June, with cholera treatment centres operating in Fuga and Um Nuhud and WASH partner
CAFA and health partner Alight leading the response while samples are sent to Abyei and Juba due to the absence of RDTs in-country
[1156]. A suspected Mpox outbreak in Central Darfur's Jebel Marrah was estimated at approximately 80 cases by mid-May, raising
additional contagion concerns [1163][1165]. Overcrowded IDP settlements — notably Tawila in North Darfur, which hosts over 700,000
IDPs — are considered high-risk settings for further spread of waterborne and other contagious diseases [1163][1165]. Community
surveys report major water quality and quantity problems: 52.9% of sampled people said water quantity is insufficient, and displaced
respondents frequently report taste, color, and visible impurities as indicators of contamination [1158].

Sanitation and hygiene needs and priorities
Recent assessments describe critical sanitation and hygiene gaps marked by widespread open defecation, non-functional latrines,
lack of dignity and privacy features for women and girls, and unaffordable hygiene commodities, raising acute public-health and
protection risks [1156][1157][1177][1158]. Severe WASH deficits are linked to disease outbreaks, including a suspected cholera surge in
An-Nuhud (West Kordofan) with over 300 cumulative cases and more than 70 deaths as of late May, heightening urgency for
cholera-focused WASH interventions [1165][1163]. Humanitarian deliveries have included a UNICEF-led WASH convoy (eight trucks to
Dilling and Kadugli) supporting nearly 39,000 people, and UNICEF/OCHA-reported safe water provision to approximately 15,500 people
in Kosti, Sindalti, and the Mountain, though needs remain far larger given severe water and sanitation shortages affecting an
estimated 1.1 million people in Southern Darfur [1186][1173]. The Sudan Humanitarian Fund's 2026 First Standard Allocation — part of a
broader $40 million allocation strategy — prioritizes WASH with $13,000,000 dedicated to WASH activities, integrating cholera/AWD
preparedness and response, sustained water trucking, latrine construction and rehabilitation, and targeted WASH-NFI distributions to
health facilities, camps, and affected households [1162][1176].
Food Security
Availability and cost of food
Markets remain generally functional in many localities but are highly fragile, with affordability and resilience rated below average in
nearly two-thirds of monitored areas [1243][1240]. Joint market monitoring identifies purchasing power — not supply availability — as
the binding constraint for households, with affordability the main barrier in roughly 70% of assessed markets (38 of 54) [1231]. Specific
markets were identified with poor scores across availability, accessibility, affordability, resilience and infrastructure — notably Dilling
(South Kordofan), As-Sunut (West Kordofan), Nyala Shimal (South Darfur) and Kereneik (West Darfur) [1243].
Food prices rose sharply in April 2026 (sorghum +23% month-on-month; wheat +17.5% month-on-month; millet +14%), with staple
prices remaining far above five-year averages, driven by seasonal stock declines, conflict-related supply disruptions, and currency
and fuel shocks [1240]. Fuel shortages and price spikes in April 2026 (gasoline +15%, diesel +55% month-on-month) are increasing
transport costs and widening regional price disparities, worsening market access costs for traders and consumers [1240]. Drone
attacks, road degradation and other conflict-related disruptions have damaged trade routes and markets and are expected to persist
through the rainy season, increasing risks of commercial and humanitarian access deterioration and expanding Emergency/IPC-5 risk
in North Darfur and South Kordofan [1240][1243].
Cash and multipurpose cash assistance (MPCA) are reported to restore access where markets function, with post-distribution
monitoring showing rapid food security gains, reductions in negative coping, high delivery satisfaction, and receipt rates of expected
cash values of 97–100% [1231][1254][1253]. Humanitarian scale remains constrained: WFP reached 3.1 million people with food, cash and
nutrition assistance in April 2026, but funding shortfalls, supply disruptions and insecurity limit coverage and the ability to meet the
planned target of approximately 4.4 million beneficiaries between June–September [1243].
Coping mechanisms
Households across displacement sites report severe short-term coping strategies. In Khazan Jedeed/Al Nahil IDP camp, many
consume only one to two meals per day and rely on food-sharing, while communal kitchens operate only intermittently (last
conducted in March 2026); targeted cash interventions by CRS (approximately 700 households) and World Vision (17 households)
have not eliminated acute needs, and severe child malnutrition-related deaths have been reported [1242]. In Ghubaysh (West
Kordofan), households mostly consume one meal per day, food distributions are non-existent, communal kitchens are irregular,
dietary diversity is extremely limited, and MPCA is absent — conditions that drive reliance on negative coping strategies and external
support [1239][1244].
Post-distribution monitoring from CCS and WHH demonstrates that multipurpose cash assistance rapidly improves food security and
reduces negative coping where markets are accessible: the proportion of households consuming one meal or less fell from 18.6% to
0.6%, Acceptable Food Consumption Score rose from 29.4% to 55.2% while Poor FCS dropped from 43.2% to 16.2%, and the share of
households reporting high reliance on negative coping fell from 31.1% to 13.0%, with low-coping households rising to 43.6% [1231].
Households are also using mobility as a food-security coping strategy: cross-border monitoring indicates movements are
predominantly individual and that over half of individuals returning from Ethiopia cited economic or livelihood opportunities as their
primary reason for return (63%) [1273]. Humanitarian assistance remains insufficient in scale, leaving many households dependent on
inadequate aid and negative coping strategies [1243].
Food security needs and priorities
Humanitarian assessments identify livelihoods and food as the top priorities for affected households, with livelihoods cited by 47.12% of
respondents and food typically ranked second across displacement statuses [1230]. Food stocks are highly limited among displaced
populations, with 74.06% of displaced households reporting food reserves lasting up to seven days [1230]. Food assistance needs differ
by living arrangement: collective shelters require support for collective meal distributions, cooking capacity, storage and kitchen items,
while households outside shelters need food parcels and flexible support to meet household-level needs [1230].

At national and regional levels, FEWS NET projects that around 40–45% of Sudan's population will need humanitarian food assistance,
with Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes expanding and credible risk of Famine (IPC Phase 5) in parts of North Darfur and South

Shelter & NFI
Damage to housing and residential infrastructure
Rapid assessments in late May–early June 2026 document widespread shelter inadequacy, makeshift housing, and severe pressure
on local housing stock across multiple Sudan states. In West Kordofan's Ghubaysh locality (assessment 19–25 May 2026), more than
22,153 IDPs were identified, most (about 69%) living within host communities under overcrowded conditions with no sustained
humanitarian shelter response planned ahead of the rainy season [1282][1295]. The Ghubaysh city population had risen to
approximately 30,000–40,000 (from ~20,000 pre-war) and the wider locality to ~250,000 (from ~150,000), sharply increasing demand
on already limited housing and services [1282]. Site-level estimates show Al Iltihad ground site hosting approximately 300 households
(~2,000 people) and Al Khansa ground site hosting approximately 150 households (~1,500 people); both sites report ~95% of residents
are women and children, shelters are handmade and highly exposed to wind and rain, and no households possess proper tarpaulins
[1282]. In East Darfur (Khazan Jedeed), a May 2026 assessment identified critical multisectoral needs and increasing pressure on local
infrastructure, with shelter needs driven by continued population influx and limited humanitarian presence [1283]. The Al Nahil IDP
camp assessment (5 May 2026) found the majority of shelters to be makeshift structures of wood, dry grass, and salvaged materials,
with no households possessing tarpaulins and shelter conditions critically inadequate ahead of the rainy season; a fire on 14 May 2026
reportedly affected roughly 400 households, and the last distribution of shelter materials in the camp had occurred in June 2025 [1281].
Overall, reports highlight acute gaps in emergency shelter and NFI coverage, high dependence on host families, exposure to
environmental hazards, and limited planned shelter distributions — factors that impede safe returns, prolong displacement, and
complicate rebuilding amid widespread destruction, looting, and abandonment of housing in affected areas [1282][1281][1283][1316].
Camp and displaced populations
Rapid multi-sector assessments in Khazan Jedeed (East Darfur) and Ghubaysh (West Kordofan) in May–early June 2026 found
displaced people living in severely precarious, overcrowded sites with minimal sustained humanitarian presence and rapidly
worsening public-health risks [1283][1295]. Key population figures from assessed camps include: Al Salam hosting 9,555 individuals; Al
Nahil hosting 18,020 individuals; Al Wahida hosting 18,000 individuals — totalling approximately 45,575 people (~8,000 households)
[1281]. Humanitarian estimates indicate more than 80,000 IDPs concentrated in the wider area, and the Khazan Jedeed administrative
unit may host several hundred thousand displaced people [1281]. Shelter conditions are overwhelmingly makeshift — constructed from
branches, straw, and fabric — providing minimal protection from weather and little privacy [1281]. Water access is critically below
emergency thresholds with almost no functioning water source inside camps, and sanitation coverage is extremely insufficient
(approximately 1 latrine per 87 households), with widespread open defecation observed across gathering sites [1281][1282]. Soap and
basic hygiene items are unaffordable for many households (soap prices reported at 1,500–1,600 SDG), further increasing disease risk
[1282]. Acute health risks were documented: cholera/acute watery diarrhoea (AWD) and measles outbreaks were confirmed in
displacement sites, and shortages of medicines and IV fluids have been linked to confirmed deaths in assessed locations
[1281][1282][1295]. Food security is severely degraded — households report consuming one meal per day, food diversity is absent, and
distributions reach less than half of camp populations, with recent arrivals often excluded [1281][1282]. The demographic profile of
assessed sites is dominated by women and children, with men largely absent. Documented protection concerns include family
separation, unaccompanied and separated children, child-headed households, cases of sexual violence against girls, and a lack of
assistive devices and adapted services for persons with disabilities [1281]. People arriving at sites also reported incidents during transit,
including harassment, looting, and physical and sexual abuse [1282]. The combination of extreme deprivation, overcrowding, poor
WASH conditions, and limited health and protection services substantially heightens protection and public-health risks across all
assessed displacement sites [1281][1282].
Shelter NFI needs and priorities
Critical NFI items reported as urgent needs across assessed sites include blankets, mattresses, mosquito nets, kitchen/cooking utensil
sets, hygiene kits (including menstrual health management kits and diapers), jerrycans, buckets, tarpaulins, and mats for household
use and shelter reinforcement [1280][1304][1281]. High prices and limited availability of NFIs on local markets are repeatedly cited as a
primary barrier to access and a protection risk — particularly exposure to mosquitoes and lack of cooking materials — with
affordability rather than absolute market failure identified as the main constraint in many areas [1281][1282][1295][1305]. Assessments
across Khazan Jedeed (5 May 2026) and Ghubaysh (19–25 May 2026) consistently highlight limited humanitarian coverage relative to
needs and high dependency on external assistance, underscoring the urgent need to scale up NFI and shelter interventions
[1281][1283][1295]. Recommended priority actions include in-kind NFI and shelter-kit distributions, provision of safe and dignified
transitional shelter, installation of overhead solar lighting around sanitation areas, and distribution of household hygiene and
menstrual kits [1281][1282][1295]. In terms of response reach, IOM Chad reported reaching 128,211 returnees with shelter/NFI
interventions — including 5,310 transitional shelters built and 27,525 NFI kits distributed — and supporting 367,757 people with
multisectoral assistance as of May 2026 [1320]. In the Central African Republic response (3–9 June 2026), partners distributed 171 NFI
kits to 171 households (approximately 850 people) containing blankets, kitchen sets, mosquito nets, buckets, jerrycans, tarpaulins, and
mats [1304][1311]. The Sudan Humanitarian Fund's First Standard Allocation 2026 earmarked USD 40 million to sustain life-saving
services, with sector lines including approximately $20.5 million for health continuity and $13 million for WASH interventions — the latter

specifying targeted WASH NFI distributions and ring-approach outbreak responses [1294][1319]. Logistics and access constraints are
significantly hampering delivery: attacks that damaged the Ardamata Bridge (reported 13 June 2026) disrupted a key West Darfur

Livelihoods & Agriculture
Impact on livelihoods and household incomes
The conflict has severely disrupted Sudan's economy, causing high inflation, currency depreciation, liquidity shortages, fragmentation
of the financial system, and the collapse of formal banking in nearly half the country [1336][1332]. Conflict-driven displacement — with
8.9 million internally displaced people as of May 2026 — and an expanding food crisis are eroding income sources and access to food
across Greater Darfur, Greater Kordofan and Blue Nile, worsening livelihoods during the lean season [1347]. Oxfam's Digital Needs
Assessment found households reporting widespread loss of income and an affordability crisis affecting rent, food, household items
(including baby supplies and medication), transport and other essentials, with livelihoods emerging as the top priority in April 2026
[1343]. Markets at the state level remain largely functional, but cash and voucher assistance is the preferred form of support, with over
90% of affected households identifying cash as the most dignified form of assistance [1332].
Barriers to farming and agriculture
Conflict, insecurity and attacks on infrastructure have restricted farmers' safe access to land, markets and supply routes. Drone strikes
and the destruction of bridges and roads in June 2026 disrupted commercial and humanitarian access across Darfur and Kordofan,
while drone attacks targeting markets and trade routes also persisted in May 2026 [1363][1347][1342]. High and rising fuel and fertilizer
costs, tight fuel supplies and sharp staple price increases since April–May 2026 have raised production and transport costs and
squeezed farmers' ability to purchase inputs [1347][1357][1394][1368]. Assessments also report shortages and unaffordability of seeds,
tools and rented land [1380][1375][1359]. Major irrigation schemes and related infrastructure have been degraded or neglected after
years of conflict, with looted machinery, destroyed research stations and blocked drainage undermining irrigation plans and
productivity [1355]. Institutional capacity to support agriculture has been further weakened by the migration of qualified irrigation
cadres, reluctance of professionals to work in reorganized ministries, and administrative changes that have eroded water- and
irrigation-management expertise and operational capacity [1392]. Humanitarian and development responses aimed at restoring
production and inputs face persistent access, market and funding constraints on the ground [1360][1370][1373].
Livelihood agriculture needs and priorities
On 8 June 2026, Finance Minister Dr. Gebreil Ibrahim chaired a high-level meeting with AfDB, WFP and others to secure urgent financing
for the 2026–2027 summer agricultural season, with the Ministry of Agriculture presenting immediate input needs — including
improved seeds, fertilisers, fuel and pesticides — for strategic crops such as sorghum and millet [1359]. Sector assessments also flag
urgent needs for rapid financing and rehabilitation of irrigation, drainage and water-management infrastructure, plus concessional
finance to enable land preparation, mechanisation and input access ahead of the season [1355][1348]. The African Development Bank
and WFP launched an $87 million BOOST project on 22 May 2026 to strengthen agrifood resilience in rain-fed areas (Sennar and Blue
Nile), targeting over 230,000 farming households to restore production, reduce post-harvest losses and increase cereal and pulse
output over four years, in a context where more than 19 million people face crisis-level hunger [1370][1360]. FAO reported vaccination
of more than 6.2 million livestock across 17 states — protecting the livelihoods of roughly 1.9 million people — and emphasised
supporting farmers, herders and fishers as a critical livelihood intervention [1394]. Cash-based assessments recommend scaling
Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) and cash-based livelihood support where markets operate, as household purchasing power is
identified as the primary constraint to food and livelihood recovery [1373][1362].
Protection
Attacks on civilians
Repeated drone and aerial strikes in Kordofan in early June 2026 caused significant civilian deaths and injuries, with the Emergency
Lawyers Group reporting at least 33 civilian deaths in North Kordofan in the seven days before 8 June 2026 and local monitors
documenting dozens of strikes between 6–8 June 2026 that killed and wounded civilians [1442]. The Abu Zaima market and
surrounding villages in Hamrat El Sheikh were struck on 6–7 June 2026, with at least 15 civilians killed and dozens injured [1442]. On 7
June 2026, drones struck two civilian vehicles in Adeid Raha (Sudri locality), killing three civilians travelling for medical treatment, and a
separate drone strike on the Charter fuel station in El Obeid triggered a large explosion that killed at least one civilian and damaged
essential infrastructure [1442]. A separate May 2026 strike on the Ghubaysh market killed at least 28 civilians; Ghubaysh has since
become a major displacement and humanitarian hotspot with IDP estimates ranging from 22,153 (DTM, 12 April 2026) to local authority
figures of 68,000 and an SI assessment closer to 30,000 [1412]. Monitoring groups and rights organisations have described these strikes
as part of a recurring pattern targeting markets, civilian vehicles and service infrastructure, and have called for independent
investigations and accountability [1442].
Attacks on healthcare and medical infrastructure have been widespread, including repeated assaults on hospitals and maternity
services in Darfur — with 88 attacks recorded between April 2023 and December 2025 and multiple deadly attacks on El Fasher
hospitals in October 2025 — further degrading access to lifesaving care [1441]. Humanitarian analysis warns of expanding
Emergency/IPC Phase 4 outcomes and localized risk of Catastrophe/IPC Phase 5 in North Darfur and Greater Kordofan, driven by
conflict, drone attacks on markets and trade routes, early depletion of food stocks, and disease outbreaks [1400].
26
Recent multisector assessments in West Kordofan and East Darfur report near-total absence of sustained humanitarian response,
critical gaps in WASH and health services, active AWD/cholera and measles outbreaks, severe food deficits with households reporting
one meal per day, and collapsing market and supply chains that compound protection risks for displaced populations
[1420][1412][1419]. The ICRC notes large-scale displacement and the need for unimpeded access to assist affected populations,
reporting extensive assistance in 2025 and warning that millions remain displaced across Sudan [1486].
Gender based violence (GBV)
In early June 2026, Legal Action Worldwide filed a criminal complaint in Nairobi on behalf of 12 Sudanese victims seeking prosecution of
10 alleged RSF members for torture and sexual violence committed in and around Khartoum between April 2023 and March 2025, some
of whom are believed to be residing in Kenya [1458][1435][1469][1464][1455]. The complaints allege rape, sexual slavery and other
forms of conflict-related sexual violence, alongside beatings, burns, suffocation, electric shocks, prolonged inhumane detention with
inadequate food and water, and forced movement of dead bodies from detention sites [1435][1458][1469][1464][1451]. The Kenya filing
invokes domestic international-crimes mechanisms and places Kenya at the centre of a potential universal-jurisdiction effort, with
prosecutors given a statutory window to decide whether to open an investigation [1469][1455][1435].
The UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission concluded in February 2026 that RSF forces committed crimes against
humanity, war crimes and genocide during the October 2025 siege and takeover of El Fasher, amid widespread reports of sexual and
gender-based violence across Sudan [1481]. UN mandate holders and rights groups report that rape has been used publicly to
terrorize populations and have documented pervasive conflict-related sexual violence, while international actors warn that impunity
remains a central driver and are urging investigations and accountability measures [1453][1481][1461][1448]. Rapid assessments in
West Kordofan (Ghubaysh) and East Darfur have additionally documented reports of SGBV on displacement routes and identified a
lack of safe and dignified facilities, safe spaces for women and girls, and confidential case management as critical gaps requiring
urgent attention [1420][1412][1417][1419].
Factors driving protection risks
Ongoing heavy fighting, aerial and drone strikes, and repeated targeting of markets, villages and health infrastructure have driven
acute civilian harm across Sudan [1442][1441][1481]. External state sponsorship and illicit gold-financing have sustained and
fragmented the war, making it resistant to resolution and prolonging civilian exposure to violence [1413]. Explosive ordnance
contamination, including landmines and unexploded remnants of war, is restricting access to agricultural land, services and
livelihoods and raising casualty risk for civilians [1407][1489].
The collapse of basic services — including imminent closure of dozens of IRC-supported health facilities, medicine shortages, halted
water trucking in Central Darfur, and outbreaks of cholera and measles — has amplified protection vulnerabilities and negative coping
[1483][1412][1419]. Economic deprivation, rising food and commodity prices, and disrupted markets are driving negative coping,
indebtedness and displacement, increasing exposure to exploitation and gender-based violence [1483]. Large-scale displacement —
with UNHCR reporting nearly 927,915 refugees in eastern Chad as of 17 May 2026, almost 1 million Sudanese recorded in refugee-like or
asylum situations in 2025, and over 11 million people displaced within Sudan — has strained host services and protection capacity
[1414][1446][1486]. IOM cross-border monitoring documents long processing times, bribery/extortion and insecurity at crossing points,
contributing to family separation, loss of belongings and administrative barriers to protection for returnees and movers [1408].
Widespread reports of sexual and gender-based violence, child recruitment, arbitrary detention and enforced disappearances,
combined with limited safe spaces, inadequate GBV services and weak accountability mechanisms, further compound protection
risks across affected areas [1481][1412][1419][1404]. UNICEF highlights sustained high child protection needs and gaps in mental health
and psychosocial support, case management and services for children with disabilities, exacerbating vulnerabilities among displaced
and conflict-affected children [1428].
Impact on mental health
Sudanese civilians are experiencing high levels of trauma — including recurring nightmares, acute PTSD symptoms and pervasive
psychological distress — with reports citing that more than 70% of displaced youth show moderate to severe psychological symptoms
[1415]. The scale of displacement, exceeding 11 million people within Sudan, amplifies demand for psychosocial support and strains
limited services [1486]. Cultural stigma and norms suppressing help-seeking are widespread, leading to underreporting and barriers
to care in affected communities [1415].
Humanitarian access constraints — including attacks on bridges, roads and health facilities and ongoing hostilities — are hindering
sustained delivery of mental health services and psychosocial support [1479][1442][1482]. UNICEF delivered integrated child-protection
and MHPSS services to 129,767 people between March–April 2026, including 67,619 children and 62,148 adults, and offered
community-based MHPSS to 17,274 children and 8,249 parents/caregivers; however, coverage overall remains limited [1428].
Assessments identify persistent gaps in specialized MHPSS, late identification of vulnerable children including those with disabilities,
and a shortage of safe, confidential spaces and psychological services for women and girls [1428][1412][1419]. Local practitioners and
commentators urge rapid expansion of community-based safe spaces, telepsychological hotlines, and integrated MHPSS
interventions to reach displaced youth and survivors of violence as response priorities [1415][1428][1412].
Protection needs and priorities
Rapid multi-sector assessments in West Kordofan (Ghubaysh), conducted 19–25 May 2026, documented severe protection gaps —
including reports of SGBV on displacement routes, lack of safe and dignified facilities or safe spaces for women and girls, absence of
confidential case management and weak accountability — and recommended establishing safe spaces, confidential case
management and psychosocial support, integrating GBV risk mitigation measures such as lighting and gender-segregated WASH,
and conducting awareness sessions on available services [1420][1412].
27
An assessment in Khazan Jedeed (East Darfur) in early May similarly identified major protection concerns including family separation,
unaccompanied and separated children, and cases of sexual violence, and called for scaled-up community-based MHPSS, outreach
and strengthened referral pathways [1417][1419].
UNICEF reporting from March–April 2026 shows substantial child protection delivery — reaching 129,767 people with MHPSS, case
management and related services — but highlights persistent gaps in specialized services, late identification of vulnerable children
and weaknesses in referral pathways, indicating limits to the effectiveness of current protection monitoring and case management
systems [1428]. IOM reported continued coordination of the returnee response in eastern Chad and assistance to protection cases,
including 55 people with specific needs in Ouaddaï, 15 women at risk in Sila and 14 protection cases in Wadi Fira, while tracking large
returnee flows — with 400,914 Chadian returnees registered as of 31 May 2026 — that increase protection pressures on host areas
[1433]. A joint mission including UNFPA and OCHA visited Ngoubi (18 km from Bria) on 3 June and registered 31 households (130 people)
among CAR returnees from Sudan, identifying protection as a priority need alongside food, shelter, WASH and health services [1429].
