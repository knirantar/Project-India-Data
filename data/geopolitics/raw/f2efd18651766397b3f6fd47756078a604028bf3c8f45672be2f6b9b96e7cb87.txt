Executive Summary
Syria’s crisis, now in its fifteenth year, continues to represent one of the most severe 
displacement and protection emergencies globally. In 2025, an estimated 16.5 million people 
inside the country required humanitarian assistance, including more than 6.4 million internally 
displaced persons (IDPs), while a further 3.9 million Syrians remain in neighboring countries. 
Protracted conflict, economic collapse, weakened rule of law, and repeated shocks have 
significantly increased exposure to protection risks, particularly for women and children. The 
political transition following renewed conflict in late 2024 has further exacerbated instability, 
disrupted services, and strained already limited protection systems.
Through the Protection Analytical Framework (PAF) process conducted by Bahar in partnership 
with Diakonie Katastrophenhilfe (DKH) and in close coordination with the Protection Cluster 
from the site selection phase through to the completion of the assessment, in Afrin district 
(Aleppo Governorate) and Sarmada sub-district (Idleb Governorate), five protection risks were 
identified as the most prevalent and severe, requiring immediate and prioritized attention.
Child, early or forced marriage: Emerged as the most critical protection concern. Findings 
indicate that economic hardship, displacement, and entrenched social norms are driving 
increased reliance on child and early marriage as a negative coping mechanism, particularly 
for girls. The perception of early marriage as a form of protection, combined with limited 
access to education and livelihood opportunities, continues to undermine girls’ safety, rights, 
and long-term well-being. Children without parental care, especially unaccompanied children 
and orphans, face the highest risk.
Psychological and emotional abuse:  Psychological distress and emotional harm emerged 
as widespread concerns across assessed communities, reflecting the cumulative impact of 
prolonged displacement, economic deterioration, loss, insecurity, and repeated exposure 
to crisis. Respondents reported increased levels of stress, anxiety, emotional exhaustion, 
social withdrawal, and hopelessness among both adults and children, particularly within 
households experiencing chronic economic and social pressure. Women, children, persons 
with disabilities, and caregivers were identified as particularly affected due to compounded 
responsibilities, disrupted support systems, and prolonged exposure to instability.

Findings also indicate that harmful coping patterns, family tension, and emotionally abusive 
behaviors have increasingly become normalized within some households and communities, 
reflecting the erosion of protective social structures and limited access to supportive 
services. Despite significant psychosocial needs, help-seeking behavior remains low due to 
stigma, limited awareness of MHPSS services, and insufficient availability of specialized and 
community-based mental health and psychosocial support interventions.
Theft, extortion, forced eviction, or destruction of personal property:  They represent a 
pervasive protection risk, particularly for displaced and returnee households lacking secure 
tenure or legal documentation. Weak accountability mechanisms, ineffective responses 
from authorities, and limited access to legal and Housing, Land and Property (HLP) services 
exacerbate exposure to exploitation, secondary displacement, and loss of livelihoods.
Gender-based violence (GBV):  (GBV) remains deeply entrenched and continues to affect 
women and girls disproportionately across assessed communities. As highlighted in the 
section on psychological distress and emotional harm, prolonged displacement, economic 
deterioration, and weakened social support systems have contributed to the normalization 
of harmful behaviors within households and communities. Within this context, psychological 
violence and deprivation of resources emerged as the most commonly reported forms of 
GBV, driven by entrenched gender inequality, economic pressure, and displacement-related 
stress. Barriers such as stigma, fear of retaliation, restricted mobility, and limited availability 
of survivor-centered services continue to constrain access to protection, reporting, and 
response mechanisms.
Child labor: It persists as a harmful coping strategy linked to poverty, loss of breadwinners, 
displacement, and school dropout. Children engaged in labor face heightened risks of 
exploitation, physical injury, and long-term psychosocial harm, further perpetuating cycles of 
vulnerability and exclusion.

Across all identified risks, vulnerability is layered and intersectional. Children without 
parental care face the highest exposure to compounded protection risks, followed by women 
and girls, displaced and returnee families, persons with disabilities, and older persons. While 
protection response capacities exist across Syria, including community-based mechanisms, 
specialized GBV and child protection services, legal assistance, and MHPSS interventions, 
available services remain insufficient, unevenly distributed, and overstretched relative to the 
scale and severity of needs.
Overall, the findings underscore the urgent need for protection responses that address 
immediate harm while tackling structural drivers of vulnerability. Strengthening community-
based protection mechanisms, improving access to inclusive and survivor-centered services, 
and linking protection interventions with education, livelihoods, and psychosocial support 
are essential to mitigate risks, reduce negative coping strategies, and support longer-term 
recovery and resilience in northwest Syria.
1. Context and Background
Since widespread protests in 2011 were met with brutal government repression, Syria 
has endured more than a decade of conflict that dismantled state institutions, shattered 
livelihoods, and created one of the largest humanitarian emergencies in modern history. 
Years of armed fighting displaced millions internally and externally, with over 13 million 
people forced from their homes since the outbreak of the conflict, and neighboring countries 
hosting millions of refugees before the political transition in late 2024 . Persistent violence, 
economic collapse, and destroyed infrastructure have weakened social protection systems 
and increased exposure to violence and exploitation across all segments of the population. 
Following the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024 and the establishment of a transitional 
government, the security landscape remains volatile. Large numbers of people continue to be 
internally displaced and newly displaced by sporadic clashes, sectarian reprisals, and residual 
conflict . Conditions in areas of return are far from secure or conducive, raising concerns 
about premature or unsafe returns. 
Protection risks in Syria remain extreme and multifaceted. Years of conflict, economic hardship, 
disrupted services, and widespread displacement have driven families toward negative 
coping mechanisms such as child labor and early marriage, which are widely documented 
across affected governorates, particularly in Aleppo and Idlib . Economic insecurity and 
displacement increase families’ reliance on these harmful strategies, making children more 
vulnerable to abuse, exploitation, and neglect . 
Children face acute threats from violence and explosive remnants of war: more than 5 
million children are at risk from unexploded ordnance , with tens of thousands of casualties 
documented. Reports indicate multiple deaths and injuries due to explosive hazards since late 
2024, including in Aleppo governorate where incidents were among the highest nationwide. 
Children out of school, estimated at over 2.4 million, are at increased risk of child labor, child 
marriage, trafficking, and recruitment .
 
Gender-based violence remains widespread, exacerbated by socio-economic pressures and 
community protection  breakdown. Assessments in northern Syria noted pervasive GBV, 
including physical and sexual violence, psychological violence, denial of resources and early 
and forced marriage , often compounded in displacement settings.

Psychological and emotional distress is widespread across all age groups, particularly among 
children and adolescents exposed to prolonged conflict, loss of family members, displacement, 
and repeated shocks such as earthquakes. Reports from relief agencies note high rates 
of mental health concerns among unaccompanied and separated children, particularly in 
Aleppo and Idlib, with community members reporting increases in child marriage and other 
negative coping mechanisms following the 2023 earthquake. 
In addition to these generalized protection risks, severe incidents of ethno-sectarian violence 
and reprisals have been documented in 2025, including mass civilian killings and collective 
punishment campaigns against Alawite communities in Latakia, Tartus, Hama, and Homs 
governorates . Thousands of civilians, a significant number of whom were non-combatants, 
have reportedly been killed in violence linked to operational shifts after the regime’s collapse, 
including documented extrajudicial killings and sectarian targeting. Such incidents have 
further intensified fear, displacement, and reluctance to report protection violations among 
affected communities.
Efforts to address protection needs derive from a mix of local, regional, and international 
actors. United Nations agencies, including UNHCR, continue humanitarian operations inside 
Syria and through cross-border deliveries, working to provide basic services, legal assistance, 
and protection programming for IDPs, returnees, and host communities. The “Syria is Home 
” digital platform launched by UNHCR aims to centralize information on legal documentation, 
services, and rights to help families make informed decisions about return and reintegration. 
Child protection programming by various humanitarian actors focuses on education access, 
psychosocial support, and explosive hazard awareness, while protection clusters coordinate 
data collection, referrals, and community-based support networks. Despite these efforts, 
gaps in funding, humanitarian access, and security constraints limit consistent service 
delivery, and protection concerns , including GBV, child labor, early marriage, trafficking, and 
explosive hazards, remain elevated across the country.
Despite the protracted crisis and ongoing instability, a range of protection response 
capacities continues to operate across Syria, led by national actors, United Nations agencies, 
and international and local NGOs. According to recent United Nations and Protection Cluster 
reporting, protection activities remain a core component of the humanitarian response, 
though coverage and quality vary significantly by location and access conditions.
The Protection Cluster, through its Areas of Responsibility (AoRs), coordinates child protection, 
Gender-based violence, housing, land and property (HLP), and mine action interventions 
across multiple governorates. These efforts include case management services, community-
based protection mechanisms, legal awareness activities, psychosocial support (PSS), and 
referral pathways for individuals at heightened risk. UNHCR and protection partners continue 
to support legal assistance, civil documentation, and HLP-related services for internally 
displaced persons (IDPs), returnees, and host communities, particularly in areas affected by 
displacement and tenure insecurity.

GBV and child protection actors provide specialized services such as survivor-centered case 
management, safe spaces, and mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), with a 
focus on women, girls, and children without parental care. However, multiple assessments 
highlight that these services remain insufficient to meet the scale and severity of needs, with 
limited geographic coverage, funding shortfalls, and persistent barriers to access, including 
stigma, mobility constraints, and security risks.
Education, livelihoods, and social protection interventions contribute indirectly to protection 
outcomes by addressing some of the underlying drivers of negative coping mechanisms, 
including child labor and early marriage. Nevertheless, United Nations reporting consistently 
notes that weakened public institutions, fragmented governance structures, and reduced 
humanitarian funding have constrained the ability of existing systems to provide sustained 
and comprehensive protection responses.
Overall, while protection capacities are present and actively responding, they remain 
overstretched and unevenly distributed. This gap between needs and available services 
underscores the importance of targeted, context-specific protection programming that 
strengthens community-based mechanisms, enhances coordination, and links immediate 
response with longer-term prevention and resilience-building efforts.
In addition to humanitarian actors operating through formal coordination mechanisms, local 
authorities, community leaders, religious figures, local councils, and informal community-
based networks continue to play an important role in responding to protection concerns 
across assessed communities. These actors contribute, with varying levels of capacity and 
influence, to mediation efforts, dispute resolution, community awareness, referrals, and 
support to vulnerable households, particularly in areas where formal services remain limited 
or difficult to access.
However, engagement with these stakeholders remains inconsistent and often constrained 
by limited technical capacity, fragmented governance structures, resource limitations, and the 
absence of standardized protection approaches. Strengthening collaboration with local duty 
bearers and non-traditional community stakeholders could contribute to more sustainable, 
locally grounded, and accessible protection responses.

2. Assessment Overview and Methodology
Purpose and Use of the Protection Analytical Framework
The Protection Analytical Framework serves as a structured analytical tool designed to 
identify, assess, and prioritize protection risks, vulnerabilities, and response capacities in 
complex humanitarian settings. The primary purpose of the PAF is to support evidence-based 
decision-making and strengthen the design of protection interventions that are context-
specific, risk-informed, and aligned with the needs of affected populations. The assessment 
aimed to contribute to timely, appropriate, and protection-centered interventions that 
address identified gaps and align with evolving realities on the ground.
The PAF is intended to inform a wide range of stakeholders, including humanitarian actors, 
protection partners, donors, and coordination mechanisms such as the Protection Cluster 
and its Areas of Responsibility (AoRs). It aims to guide programmatic prioritization, enhance 
coordination, and support advocacy efforts by providing a shared understanding of key 
protection concerns and their underlying drivers.
In addition, the PAF contributes to identifying gaps in service delivery, highlighting at-risk 
population groups, and promoting integrated, multi-sectoral responses that link protection 
with other sectors, including education, livelihoods, legal assistance, and mental health and 
psychosocial support (MHPSS). Ultimately, the framework seeks to strengthen both immediate 
protection responses and longer-term strategies for prevention, resilience, and recovery.
Against this backdrop, Bahar, in partnership with Diakonie Katastrophenhilfe, conducted a 
Protection Analytical Framework assessment between September and November 2025 ,in 
two locations: Afrin (Aleppo Governorate) and Sarmada (Idleb Governorate).

 While data collection for this assessment was conducted between September and November 
2025, the report was finalized within a rapidly evolving operational and political context. Given 
ongoing population movements, changes in humanitarian access, and fluctuating protection 
conditions across Syria, some context-specific dynamics may have evolved since the time 
of data collection. Nevertheless, while the intensity and manifestation of specific risks may 
continue to evolve, the analysis indicates that the broader structural drivers of vulnerability 
and the identified protection trends remain highly relevant across the assessed areas.
Methodology 
The assessment adopted a qualitative and participatory methodology, building on existing 
secondary data and previous assessments. Data collection was structured around four 
thematic areas:
• Protection risks, threats, and vulnerabilities
• Protection responses, capacities, and self-protection strategies
• Protection stakeholders, programs, and activities
• Existing capacities and priority recommendations
Sampling 
This sampling approach supported the collection of qualitative and contextualized data, 
enabling an in-depth analysis of differentiated protection risks, access to services, and coping 
mechanisms among diverse population groups.
The assessment adopted a purposive sampling approach to ensure representation of 
population groups most exposed to protection risks in Afrin (Aleppo Governorate) and Sarmada 
(Idleb Governorate), while accounting for geographic location, age, gender, displacement 
status, and levels of vulnerability.

Key Informant Interviews were conducted with individuals selected based on their direct 
experience and in-depth knowledge of the local context, including community leaders, service 
providers, and humanitarian actors. This approach aimed to capture nuanced perspectives 
on protection risks, existing response mechanisms, and gaps in service provision.
Focus Group Discussions were organized according to age and gender criteria, with separate 
groups for women, men, boys, and girls, to ensure a safe and enabling environment for open 
and meaningful discussion. Priority was given to participants from population groups directly 
affected by protection risks, including displaced households, children without parental care, 
and other highly vulnerable community members.
Household interviews were conducted using a purposive approach complemented by 
elements of convenience sampling, targeting communities and locations with higher levels of 
vulnerability and reported protection concerns. Efforts were made to include displaced and 
returnee households, female-headed households, and households including persons with 
disabilities or children at heightened risk, in order to reflect diverse protection experiences 
across the two locations.
Data analysis
Data collected through KIIs, FGDs, and household interviews was cleaned, coded, consolidated, 
and analyzed using both quantitative and qualitative analytical approaches.
Quantitative data was analyzed using Microsoft Excel to identify frequencies, prevalence 
levels, trends, and comparative patterns across assessed protection risks, population groups, 
locations, gender, age categories, and displacement status. Basic analytical functions and 
comparative tabulation techniques were used to support data interpretation and consistency 
checks.

Qualitative data collected through FGDs and KIIs was reviewed thematically through 
comparative analysis and protection-focused interpretation to identify recurring concerns, 
contextual drivers, coping mechanisms, vulnerabilities, community perceptions, and 
response gaps. Analysis was informed by protection principles and sectoral expertise to 
support contextual understanding of the findings.
Triangulation was applied across the three data collection methods to strengthen the 
reliability and consistency of findings and to identify converging or diverging trends between 
different respondent groups and locations.
Findings were analyzed using a Protection Analytical Framework (PAF)-informed approach, 
linking contextual dynamics, protection threats, affected population groups, coping strategies, 
and existing response capacities.
Limitations
The assessment adopted a purposive and community-based sampling 
approach focused on populations identified as highly exposed to 
protection risks. As a result, findings are intended to provide contextual 
and analytical insights into protection trends and vulnerabilities rather 
than statistically representative results for the broader population.
Data collection relied partially on self-reporting and community 
perceptions, which may be influenced by underreporting, stigma, 
social desirability bias, fear of disclosure, or normalization of certain 
harmful practices, particularly in relation to GBV, psychological harm, 
and protection incidents occurring within households.
To mitigate these limitations, information was triangulated across KIIs, 
FGDs, and household interviews, while separate group discussions by 
age and gender were conducted to support safer participation and 
improve the reliability of sensitive disclosures.
The assessment findings should also be interpreted in light of the 
evolving operational and political context in Syria, as outlined earlier 
in the methodology section. While context-specific dynamics may 
continue to shift over time, the analysis remains relevant in highlighting 
broader protection trends, vulnerabilities, and structural drivers 
affecting assessed communities.

3. Protection Risks Analysis
Building on the evolving context and existing protection response capacities across Syria, the 
Protection Analytical Framework analysis highlights how prolonged displacement, economic 
deterioration, and weakened protective systems have reshaped the nature and severity of 
protection risks in Afrin and Sarmada.
The following section examines the five priority protection risks identified through the 
assessment, analyzing their prevalence, key drivers, affected population groups, impacts, 
and existing response gaps. This analysis adopts a PAF-informed approach, linking contextual 
factors to observed harm and identifying critical entry points for prevention, mitigation, and 
response.  
  
Risk 1: Child, Early, and Forced Marriage
Child, early, and forced marriage (CEFM) emerged as the most critical protection risk across 
the assessed communities in Afrin and Sarmada, with 93% of respondents perceiving early 
marriage as highly prevalent.
The practice is overwhelmingly gendered, disproportionately affecting adolescent girls, 
particularly those from economically vulnerable, displaced, or returnee households. Children 
without parental care, especially unaccompanied children and orphans, face the highest 
exposure to this risk. Economic hardship was identified as the primary driver, compounded 
by displacement, loss of livelihoods, school dropout, and limited access to safe income 
opportunities. Entrenched social norms and the perception of early marriage as a protective 
strategy in insecure environments further reinforce the practice, particularly where rule of 
law and service availability remain weak.
The consequences are severe and long-lasting, including increased exposure to domestic 
violence, early pregnancy and associated health risks, psychological distress, school dropout, 
and long-term economic dependency. Although limited awareness and case management 
services exist, the majority of respondents reported insufficient specialized support,  including 
limited engagement by local authorities, often constrained by weak institutional capacity and 
inconsistent service availability, highlighting significant gaps in prevention, protection, and 
structural response mechanisms.
Risk 2: Psychological and Emotional Abuse
Psychological and emotional abuse emerged as one of the most prevalent and socially 
normalized protection risks across the assessed communities, with 55% of respondents 
reporting an increase over the past year. 
This form of abuse occurs primarily within the household and is closely linked to entrenched 
power imbalances, prolonged displacement, economic stress, and the erosion of traditional 
support networks. Fathers and husbands were most frequently identified as perpetrators, 
as reported across different respondent groups, including women, men, and key informants, 
with common manifestations including verbal humiliation, threats, neglect, social isolation, 
and constant criticism.

 High levels of perceived social acceptance, with 65% of respondents considering this form 
of violence socially acceptable, indicate the normalization of non-physical violence, often 
perceived as a legitimate form of discipline or control. Women were identified as the most

Risk 3: Extortion, Forced Eviction, and Destruction of Property
Extortion, forced eviction, and destruction of property constitute a widespread and persistent 
protection risk in Afrin and Sarmada, with 75% of respondents reporting these violations as 
highly prevalent. These risks are closely linked to insecure tenure, lack of civil documentation, 
limited access to legal recourse, and fragmented governance structures, particularly affecting 
displaced and returnee households.
Economic hardship and power imbalances further exacerbate vulnerability, while fear of 
retaliation and low trust in authorities contribute to significant underreporting.Displaced and 
returnee families, women-headed households, and individuals lacking documentation are 
disproportionately affected, often facing coercion or informal demands to vacate housing or 
surrender property without effective means of redress. Among those affected, 50% reported 
experiencing violence or threats during these incidents. The impacts include loss of shelter 
and livelihoods, accumulation of debt, psychological distress, and secondary displacement.

The destruction or loss of personal documentation further restricts access to services, legal 
protection, and durable solutions. Although legal assistance and Housing, Land and Property 
(HLP) support are provided by some actors, including humanitarian organizations and legal 
assistance providers, respondents reported extremely limited availability and accessibility of 
services, underscoring major gaps in accountability, prevention, and response mechanisms.
Risk 4: Gender-based violence
Gender-based violence (GBV) remains a deeply entrenched and pervasive protection risk in 
the assessed communities of Afrin and Sarmada, consistent with broader United Nations 
and protection sector reporting that identifies GBV as a critical and underreported concern 
across Syria. Assessment findings indicate that 74% of households perceive GBV as a major 
risk affecting women and girls, as reported across different respondent groups, including 
women and men within households, reinforcing national-level analysis.
 Psychological violence and deprivation of resources were identified as the most prevalent 
forms reported over the past 12 months, driven by entrenched gender inequality, patriarchal 
norms, displacement-related stress, and economic hardship. Secondary reporting further 
highlights how overcrowding, lack of privacy, weakened community protection mechanisms, 
and limited access to services in displacement settings heighten exposure to multiple forms 
of violence and normalize harmful practices.
Incidents were reported to occur primarily within the household, followed by public spaces 
such as markets, workplaces, and, to a lesser extent, schools and service centers Husbands 
were identified as the primary perpetrators, followed by fathers and male members of the 
extended family, with some reports also indicating involvement of armed actors. Women and 
girls are disproportionately affected, with adolescent girls facing compounded risks including 
early and forced marriage, psychological abuse, and restrictions on mobility.

GBV results in severe and long-lasting consequences, including psychosocial distress, physical 
harm, school dropout, restricted livelihood opportunities, and social isolation. Despite 
the presence of specialized services such as survivor-centered case management, safe 
spaces, and MHPSS. Existing response capacities include services provided by humanitarian 
organizations, such as survivor-centered case management, women and girls’ safe spaces, 
mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), and limited engagement of local authorities 
and community-based mechanisms. Both assessment findings and sector reporting point to 
significant gaps in coverage, accessibility, and sustainability, while stigma, fear of retaliation, 
mobility constraints, and limited geographic reach continue to suppress reporting and restrict 
access to timely and appropriate support.
Risk 5: Child Labor
Child labor remains a prevalent protection risk across Afrin and Sarmada, with 69% of 
respondents reporting it as a common coping strategy linked to prolonged economic 
deterioration, displacement, and repeated shocks that have weakened household resilience.
Poverty and the absence of a breadwinner were identified as primary drivers, compounded 
by school dropout and limited livelihood opportunities for caregivers. The growing number of 
returnees is placing additional pressure on Syria’s already fragile protection systems, further 
straining local services and household coping capacities and increasing reliance on harmful 
strategies such as child labor.
Boys are more frequently engaged in hazardous or exploitative labor in agriculture, workshops, 
factories, and informal trades, while girls may be involved in domestic or less visible forms of 
work that often remain underreported.

Children from displaced, returnee, and economically vulnerable households, as well as

Engagement in labor results in school dropout, physical injury, exploitation, and long-term

4. Recommendations
Based on the assessment findings, the following strategic recommendations are proposed 
to inform protection programming, coordination, and advocacy efforts in Afrin (Aleppo) and 
Sarmada (Idleb). These recommendations prioritize prevention, mitigation, and response to 
identified protection risks, while addressing underlying drivers and structural gaps.
Risk I: Child, Early, and Forced Marriage
• Strengthen community-based prevention mechanisms led by NGOs in coordination with 
community structures and relevant local authorities, addressing child, early, and forced 
marriage, with a specific focus on high-risk groups, including children without parental 
care, displaced families, and adolescent girls. This includes establishing and supporting 
community-based protection committees, conducting regular awareness sessions and 
caregiver dialogues, training community focal points to identify and refer at-risk cases, 
and engaging religious and community leaders to promote protective norms and early 
identification of risks.

• Expand access to safe,inclusive education opportunities and support school retention 
for girls, back-to-school campaigns, provision of learning materials and transportation 
support, establishment of safe learning spaces, and conditional or unconditional cash 
assistance to support school attendance. Education interventions should be linked with 
protection services to ensure follow-up for girls at risk of dropout.
• Promote sustained community engagement initiatives including community dialogue 
sessions, awareness campaigns, peer group discussions, and engagement with religious 
and community leaders, aimed at addressing harmful social norms and perceptions that 
frame early marriage as a protective strategy. These efforts should prioritize dialogue with 
caregivers, men and boys, and influential community actors to support behavior change 
and shared responsibility for girls’ protection.
• Strengthen case management and referral pathways for girls at risk of or affected by early 
and forced marriage, ensuring timely access to psychosocial support, legal assistance, and 
child protection services, particularly for unaccompanied and separated children.
• Integrate protection interventions with livelihoods and economic support for vulnerable 
households to reduce reliance on negative coping mechanisms, while ensuring that 
economic assistance is complemented by community-based prevention and awareness 
activities.
• Advocate for improved access to legal frameworks and protective services that uphold 
the rights of girls, targeting relevant local authorities, de facto governance structures, and 
coordination platforms, through joint advocacy efforts, policy dialogue, and coordination 
with the Protection Cluster and its AoRs, to support prevention and response to early and 
forced marriage.
Risk 2: Psychological and Emotional Abuse
• Increase community awareness and recognition of psychological and emotional abuse 
as forms of violence with serious and long-term psychosocial consequences. Awareness 
activities should be culturally sensitive and tailored to address the normalization of 
non-physical violence within households and communities, and may include NGO-led 
community awareness sessions, interactive theatre and storytelling activities, school-
based awareness initiatives, and engagement through local media and social media 
platforms, implemented in coordination with community leaders and local structures.
• Integrate mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) across protection and child 
protection interventions to address the cumulative impact of chronic stress, displacement, 
and emotional abuse. This includes embedding basic psychosocial support within 
community-based activities and ensuring referral pathways to specialized services when 
needed, through training of frontline staff and community volunteers, establishing mobile 
MHPSS teams, and integrating psychosocial support into education and community 
centers, led by NGOs in coordination with health and protection actors.
• Promote positive parenting approachesand non-violent coping strategies through 
caregiver-focused interventions, recognizing the role of prolonged stress and economic 
hardship in shaping harmful household dynamics, including structured parenting sessions, 
peer support groups for caregivers, home visits for high-risk families, and the use of digital 
or audio-visual tools to reinforce positive practices, implemented by trained facilitators 
and community-based organizations.

individuals experiencing psychological and emotional abuse, with particular attention to 
women, children, persons with disabilities, and older persons, including the establishment

safe entry points within community centers and service facilities, managed by protection

providers to identify, respond to, and follow up on cases of psychological and emotional 
abuse in a survivor-centered and age-appropriate manner, through structured training 
programs, on-the-job coaching, development of standard operating procedures (SOPs), 
and regular supervision and mentoring, led by specialized protection and MHPSS actors.
Risk 3: Extortion, Forced Eviction, and Destruction of Property
• Strengthen community-based protection mechanisms to prevent and respond to extortion, 
forced eviction, and property-related violations, through NGO-led establishment and 
support of community protection committees, community-based complaint and feedback 
mechanisms, and awareness sessions on HLP rights, as well as training community focal 
points to identify and refer cases to legal and protection service providers. 
• Expand access to legal assistance and Housing, Land and Property (HLP) support for 
displaced and returnee households, through mobile legal clinics, legal aid desks in 
community centers, and provision of legal counseling and representation, with a focus 
on individuals lacking civil documentation, women-headed households, and other groups 
at heightened risk of eviction and exploitation, implemented by legal aid organizations in 
coordination with protection actors.
• Enhance legal awareness and information-sharing initiatives to improve understanding 
of tenure rights, documentation processes, and available dispute resolution mechanisms, 
through targeted awareness campaigns, distribution of simplified information materials, 
community workshops, and engagement with local leaders, particularly in areas 
experiencing high levels of displacement and return.
• Advocate for improved accountability and more effective responses from relevant 
authorities and local governance structures, in coordination with protection and HLP actors, 
to address property-related violations and reduce impunity, through coordinated advocacy 
efforts, engagement with local councils and de facto authorities, case documentation 
and evidence-based reporting, and participation in coordination platforms such as the 
Protection Cluster and HLP AoR
• Integrate protection interventions with livelihoods and emergency assistance to mitigate 
the economic drivers of eviction and extortion, through cash assistance, rental support, 
and livelihood opportunities for at-risk households, while ensuring that assistance is 
delivered in a protection-sensitive manner and linked with case management and referral 
systems.
Risk 4: Gender-based violence
• Scale up comprehensive, survivor-centered GBV prevention and response services that 
are safe, confidential, and accessible, including case management, psychosocial support, 
and referral pathways. implemented by specialized GBV actors and NGOs in coordination 
with health and protection partners, through fixed and mobile service delivery, safe 
spaces, and outreach teams. Services should prioritize women and girls at heightened 
risk, particularly in displacement and return settings.

• Strengthen community-based GBV prevention initiatives that address harmful gender 
norms, power imbalances, and the normalization of violence against women and girls. 
through NGO-led community dialogues, peer-to-peer group sessions, engagement of 
men and boys as change agents, and innovative approaches such as community theatre,

• Improve access to GBV services by addressing barriers such as stigma, fear of retaliation,

• Enhance coordination and information-sharing among GBV, protection, health, and

• Integrate GBV risk mitigation measures across sectors, including education, livelihoods,

do not inadvertently increase risks for women and girls, through safety audits, gender-

safeguards in program implementation.
Risk 5: Child Labor
• Reduce household economic pressure through protection-sensitive interventions that 
address the underlying drivers of child labor, including poverty, loss of breadwinners, and 
displacement. Economic assistance should be linked with child protection monitoring and 
follow-up to prevent unintended harm.
• Strengthen access to safe, quality education and alternative learning opportunities for 
children engaged in or at risk of child labor, recognizing education as a key protective 
factor. Education interventions should be flexible and adapted to the needs of displaced 
and working children.
• Support and reinforce community-based child protection mechanisms to identify, refer, 
and monitor cases of child labor, ensuring timely access to appropriate services and 
follow-up support.
• Enhance case management and referral pathways for children exposed to exploitative or 
hazardous labor, with a focus on long-term protection outcomes, psychosocial well-being, 
and reintegration into education or safe livelihoods.
• Integrate child labor prevention and response measures across sectors, including 
livelihoods, education, and humanitarian assistance, to reduce reliance on child labor as 
a negative coping strategy.

6. Conclusion
This Protection Analytical Framework (PAF) highlights an evolving and increasingly complex 
protection environment in Afrin (Aleppo) and Sarmada (Idleb), shaped by protracted conflict, 
economic deterioration, repeated displacement, and weakened protective systems. While 
protection response capacities continue to operate across Syria, the scale and severity of 
needs far exceed available services, resulting in persistent gaps in prevention, mitigation, 
and response.The analysis identifies child, early, and forced marriage as the most critical 
protection risk, alongside widespread psychological and emotional abuse, pervasive gender-
based violence, property-related violations, and child labor as a harmful coping mechanism.
These risks are interconnected and reinforced by structural drivers, including poverty, 
displacement, entrenched social norms, and limited access to legal, educational, and 
psychosocial services.
Children without parental care, women and girls, displaced and returnee families, and 
persons with disabilities face compounded vulnerabilities and significant barriers to accessing 
timely and appropriate support.Findings also underscore the normalization of violence and 
underreporting of protection violations, which continue to undermine accountability and

effective response. Despite the presence of community-based mechanisms and specialized 
services, gaps in coverage, accessibility, and sustainability constrain their impact, particularly 
for the most vulnerable population groups.
The evidence generated through this assessment provides a critical foundation for targeted, 
protection-centered programming grounded in the Protection Analytical Framework. 
Addressing identified risks will require integrated, multi-sectoral approaches that link 
protection with education, livelihoods, legal assistance, and mental health and psychosocial 
support, while strengthening community-based prevention and referral mechanisms. 
Sustained investment in protection risk monitoring, coordination, and inclusive service 
delivery will be essential to mitigate harm, reduce reliance on negative coping strategies, and 
support longer-term recovery and resilience in northwest Syria.
Beyond immediate protection risks, the findings point to a gradual normalization of harmful 
practices and coping mechanisms within affected communities, driven by prolonged crisis 
conditions, economic instability, and weakened institutional structures. This normalization 
reflects not only increased exposure to risk but also a shift in social perceptions, where certain 
forms of violence and exploitation are increasingly tolerated or perceived as necessary for 
survival. This trend has significant long-term implications for social cohesion, protection 
outcomes, and the effectiveness of future interventions.
The analysis further highlights that protection risks are not isolated phenomena but are 
deeply interconnected and reinforced by structural and systemic factors, including poverty, 
displacement, lack of access to services, and gaps in governance and accountability. As a 
result, single-sector responses are unlikely to achieve sustainable impact. Instead, addressing 
protection risks in northwest Syria requires integrated and multi-sectoral approaches that 
simultaneously tackle both immediate needs and underlying drivers of vulnerability.

In the longer term, strengthening local systems and community resilience will be critical. 
This includes investing in community-based protection mechanisms, enhancing the capacity 
of local actors, and improving coordination between humanitarian, development, and

Without sustained and coordinated efforts to address both the symptoms and root causes of 
protection risks, negative coping strategies are likely to persist, potentially leading to further 
deterioration of protection conditions and limiting prospects for recovery and durable 
solutions.
