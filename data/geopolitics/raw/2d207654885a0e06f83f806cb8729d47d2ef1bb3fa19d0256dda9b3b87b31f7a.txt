Insecurity Insight has released a new
 
report
 
on anticipatory action and risk mitigation for food insecurity in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, analysing how conflict-related violence continues to damage local food systems and undermine livelihoods long after individual incidents occur.
 The report covers the period from January 2023 to May 2026 and explores how humanitarian actors can move from reactive responses towards proactive risk mitigation.


Our latest report analyses:


465 incidents of food-related violence recorded across eastern DRC between January 2023 and May 2026


Violence affecting food production, transport, markets, livestock and humanitarian food assistance across conflict-affected communities


Geographic patterns of violence across eastern DRC, with incidents heavily concentrated in North Kivu and Ituri provinces


The cumulative and long-term impacts of attacks, displacement, extortion and restrictions on agricultural access on local food systems


Key findings:


Food-related violence was overwhelmingly concentrated in two provinces.
 North Kivu accounted for 55% of all recorded incidents and Ituri for 37%, together they represented 92% of all documented food-related violence during the monitoring period.


People, not infrastructure, bore the brunt of attacks.
 Nearly two-thirds of incidents (61%) involved violence against people engaged in food-related activities, including farmers working their fields, traders and marketgoers, and humanitarian workers delivering food assistance.


Livestock theft and destruction remained a major driver of insecurity.
 One quarter of all incidents involved the looting, killing or injuring of livestock, directly undermining household income, food availability and community resilience.


The most damaging impacts often unfold long after the violence itself.
 Landmines, displacement, abandoned fields and disrupted harvest cycles mean that even where incident numbers decline, food insecurity can continue to worsen months or years later.


Extortion and informal taxation have become structural pressures on food systems.
 Armed actors, including FARDC, Wazalendo, M23, Mayi Mayi militias and ADF, routinely imposed illegal payments, erected checkpoints and restricted access to agricultural land, creating persistent barriers to production and trade.


The report argues that humanitarian responses must shift from reacting to food crises after they emerge to anticipating predictable consequences of conflict violence and acting earlier to protect livelihoods, sustain local food systems and reduce humanitarian need.
