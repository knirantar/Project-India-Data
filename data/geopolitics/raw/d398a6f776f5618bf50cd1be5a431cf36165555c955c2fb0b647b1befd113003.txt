analytical framework. These outputs are then reviewed, validated, and refined by analysts to ensure accuracy, contextual relevance,
and operational utility. The workflow is organized into four key phases: planning and setup, ongoing operations, quality assurance, and

Summary
Myanmar has been in crisis since 1 February 2021, when the military (Tatmadaw) overthrew the democratically elected government.
This coup sparked widespread protests and armed resistance, leading to a severe political, economic, and humanitarian crisis. The
military regime, known as the State Administration Council (SAC), has responded with brutal force, resulting in thousands of civilian
deaths and widespread human rights abuses. As of March 2026, approximately 3.7 million people are internally displaced across the
country—a dramatic increase from roughly 300,000 pre-2021—with cross-border displacement projected to rise toward 5.2 million in
2026.
In attempt to seek international legitimacy, the military held general elections in three phases over December 2025 and January 2026
for the first time since the coup. The overwhelming majority of seats were won by the pro-military Union Solidarity and Development
Party (USDP) and saw SAC chairman Min Aung Hlaing elected as president in April 2026. The elections have been widely denounced as
neither free nor fair. Despite the transition to a quasi-civilian presidency, armed clashes have continued in multiple parts of the
country, including Sagaing, Magway, northern Shan, Kayah, Kayin, Kachin, Chin, and Rakhine. The Arakan Army (AA) now controls all
but three of Rakhine State's 17 townships, while resistance forces including the Karen National Liberation Army (KNLA) have
consolidated influence across parts of central and southeastern Myanmar. The UN's 2026 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan
(HNRP), released 10 December 2025, projects over 16 million people will require humanitarian assistance this year.
Recovery from the March 2025 earthquake remains slow, with an estimated 3.5 million tonnes of debris and reconstruction hampered
by insecurity, shortages of heavy machinery (43%) and labour (40%), and limited access. Many affected communities, particularly in
Magway, Sagaing, and Mandalay, continue to live in temporary shelters, while essential services remain disrupted. Approximately
24,200 houses were damaged (24% completely destroyed), and 132 health facilities were damaged—half in Mandalay.
The Rohingya crisis continues to worsen, with the junta's blockade of Rakhine preventing international aid delivery since late 2023,
leaving hundreds of thousands of IDPs with severely limited assistance. On 10 December 2025, a military airstrike destroyed Mrauk-U
General Hospital, killing at least 33 people and injuring 76-80—one of the deadliest attacks on healthcare in the conflict. Chronic
poverty now affects over 80% of the population; the World Bank projects GDP will contract 2.0% in FY2025/26 with inflation remaining
above 20%, and market disruptions have deepened food insecurity nationwide. The 2026 HNRP appeals for approximately $890 million
but reflects severely constrained funding and narrowed operational space, forcing agencies to prioritize only 2.6 million people with
the most severe needs for life-saving support.
Key Insights
1. Myanmar's humanitarian crisis continues to deepen amid persistent conflict, economic collapse, and the lingering impacts of the
March 2025 earthquake. An estimated 16 million people—nearly one in three citizens—are projected to require humanitarian assistance
in 2026, as conflict-driven displacement, natural disasters, funding shortages, and humanitarian access constraints exacerbate
vulnerabilities nationwide.
The 28 March 2025 earthquake remains a defining event in the humanitarian landscape. Official figures from SAC confirm 3,770 deaths
and over 5,000 injuries, with the Early Recovery Cluster reporting over 24,200 houses damaged (24% completely destroyed), 4,857
apartments affected, and 132 health facilities damaged. An estimated 3.5 million tonnes of debris blocks roads and markets, with
clearance impeded by shortages of heavy machinery (43%) and labour (40%). Nearly three-quarters of the most affected townships
continue to face critical shelter and livelihood gaps—64% of communities reported job losses and 30-31% reported damage to crops
and workplaces—while disrupted water systems (44% of groundwater sources contaminated) have fueled outbreaks of cholera and
dengue.
2. Following the junta-run general elections held over December 2025 and January 2026, as well as ceasefires between the regime and
some ethnic armed organizations in northern Shan, the number of armed conflict incidents has gradually decreased. Nonetheless, air
and ground offensives continue in the Central Dry Zone, as well as Rakhine, Chin, Kayah, Kayin, and Kachin States. The new
government's 100-day roadmap announced on 21 April 2026, which invites resistance groups to enter peace talks and to surrender,
have not been widely accepted. 
Between January and November 2025, the military conducted 2,165 airstrikes, up from 1,716 in all of 2024. On 10 December 2025, a
military airstrike destroyed Mrauk-U General Hospital in Rakhine State, killing at least 33 people and injuring 76-80—prompting UN,
WHO, and Amnesty International condemnation and calls for investigation as a possible war crime.
IDPs have reached approximately 3.7 million people as of March 2025, with projections indicating it could rise further through 2026. The
AA now controls all but three of Rakhine's 17 townships, while resistance forces have captured extensive border areas in the southeast.
Attacks on healthcare are widespread: Insecurity Insight recorded 1,815 attacks on health care between February 2021 and November
2025, with WHO verifying 67 attacks in 2025 alone. Access restrictions, including the junta's blockade of Rakhine since late 2023,
continue to endanger civilians—particularly Rohingya communities facing persistent protection risks, restricted mobility, and limited
access to aid.
3. Humanitarian access remains severely limited by insecurity and deliberate obstruction. The 2026 HNRP appeals for approximately
$890 million but reflects severely constrained funding and narrowed operational space, prioritizing only 2.6 million people with the
most severe needs at a cost of $521 million.

Operational Environment
Economy
Current Economic Situation
Conflict-driven disruptions and blockades have sharply reduced internal trade and cash circulation, contributing to rising commodity
prices and worsening living conditions in western and central areas including Rakhine and Magway [8][4]. Supplies from mainland
Myanmar have largely been cut off, with imports from Bangladesh and India only partially preventing critical shortages [8]. The junta
has also moved to regain control of key cross-border trade corridors (notably efforts to retake the Kalay–Tamu road) to restore
access and revenue from India-border trade routes [24][8]. Rising global prices and regional instability have pushed up fuel costs,
adding inflationary pressure to households and markets [18]. Agricultural output remains broadly favourable: dry-season rice planting
completed on about 1.1 million hectares (96% of the national plan), with ~750,000 hectares harvested producing 4.16 million tonnes of
paddy at ~5.5 t/ha; dry-season maize had 10,000 hectares harvested (64% of plan) with an estimated yield of 5.06 t/ha and projected
production of 50,000 tonnes; wet-season maize sowing is also underway. However, forecasts warn of elevated El Niño-related
below-normal rainfall risks that could affect production later in 2026 [18][30]. Humanitarian access constraints and funding shortfalls
limit assistance to displaced populations, compounding socio-economic hardship and weakening immediate shock-absorption
capacity in affected areas [4][13].
Conflict Economic Landscape
Myanmar's economic landscape remains deeply constrained by sustained conflict and large-scale displacement, with 15,371
conflict-related deaths in 2025, more than 3.5 million internally displaced, and over 1.5 million refugees abroad [9]. The junta is
advancing large infrastructure and extractive initiatives — including renewed activity on the Yadanabon Cyber City zone, and pledges
for the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Project and the India–Myanmar–Thailand Trilateral Highway — prompting accelerated land
clearances and rising land values [15][8][5]. Lands cleared through systematic village destruction in Rakhine are being repurposed for
infrastructure and natural resource extraction projects (pipelines, roads, rail links), bolstering military-controlled revenues, while
Rohingya-vacated lands are similarly redirected toward junta-linked commercial interests [5][15]. Recent evictions and
project-related land clearance (notably around Pyin Oo Lwin) have displaced traders, tenants and small businesses, undermining
local livelihoods while rents and property prices rise [15]. Explosive incidents and insecurity (for example the Namhkam
explosives-depot blast) have caused casualties and substantial rebuilding needs for homes and services in affected communities
[28]. Returns and reintegration trends are increasing but remain precarious, with UNHCR and global forced displacement reporting
noting returns to Myanmar in 2025–26 amid widespread infrastructure damage, limited services, high poverty and fragile livelihood
prospects, indicating urgent needs for investment in jobs, basic services and reconstruction [43][51]. Humanitarian and development
financing constraints — including large donor funding reductions and reports of unspent allocations — are limiting recovery,
reconstruction and service restoration at scale [13]. Ongoing regional diplomacy, including ASEAN ministerial visits such as Indonesia's
foreign minister to Naypyidaw on 8 June 2026, may open channels for political and economic re-engagement, but analysts warn such
bilateral engagement risks normalising the junta absent tangible progress on peace and implementation of the Five-Point Consensus
[10][7].
Stability of Currency and Bank System
Conflict-driven supply disruptions and blockades in Rakhine and other western areas have sharply reduced goods inflows and cash
circulation, contributing to rising commodity prices, local liquidity shortages and market dysfunction in conflict-affected areas [8][4].
Rising global prices and regional instability have further pushed up fuel costs, adding inflationary pressure to households and markets
[18]. Since early 2026, accelerated land clearance and redevelopment tied to the Yadanabon project and the junta's 100-Day Plan
have driven sharp increases in local property prices and rents around Pyin Oo Lwin and elsewhere in Mandalay Region [15].
Employment
Humanitarian and economic disruptions have sharply constrained livelihoods and job opportunities for displaced and other
vulnerable populations in Myanmar, with WFP assistance halted and domestic aid declining, undermining survival and income options
for many displaced households [1]. Key displacement figures illustrate the scale of livelihood disruption:
- Nationwide, more than 3.7 million people are displaced since the coup; Kachin State had 244,550 IDPs as of 1 June 2026 (an
increase of ~12,000 year-on-year) [1].
- Magway Region: over 44,000 people displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships since mid-May 2026; western Bago Region: more
than 25,000 displaced since late May 2026 [4].
- Myitche (Magway) crisis: over 19,100 IDPs after operations in May–June 2026, with nearly 40 civilians killed and ~1,000 homes
destroyed, severely disrupting local markets and livelihoods [3].
Shrinking cash circulation, rising commodity prices and disrupted supply routes (notably affecting Rakhine) have further reduced
market access and income opportunities for civilians in contested areas [8][3]. Attacks on health services have been extensive (1,877
reported attacks on health care 2016–2025), contributing to loss of jobs and heightened risks for health workers and health sector
employment continuity [46]. Rohingya communities have been subjected to unlawful conscription and large-scale destruction of
villages, removing local livelihoods while vacated land is repurposed for junta-linked infrastructure and resource projects that shift
economic opportunities away from affected communities [5].

Politics
Governance Structures and Rule of Law
The junta's National Solidarity and Peacemaking Negotiation Committee (NSPNC) met on 4 June to consider a Tai-Leng Nationalities
Development Party (TNDP) proposal to create a Shanni self-administered region encompassing Homalin, Hkamti, Kale and Tamu
townships, a move publicly opposed by the United Kuki State Council in a 7 June statement that cited historical injustices including a
1967 military campaign that burned over 40 Kuki villages and displaced more than 20,000 people [105]. Nationwide displacement and
constrained service delivery continue to undermine governance: Myanmar had about 3.6 million internally displaced persons at
end-2025, with Sagaing Region recording over 1 million displaced and Kachin State displacement rising, while domestic aid is declining
and some international assistance, including a WFP halt, has reduced humanitarian capacity [89][106]. The ASEAN Five-Point
Consensus (5PC) — cessation of violence, constructive dialogue, designation of an ASEAN special envoy, humanitarian assistance and
envoy visits — continues to frame regional diplomacy and is invoked by stakeholders advocating for people-centred political
roadmaps [83]. ASEAN engagement remains active but contested, with analysts warning that bilateral visits by member envoys risk
being perceived as legitimising the military leadership if not tied to measurable progress on cessation of violence and accountability
[69]. Regional information flows and research on Myanmar face constraints tied to broader security dynamics, exemplified by the
detention in China on 3 June of the Executive Director of the Institute for Strategy and Policy Myanmar (ISP-M), highlighting limits on
cross-border research and transparency [99].
Political Freedoms and Recent Laws
Since early June 2026, ASEAN engagement and domestic repression have continued following the disputed December 2025–January
2026 electoral process that led to Min Aung Hlaing being appointed president in April 2026 [69][96]. Human rights observers have
widely criticised the polls as a "sham," and Amnesty International researchers and other monitors interpreted recent releases as
reputation management rather than signs of substantive reform [69][96]. The Assistance Association for Political Prisoners (AAPP)
estimated about 22,064 pro-democracy activists and civilians remained detained even after an amnesty that freed roughly 4,000
people and saw Aung San Suu Kyi's sentence reduced and her allegedly moved to house arrest [96]. Since the 2021 coup, more than
30,000 political prisoners have been held, including over 6,400 women, according to the AAPP, and the UN has identified significant
evidence of systematic torture, killings and sexual and gender-based crimes in Myanmar's prisons [96]. Intensified military operations
have driven protection crises: Pakokku and Saw townships in Magway Region recorded more than 44,000 people displaced since
mid-May 2026, western Bago Region over 25,000 displaced, and airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killed nine civilians
and destroyed homes [87]. Overall reporting documents patterns of selective law application, ongoing arbitrary arrests and
large-scale detentions that continue to constrain freedoms of expression, assembly and association in Myanmar [96][83].

Security And Stability
Security Overview
Displacement continues to rise across Myanmar: more than 44,000 people were displaced in Magway Region's Pakokku and Saw
townships since midMay, and over 25,000 were displaced in western Bago Region since late May, while airstrikes between 27 May and
2 June reportedly killed nine civilians and destroyed homes across multiple villages; humanitarian response remains limited due to
access constraints and funding shortfalls [163]. Junta airstrikes have also hit a hospital and IDP camps in Kachin and Karenni States,
killing three civilians amid ongoing heavy fighting and displacement in those areas [171]. Fortify Rights announced on 9 June that its
investigation confirmed a mass killing from an airstrike and shootings in Yaetwinkone village (Bago Region) on 5 March that killed 28
civilians, including children and a pregnant woman, and identified involvement by Light Infantry Division 77 units (LIB 20, LIB 439) and
Infantry Battalion 264 [150][164]. The organisation further stated the monastery bombing and detention of civilians may amount to war
crimes and called on UN member states to pursue accountability and increased action against the junta [164][150].
Security Forces and Law Enforcement
Since May 2026, the junta has deployed thousands of troops to Magway Region—including major deployments to Saw and Mindon
townships and reinforcements of over a thousand troops in Mindon—aiming to disrupt resistance supply routes and triggering intense
clashes, repeated airstrikes, and destruction of villages [133]. The military now controls roughly 21% of the country amid a civil war that
international monitors estimate has caused over 96,000 deaths and displaced some 3.6 million people [131]. China and Russia
continue to supply arms to the military, sustaining its operational capacity [139][143]. The junta and some ethnic armed groups are
financed in part through an expanding illicit drug economy—estimated to have risen to about US$35 billion by 2024—which sustains
armed actors and complicates interdiction efforts [127]. Human rights organisations, UN-linked reporting, and NGOs document
systematic rights abuses by junta security forces, including torture and deaths in detention, widespread sexual violence used as a tool
of intimidation, and attacks on health facilities and civilians that may constitute war crimes [152][129][136][150]. Judicial and policing
processes display limited transparency in high-profile cases, with authorities frequently refusing to release information and restricting
access to court proceedings [141].
Demography
Demographic Overview
More than 3.5–3.7 million people are internally displaced in Myanmar, with Sagaing Region alone accounting for over 1 million
displaced, while over 1.5 million people have sought refuge abroad, primarily in Bangladesh, Malaysia and Thailand [184][192].
According to UNHCR's Global Trends 2025, an estimated 225,200 stateless Rohingya were internally displaced within Myanmar and
519,600 stateless people were reported in the country, while roughly 1.2 million Rohingya remained in Bangladesh at end2025 [182].
In Kachin State, UNHCR–Myanmar recorded 244,550 people displaced as of 1 June 2026, equivalent to nearly 15% of the state's
approximately 1.6 million population and about 12,000 higher than the previous year, while data up to December 2025 shows more
than 70,000 previously displaced people had returned home [192]. In Magway Region, more than 44,000 people were displaced in
Pakokku and Saw townships since midMay 2026 [185]. The Myitche crisis (10 May–5 June 2026) produced over 19,100 IDPs, with nearly
40 civilians killed and approximately 1,000 homes destroyed [186]. Repeated airstrikes and fighting around Mindat township forced
more than 17,000 residents to flee [195].

ALP, CNF, DKBA, KNU, KNU/KNLA-PC, PNLO, RCSS, NMSP, LDU, KIA, NDAA, MNDAA, SSPP/SSA, TNLA, AA, UWSA, ARSA, KNPP and numerous PDFs,

10 May — during which nearly 40 civilians were killed and approximately 1,000 homes burned [224], over 44,000 people displaced in
Pakokku and Saw townships (Magway) since mid-May amid continuing military operations and airstrikes [227], and more than 17,000
residents displaced in Mindat (southern Chin State) following repeated airstrikes and attacks [225].

Gender Dynamics
Reports document widespread and systematic sexual and gender-based crimes in Myanmar, with investigators noting persistent
impunity for these violations [221][245]. Over 900 documented cases of conflict-related sexual violence against Rohingya were
recorded between February 2021 and May 2025 [243]. Legal Action Worldwide (LAW) and related investigations concluded that
documented acts — including rape, torture, and political and gender-based persecution — constitute crimes against humanity and
war crimes in the post-coup context [221]. The AAPP reports that more than 6,400 women are among over 30,000 political prisoners
detained since the 2021 coup, with multiple accounts of sexual abuse and torture in detention [245]. Gender-based violence risks
remain elevated among displaced Myanmar populations and in host settings, while gender-based violence (GBV) prevention and
response services face reduced coverage due to access and funding constraints [234]. Civil society and women's rights networks —
including Rohingya Women's Network members who work with UNHCR — continue to document abuses and advocate for
accountability and protection measures [245][243].
Impact of Socio-Cultural Drivers on Aid
Myanmar accounts for the largest number of recorded incidents and arrests of health workers over the past decade, particularly
following the February 2021 military coup, with one mass series of arrests in Yangon involving more than 200 health workers [251].
Arrests were often carried out during raids on health facilities, at checkpoints, or at workers' homes, and detainees are overwhelmingly
national health workers [251]. With 1,877 incidents recorded in one dataset, Myanmar accounts for a leading share of attacks on
healthcare globally, exacerbating risks to women's access to health services and to health workers of all genders [251]. Independent
media and civil society actors face criminalisation, systematic suppression and an extensive exile context, constraining local
information channels and limiting direct donor engagement with grassroots actors [248]. Rohingya refugees continue to face
escalating hate and discrimination in host countries, with civil society in Malaysia warning of growing normalisation of hate speech
and calling for protection and accountability [262]. UNHCR reported that Myanmar was among the six countries receiving the largest
number of returns in 2025, alongside the DRC, Sudan, Syria, Afghanistan and Ukraine [263]. Analysts have cautioned that bilateral or
unilateral ASEAN engagement risks being perceived as legitimising Myanmar's military leadership unless accompanied by tangible
progress on the ground, a perception that could undermine regional coherence and complicate coordinated humanitarian
approaches [256].
Environment
Environmental Conditions
Parts of the Mekong sub-region, including Myanmar, experienced severe transboundary haze from land and forest fires, with impacts
peaking in March 2026 and posing elevated health risks for children, the elderly, and people with chronic conditions [278]. Fires also
degrade land capacity to absorb heavy rainfall, linking increased fire incidence to higher flood risk when rains return in the region
[278]. The Mekong sub-region's substantial reliance on hydropower — which includes Myanmar — raises transboundary
water-governance and sustainability concerns that can amplify environmental and livelihood risks when rainfall and river flows are
volatile [295]. On the coast, mangrove extent and closed-canopy mangroves in Myanmar have grown since the 2010s, aided by
post-Cyclone Nargis shifts in policy and a national logging ban enacted in 2016, enhancing coastal resilience, carbon sequestration
capacity, and protection against storm surge and erosion [303][307]. Regional public health risks linked to environmental factors —
including increased contamination and foodborne disease risks exacerbated by climate change — further heighten vulnerability
across Southeast Asia, with implications for Myanmar's population and food systems [290].
Environmental Challenges
Myanmar is highly vulnerable to the developing 2026–27 El Niño. GEOGLAM/NOAA outlooks forecast a rapid El Niño onset (82% chance
May–July 2026) and a high likelihood of a moderate-to-strong event by late 2026 into early 2027, with a ca. 65% chance of strong to
very strong conditions in October–December 2026 and a possible positive Indian Ocean Dipole reinforcing regional drying
[284][294][298]. Short disruptions to rainfall and cropping tied to El Niño can devastate largely rain-dependent farming — up to 85% of
farmland regionally is dependent on rainfall — increasing the likelihood of drought, fires, and erratic rainfall across the Mekong
sub-region [284][294]. In response, World Vision reported that in 2025 it trained tens of thousands of farmers across countries
including Myanmar on climate-smart agriculture and water management practices to build resilience to such shocks [284].
Displacement-related environmental pressures also affect WASH and waste systems: community-based waste management in Mae
La camp in Thailand processes over five metric tons of solid household waste daily, but systems face financial strain, ageing
infrastructure, and increased pressures from consolidation and relocations, with capacity-building and planned service handover
activities through 2026–March 2027 [293].

Environmental Risks and Mitigation
Seasonal and subseasonal forecasts issued in late May–June 2026 indicate below-average June rainfall in central Myanmar and likely
below-normal June–August rainfall in parts of the south, creating heightened drying and short-term drought risk, while models project
much hotter-than-normal temperatures across Myanmar through December 2026, increasing heat stress and evapotranspiration
pressures on water supplies and crops [298][294]. Despite those risks, May 2026 agricultural reporting showed slightly above-normal
pre-monsoon rainfall in key producing areas that supported dry-season rice planting (planting complete on about 1.1 million ha) and
a reported paddy harvest of about 4.16 million tonnes to date, indicating heterogeneous local conditions [283]. Past disaster impacts
underline compound risks: the March 2025 earthquake and associated floods and storms triggered large disaster displacements in
Myanmar (reported 405,600 new disaster displacements in 2025), and the country had an estimated 3.6 million IDPs at end-2025,
illustrating how environmental hazards interact with conflict to drive humanitarian need [311]. Adaptation and sustainable aid
measures being promoted in the region include climate-smart agriculture, farmer training, community rainwater harvesting,
micro-irrigation, and scaling renewable energy (notably solar) to reduce livelihoods' vulnerability to fuel shocks [284][278]. At the
regional level, ASEAN-oriented mitigation priorities stressed in 2026 include improved land management, fire monitoring and
firefighting capacity, and implementation of transboundary haze prevention frameworks to reduce fire risk and downstream flood
vulnerability [278].
Conflict
Overview of the Ongoing Armed Conflict
Since May 2026, fighting has intensified across western and northern Myanmar, with major active fronts in Magway (Saw, Mindon,
Pakokku), southern Chin and the corridor into Rakhine, as well as sustained clashes in Kachin, northern Shan and Karenni states
[342][357][346][360]. The junta has deployed large troop columns—reported in some areas as numbering from hundreds to over a
thousand—and conducted airstrikes and bombardments aimed at severing resistance supply routes and contesting key towns such
as Mindat, Matupi and Paletwa, while clashes and strikes were reported along routes between Shwegu and Ngar Oe [342][360].
Displacement and civilian impact have surged significantly. More than 44,000 people were displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships
since mid-May, over 25,000 displaced in western Bago since late May, and UNHCR-Myanmar reported approximately 244,550 people
displaced in Kachin State as of 1 June 2026, with daily clashes in Waingmaw, Bhamo and Hpakant and evacuation notices issued for
Shwegu on 8 June [338][357]. Air operations and bombardment between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killed nine civilians and
destroyed homes across multiple villages in western Bago, and separate junta airstrikes struck a hospital and IDP camps in Kachin and
Karenni States, killing at least three people and damaging civilian shelters and a village church [338][360]. An attack on Myitche
beginning 10 May forced over 19,100 IDPs, killed nearly 40 civilians (10 May–5 June), and destroyed approximately 1,000 homes across 18
villages [373].
A massive explosives-depot accidental blast in Namhkam township, northern Shan State on 31 May caused dozens of deaths—the
TNLA reported 43 killed and 112 injured while local rescue teams reported more than 60 killed—, left many homeless, and drew local
allegations of large munitions stockpiles and possible Chinese involvement in the supply or presence of Chinese nationals at the site
[346]. Armed group presence and weapons proliferation are evident across the theatre, with the Chin Brotherhood Alliance, Arakan
Army, Kachin Independence Army and multiple People's Defence Force units controlling or contesting territory; resistance analysts
report stockpiled munitions and efforts to disrupt logistics, and incidents involving large explosive stores have amplified protection
risks for civilians in populated areas [342][357][346]. Humanitarian access remains constrained by ongoing hostilities and funding
shortfalls, limiting response capacity to the rising displacement and protection needs across affected areas [338].
Conflict Stakeholders
The primary state actor is the Myanmar military junta (Tatmadaw) which is estimated to control roughly 21% of the country amid
continued civil war dynamics [350]. The broader human toll of the conflict is severe, with international monitors reporting more than
96,000 killed and at least 3.6 million internally displaced in Myanmar during the ongoing civil war [350][339].
Non-state and anti-junta actors include the National Unity Government (NUG) and its People's Defence Force (PDF) units, as well as
multiple ethnic armed organisations such as the Karen National Liberation Army (KNLA)/Karen National Union (KNU), Chin resistance
formations (Chin People's Union/Chin People's Army and Chin Brotherhood/Chin National Front), and the Arakan Army (AA), all of
which hold and contest significant territory [364][374][342][366]. Since mid-May, the junta has intensified offensive
operations—combining airstrikes, bombing runs, and large ground deployments—to retake western and central fronts including
Magway, Mindat/southern Chin, Myitche and parts of Bago, with reports of more than 2,000 troops deployed around Mindat and
repeated aerial bombardment of population centres [342][374][364].
International advocacy groups such as Fortify Rights have publicly characterised junta strikes on civilian sites—including a Bago
monastery—as potential war crimes, and have called on UN member states to support accountability measures and provide political,
economic and material assistance to the NUG and resistance administrations [364][366]. External diplomatic engagement is also
rising: ASEAN members have begun re-engagement with Naypyidaw, including an official Indonesian foreign minister visit on 8 June
2026 and planned outreach by the Philippines' ASEAN special envoy to meet ethnic armed groups, reflecting a regional recalibration
toward inclusive dialogue [329][330][362][333].
Peace Negotiations
ASEAN's 5PC remains the official framework for regional diplomacy on Myanmar, though progress against its conditions has been
scant and member states are reassessing engagement tactics rather than pursuing sustained isolation [330][329].

Hlaing in a reportedly "cordial, positive" atmosphere, signalling renewed bilateral engagement by some ASEAN members with the junta
and conveying Jakarta's support for an inclusive peace process under the 5PC [329][330]. The Philippines, as ASEAN chair in 2026, has

Conflict Outlook
Militarily, the junta has concentrated forces in Magway and intensified operations in southern Chin State—including repeated airstrikes
around Mindat—as it seeks to regain territory and secure key transport and supply corridors, while resistance groups continue to hold
large swathes of western Myanmar, increasing the likelihood of further offensives [342][374]. These operations have already driven
significant displacement, with more than 17,000 residents from Mindat and nearby villages fleeing to forests and other areas, and
Rakhine State reporting sharply reduced supplies from mainland Myanmar with rising prices and worsening living conditions
[374][342].
On the diplomatic front, concurrent external engagement—including India's infrastructure pledges and potential diplomatic openings
with ASEAN and China—may strengthen the junta's political standing or create incentives for further military action, complicating
prospects for a negotiated settlement [342][380]. Analysts caution that bilateral engagement by ASEAN members, if not tied to
verifiable progress on ceasefire and inclusive talks, risks exposing divisions within the bloc and further legitimising the junta [330][329].
Absent coordinated regional pressure and concrete incentives for ceasefires and inclusive negotiations, the outlook is for continued or
intensified hostilities in western Myanmar, with further displacement and worsening humanitarian conditions in the months ahead
[330][329][342][374].
Displacement
Displacement Overview
Displacement continues to rise across Myanmar amid ongoing military operations and airstrikes. Since mid-May 2026, more than
44,000 people were displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships (Magway Region), and intensified operations since late May displaced
over 25,000 in western Bago, with airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killing nine civilians and destroying homes [418]. In
southern Chin State, the offensive around Mindat has forced over 17,000 residents from the town and surrounding villages into forests,
neighbouring communities and bunkers [423]. Clashes and daily fighting in Waingmaw, Bhamo and Hpakant, and evacuation orders
affecting Shwegu township (notified on 8 June), are driving further displacement in Kachin State [416]. UNHCR-Myanmar reported
244,550 displaced people in Kachin State as of 1 June 2026, an increase of around 12,000 compared with June 2025; displacement in
Kachin is protracted — nearly 15 years since renewed fighting began in 2011 [416]. Nationwide, more than 3.7 million people have been
displaced since the 2021 coup, with Sagaing Region recording the highest figure at over 1 million [416]. According to UNHCR, Myanmar
was among six countries receiving large numbers of returns in 2025, with 415,200 returnees reported that year; UNHCR warned many
returns occurred under pressure and into areas with insecurity, damaged infrastructure and limited services [392]. In Kachin, over
70,000 previously displaced people had returned by December 2025, but many returned due to aid suspension, poor living conditions
and camp closures rather than improved security, creating risks of secondary displacement [416].
Living Conditions of the Displaced Population
Living conditions for displaced populations across Myanmar are dire and deteriorating. In southern Chin State, the offensive around
Mindat has forced over 17,000 residents into forests, neighbouring communities and bunkers, with displaced households reporting
rising living costs, food shortages and disrupted farming and market access [423]. Clashes and daily fighting in Waingmaw, Bhamo
and Hpakant, alongside evacuation orders affecting Shwegu township (notified on 8 June), continue to displace additional
populations in Kachin State, where nearly 244,550 people were already displaced as of 1 June 2026 [416]. Humanitarian response
across the country is severely constrained by access limitations and funding shortfalls; partners report limited assistance, WFP has
halted operations in some areas and domestic aid is declining, leaving large numbers of displaced people without adequate support
[418][416].
Drivers of Displacement
The primary driver of displacement in Myanmar is armed conflict. Intensified military operations and airstrikes since mid-May to early
June 2026 have driven new large-scale displacement across multiple regions [418]. Cumulatively, conflict has produced over 3.5
million IDPs and more than 1.5 million people who have sought refuge abroad [391]. Targeted persecution and systematic destruction
have compounded displacement, including documented attacks on Rohingya villages, widespread village clearance facilitating
military economic projects, at least 136 airstrikes that destroyed hospitals, markets and homes, and over 900 documented cases of
conflict-related sexual violence — driving ethnic-based displacement and longstanding statelessness among Rohingya communities
[409][411]. Structural drivers sustaining the conflict include illicit economies and armed-group financing, notably a sharp expansion of
Myanmar's drug economy that has increased revenue flows to ethnic armed organisations and the junta [405]. Natural disasters have
also contributed: the March 2025 earthquake plus floods and storms triggered approximately 405,600 new displacements in 2025, with

roughly 281,000 people remaining displaced at year end [389]. Returns and secondary displacement remain significant and
precarious: UN reporting recorded approximately 415,200 returns to Myanmar in 2025 amid ongoing insecurity, while in Kachin many
returns were driven by aid suspension, poor living conditions and camp closures rather than genuine improvements in safety,
complicating sustainable reintegration [404][416]. Displaced households in areas such as Mindat (southern Chin State) report rising
living costs, food shortages and disrupted farming and market access as secondary consequences of displacement [423].
Integration
Integration prospects for displaced populations both inside Myanmar and in neighbouring countries remain deeply constrained.
Humanitarian support gaps are widening as WFP has halted assistance in some areas and domestic aid is declining, making survival
increasingly difficult and limiting the conditions necessary for durable solutions [416][418]. UNHCR warned that returnees to Myanmar
in 2025 faced limited access to basic services, reconstruction needs and unemployment, undermining meaningful reintegration
[398][392]. In neighbouring Thailand, a government-led, time-bound transition is being advanced to adapt long-standing temporary
shelters and expand displaced Myanmar persons' access to legal employment, national services and education through measures
such as Thai-language instruction, curriculum alignment, bridging programmes and vocational pathways [400]. In Bangladesh, the
continued hosting of Rohingya populations strains local resources, and international funding reductions have increased the need for
investments in infrastructure, livelihoods and services to preserve social cohesion [426].
Humanitarian Access
Humanitarian Access Overview
Humanitarian operations in Myanmar face severe and compounding constraints. OCHA reported that displacement continued to rise
in early June 2026, with more than 44,000 people displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships (Magway Region) since mid-May and over
25,000 displaced in western Bago Region since late May; airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killed nine civilians and
destroyed homes across multiple villages [469]. Data compiled by ACLED showed 15,371 conflict-related deaths in Myanmar in 2025
and estimated more than 3.5 million IDPs and over 1.5 million refugees abroad, pressures that significantly affect operational reach
and access planning [468]. UN reporting and LAW documented widespread abuses, including sexual violence and torture by security
forces and non-state actors, which increase protection risks for civilians, impede survivors' access to services and justice, and
complicate humanitarian operations in affected areas [468]. The International Rescue Committee (IRC) noted logistics and
supply-chain disruptions have reduced deliveries of medicines to Sittwe, Rakhine State and forced agencies to reduce travel to project
sites, constraining last-mile engagement with affected communities; rising global logistics costs and reduced air-freight capacity
further delay shipments and raise operational expenses [453]. Myanmar is listed on the IRC's 2026 Emergency Watchlist as a country
at high risk of worsening humanitarian emergency, indicating elevated needs and constrained response capacity [453]. UN agencies,
international NGOs (including IRC), and national and local partners are the principal actors engaged in response, while cross-border
sheltering and transition efforts for Myanmar refugees involve the Royal Thai Government alongside UN and NGO partners in Thailand
[447][453].
Barriers to Humanitarian Access
Armed conflict, airstrikes, and deliberate attacks on civilian infrastructure constitute the most direct barriers to humanitarian access.
Between November 2025 and April 2026 there were 411 armed clashes and attacks that killed 288 people and included 136 airstrikes
across 12 townships that destroyed hospitals, markets, and displacement sites, degrading civilians' access to services [448]. In
Magway's Myitche area, local aid workers reported more than 19,100 IDPs after junta raids beginning 10 May, with nearly 40 civilians
killed and approximately 1,000 homes destroyed, leaving overcrowded camps facing critical food, water, and medical shortages [452].
Frontline operations in Chin State saw Thantlang heavily shelled and largely burned, leaving most residents displaced and
complicating access for responders [489]. Magway Region has also emerged as a key junta front as forces concentrate on securing
gateways and supply corridors into Rakhine, increasing operational constraints and risk to aid convoys trying to reach western areas
[491]. Attacks on healthcare have been pervasive: monitoring shows Myanmar accounts for the largest number of recorded incidents
and arrests of health workers since the 2021 coup, with health facilities and staff frequently targeted and detained, undermining the
health sector's ability to deliver services [435]. Nationwide, more than 3.7 million people have been displaced since the 2021 coup, with
Sagaing Region recording over 1 million displaced; the WFP has halted assistance in parts of the country and domestic aid is declining,
constraining survival prospects [451]. UNHCR-Myanmar reported 244,550 people displaced in Kachin State as of 1 June 2026, with daily
clashes, evacuation orders (Shwegu on 8 June), and returns driven by aid suspension and poor living conditions rather than improved
security [451]. At the diplomatic level, Indonesia's foreign minister visited Naypyidaw on 8 June 2026, a move analysts warned could
risk legitimising the military junta and complicate advocacy for restored rights and unfettered humanitarian access [475].
Humanitarian Access Gaps
Certain regions and population groups face the most acute gaps in humanitarian coverage. Kachin State ranks among the highest in
displaced numbers, with 244,550 displaced as of 1 June 2026, while Sagaing Region records the highest figure nationally at over 1
million [451]. Funding shortfalls and access constraints have left humanitarian response severely limited across these areas, with the
WFP having halted assistance in parts of Kachin and domestic aid continuing to decline [451]. Ethnic and administrative
disputes—such as opposition by the United Kuki State Council to proposed boundary changes in northern Sagaing—add localised
access risks by heightening tensions and potentially closing contested areas to aid actors [480]. ASEAN diplomatic engagement is
ongoing: the Philippine foreign minister has signalled plans to meet ethnic armed groups as ASEAN special envoy, which may influence
access dynamics if it leads to negotiated humanitarian channels or, conversely, to shifts in frontlines and control that further restrict
delivery [484][485].

Information, Communication and AAP
Information Landscape
Humanitarian information on Myanmar is heavily dependent on local and open source reporting. Local displacement committees,
community volunteers, and local aid workers are the primary sources for on-the-ground data, with the Mindat Township Displaced
Persons Management Committee reporting more than 17,000 people displaced from Mindat town and surrounding villages [530], and
local aid volunteers reporting over 19,100 IDPs in Myitche (Magway Region), nearly 40 civilians killed between 10 May and 5 June 2026,
and roughly 1,000 houses burned across affected villages [542]. Local and social media sources — including military-linked Telegram
channels — are used to share incident reports and imagery, which both supplements and complicates verification efforts [530][542].
Official transparency is limited: authorities frequently withhold information from the press and access to judicial proceedings is highly
restricted, constraining access to authoritative government data [522]. Monitoring and reporting reliability are further weakened by
declining humanitarian access and shrinking aid operations — WFP has halted assistance and domestic aid is falling — while open
source monitoring can diverge markedly from institutional reporting when access is constrained, complicating validation and
coverage [526][497].
Communication Channels
The main channels of information exchange include social media and messaging platforms (notably Telegram), open source digital
reporting, community-based structures such as Community Information Teams (CITs), Information Sharing Centres (ISCs) and camp
committees, mobile outreach and IEC materials, and local health workers who function as key eyewitness reporters [497][495][530].
Access and reliability of mobile and digital networks are uneven across Myanmar — health workers and relatively connected
communities have greater access, while many conflict-affected areas remain cut off, reducing institutional reporting coverage and
field presence [497][504]. Digital platforms and open source monitoring provide faster reporting and fill gaps left by restricted
institutional channels, but they also amplify misinformation and narratives that can criminalise aid or question the civilian character of
services [497]. Community meetings and refugee camp information structures support two-way communication and outreach,
including coordinated messaging and mobile outreach for Myanmar refugees in Thailand, yet active fighting, access constraints, and
funding shortfalls limit in-person reach and consistent service delivery in affected townships in Myanmar [495][520][530]. State- and
military-linked channels have been used to disseminate propaganda and contested casualty claims, undermining trust and
complicating verification of timely and accurate information [530][497].
Barriers to Information
Displacement has surged since mid-May — over 44,000 people were displaced in Magway's Pakokku and Saw townships and more
than 25,000 in western Bago since late May — and the humanitarian response remains limited by access constraints and funding
shortfalls, reducing community access to information and services [520]. Escalating airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly
killed nine civilians and destroyed homes across multiple villages, further disrupting local information channels and outreach networks
[520]. The IRC reported that fuel, shipping, and logistics shocks have disrupted supply chains in Myanmar, causing regular medicine
shortages in Sittwe and reducing the frequency of travel to project sites, which diminishes in-person contact and community outreach
[504]. Authoritarian pressure, restrictive laws, and censorship have narrowed the information space for independent media and exiled
Myanmar actors, constraining reporting and media development efforts [514]. Local communities have been left unaware of
hazardous activities — for example, residents reportedly were not informed about explosives stored near residential areas after the
Namhkam depot blast — illustrating critical local information gaps [536]. Attacks on education and information blackouts contribute
to under-reporting and hamper civilians' ability to obtain reliable information about aid, rights, and safety [515].
Misinformation and Rumors
Social media dynamics — where a small number of highly active users, coordinated accounts, and automated amplification shape
commentary — drive the spread and perceived consensus of false or misleading narratives that reshape public perceptions of
healthcare and response activities [497]. Harmful information in this context includes outright false claims as well as insinuations that
cast doubt on the neutrality or legitimacy of health services and health workers, undermining trust in care and complicating outbreak
or emergency responses [497]. Misinformation about Myanmar-related populations — notably the Rohingya — is being manufactured
and amplified on social media in neighbouring countries, fuelling hate narratives, doxxing, and threats against community leaders and
refugees, which civil society actors have called out and sought to counter [541][528]. Media development actors note that
authoritarian pressure and suppression of independent journalism in Myanmar weaken credible local information channels,
prompting increased investment in fact-checking, journalism ethics, digital safety, and targeted training for content creators to
reduce misinformation risks [514]. Efforts to counter these trends combine community-level coordinated communications through
trusted structures such as CITs, ISCs, and Camp Committees — emphasising coordinated messaging, mobile outreach, IEC materials,
and two-way feedback — alongside fact-checking and media literacy initiatives, though gaps remain in consistency, reach, and
protection of vulnerable groups [495][514][497].
Accountability Needs and Transparency
Kachin displacement rose to 244,550 as of 1 June 2026 (an increase of approximately 12,000 year-on-year), while WFP has halted
assistance and domestic aid is declining, exacerbating gaps in service delivery and feedback mechanisms for displaced communities
[526]. Fear of reprisals and stigmatisation discourages victims from filing complaints and reporting abuses, contributing to severe
underreporting of sexual violence and other violations and limiting access to justice [516][505]. Attacks on healthcare have been
widespread, with the Myanmar Armed Forces identified among the state actors most linked to such incidents, prompting calls for
strengthened accountability including ICC investigations and enhanced UN reporting [497]. Opaque judicial processes, restricted
press access, and shrinking civic space impede transparency and complaint mechanisms, with limited official information available
on investigations or court proceedings [522][514].

Legal and Policy
Legal and Policy Framework
International humanitarian law (IHL) requires parties to distinguish and protect medical services and civilians, binding both states and
non-state actors under customary IHL; a recent review recommends the ICC prioritise investigations of attacks on healthcare and
urges the UN to strengthen reporting and accountability mechanisms for such attacks [570]. International prosecutions and remedies
remain active: legal actions concerning atrocity crimes against the Rohingya are being pursued before the ICC and in other
jurisdictions, including Argentina and Timor-Leste, while advocates continue to reference the ICJ's 2020 provisional measures as part
of the accountability framework [572]. UN reporting and human rights investigations have documented widespread use of sexual
violence in the post-coup conflict, noting pervasive impunity and findings by LAW that documented acts may constitute war crimes
and crimes against humanity [573]. International arms-transfer norms and UN/ATT obligations have been invoked in debates over
responsibility for munitions flows and storage in Myanmar, with analysts arguing supplier states may bear legal responsibility where
transfers contribute to civilian harm [579].
ASEAN's 5PC (2021) continues to serve as the region's primary political framework, calling for immediate cessation of violence, dialogue
among all parties, appointment of a special envoy, humanitarian assistance, and a visit by the special envoy [574][588]. Recent
diplomatic activity includes Indonesia's foreign minister visiting Naypyidaw on 8 June 2026, Thailand and Malaysia conducting visits in
April–May 2026, and the Philippines designating Ma. Theresa Lazaro as ASEAN Special Envoy to Myanmar — signalling a shift toward
broader, more inclusive engagement — with ASEAN foreign ministers agreeing to further contacts and planning to discuss terms of
reference for a permanent special envoy at the July foreign ministers' meeting [595][574][588][592]. An investigation by Fortify Rights
published on 9 June 2026 confirmed an airstrike and shootings on 5 March in Yaetwinkone village, Bago Region, that killed 28 civilians
and urged UN member states to pursue accountability, stating the attack may amount to war crimes [585][594].
Humanitarian Impact of Legislation
Counterterrorism laws have been weaponised against health workers and humanitarian personnel: two doctors in Mandalay were
arrested and later sentenced under counterterrorism charges, clinics were sealed and confiscated, and these cases form part of a
broader pattern of health worker arrests recorded since 2016 [570]. The opacity of the justice system since the 2021 coup further limits
transparency around prosecutions and detentions, increasing legal risks for humanitarian and foreign staff operating in Myanmar
[586][608].
Displacement and constrained humanitarian access have intensified markedly. In Magway Region, more than 44,000 people were
displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships since mid-May 2026, with over 19,100 IDPs in Myitche alone requiring urgent food and medical
aid following operations from 10 May–5 June that killed nearly 40 civilians and destroyed around 1,000 homes; overcrowded camps
face outbreaks of diarrhoea and critical supply shortfalls [581][577]. Intensified operations in western Bago since late May displaced
over 25,000 people, with airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killing nine civilians [581]. UNHCR reported 244,550 displaced
people in Kachin State as of 1 June 2026 [583][581]. Nationwide displacement has exceeded 3.7 million in the five years since the 2021
coup [583].
Humanitarian response is hampered by funding shortfalls and access constraints: WFP has halted assistance in some areas, domestic
aid is declining, and donors' cuts combined with rising logistics costs have reduced delivery of medicines and forced agencies to scale
back health services [583][612]. UNHCR reported approximately 415,100 returns to Myanmar in 2025, yet many returnees face insecurity,
destruction, and limited access to services and documentation — raising acute needs for legal identity, property, and civil
documentation to enable access to rights and services [584]. ASEAN engagement and bilateral visits to Naypyidaw in April–May 2026
are reshaping the regional approach, with implications for humanitarian access and the implementation of the 5PC as members
weigh reintegration against pressure on the junta [595][588].
International Agreements Impacting the Crisis
ASEAN's 5PC remains the primary regional framework guiding engagement with Myanmar, but member states report minimal
progress on its conditions and debate has intensified over whether engagement or isolation will advance peace [574][588]. Several
ASEAN members resumed high-level contacts with Naypyidaw in mid-2026, prompting debate that bilateral engagement risks
legitimising the junta unless tied to tangible progress on the 5PC [588][595]. The Philippines' newly designated ASEAN Special Envoy
plans to meet ethnic armed organisations, signalling a shift toward broader, potentially more inclusive engagement under the 5PC
framework [574].
International accountability efforts continue in parallel: the junta faces ICC investigations and legal actions in other jurisdictions, while
the regime has reportedly sought to apply diplomatic pressure to halt universal-jurisdiction cases [572]. Human rights and
accountability NGOs have called for stronger international measures, including political and financial support for the NUG and
increased pressure on the junta for alleged attacks on civilians, reinforcing the gap between diplomatic engagement and
accountability actions [585].
Humanitarian coordination under these frameworks includes UNHCR and UN agencies engaging with the Royal Thai Government and
partners to transition protracted Myanmar refugee arrangements in Thailand toward development-oriented, government-linked
solutions, with World Bank/IFC partnerships cited in planning for sustainable responses [571][578].

However, OCHA and regional humanitarian updates in June 2026 reported rising displacement, limited access for aid delivery, and

Limitations of Current Policies
ASEAN engagement is fragmented and risks conferring legitimacy on the junta as individual member states pursue bilateral contacts
despite limited progress on the 5PC; the December 2025–January 2026 elections were widely criticised, and Min Aung Hlaing became
president in April 2026, prompting debate over reintegration strategies [588][595]. Inclusion of resistance actors in dialogue remains
uneven and ad hoc: while the Philippines' ASEAN Special Envoy has signalled intent to engage ethnic armed organisations, this shift
has yet to be systematically embedded in regional diplomacy [574][597][596].
Domestically, opaque justice processes, widespread detention of political opponents, and documented patterns of torture and
conflict-related sexual violence undermine the rule of law, with rights investigators concluding some abuses amount to crimes against
humanity [603][573]. At the international level, accountability avenues including the ICC and universal jurisdiction cases remain
active, yet the junta has actively sought diplomatic pressure to resist prosecution efforts, weakening enforcement of international
obligations [572].
Humanitarian access is severely constrained by ongoing military operations, airstrikes, operational bottlenecks, and funding shortfalls,
and key agencies have curtailed assistance in some areas [581][583]. Repeated airstrikes and mass killings — including the 5 March
attack on Yaetwinkone monastery that killed 28 civilians — exemplify the failure to protect civilians and the absence of effective
domestic enforcement of IHL, prompting calls from Fortify Rights and others for international action [585][594][572]. These combined
gaps — fragmented regional policy, exclusionary dialogue processes, opaque judicial mechanisms, impunity for attacks on civilians,
and constrained humanitarian access — reduce policy adaptability and hinder effective protection and relief delivery in Myanmar

Food Security and Livelihoods
Access to Food and Market Functionality
Intense military operations, airstrikes, and offensives have disrupted farming, supply routes, and market functioning across Magway,
Myitche (Magway Region), southern Chin (Mindat), and western Bago, reducing local food availability and raising prices
[668][651][680][659]. Myitche's central market was torched during operations and roughly 1,000 houses were burned across affected
villages, sharply constraining local food supply [651]. In Rakhine State, supplies from mainland Myanmar have largely stopped entering
key routes, contributing to rising commodity prices despite some mitigation from imports via Bangladesh and India [659]. Ongoing
insecurity has disrupted cultivation and market access for rural households, reducing own production and purchasing power and
worsening food affordability in conflict-affected markets [680][659][651].
Key displacement figures driving acute food access needs include:
- More than 44,000 people displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships since mid-May (Magway) [668].
- Over 25,000 people displaced in western Bago since late May [668].
- At least 19,100 IDPs in Myitche (Magway) urgently requiring emergency food and medical aid, with 12 displacement sites
consuming roughly 40 bags of rice and about 36 viss (≈58.6 kg) of cooking oil daily [651].
- Mindat Township displacement exceeded 17,000 residents who fled to forests and neighbouring areas following repeated
airstrikes [680].
Disease outbreaks (diarrhoea) have been reported in overcrowded displacement camps, compounding food and health needs [651].
Coping Mechanisms
Displacement-driven coping is acute across multiple regions. In Kachin State, UNHCR-Myanmar reported 244,550 people displaced as
of 1 June 2026 (up approximately 12,000 from the prior year), and noted that over 70,000 returns through December were driven by aid
suspension, poor living conditions, camp closures, and scarce jobs rather than improved security — indicating negative coping
through premature returns and livelihood-related pressures [697]. Among returnees more broadly, only 60% reported having an
income source, and more than four in five returnee households reported skipping at least one meal a day, reflecting severely reduced
purchasing power [650]. Local responders in Myitche noted that displacement sites have reached a critical breaking point in
emergency food stocks, with populations relying entirely on external assistance as supplies run low [651].
Livelihood Disruption
The IRC included Myanmar among the top 10 countries at greatest risk of new or worsening humanitarian emergencies for 2026,
signalling heightened risk to livelihoods and household economic stability [638]. UNHCR reported that many returnees — including
those returning to Myanmar — returned to difficult conditions marked by insecurity, widespread destruction, weak economic
conditions, and limited access to services and jobs [661]. Reported natural-resource recovery, including mangrove regrowth in
Myanmar, may support coastal and fisheries livelihoods, though sources do not quantify employment or income impacts [705].
Impact on Agricultural Activities and Production
Dry-season crop harvesting in Myanmar is reported as wrapping up [660]. The developing 2026–27 El Niño is forecast to increase the
likelihood of below-normal rainfall in parts of Southeast Asia, raising risks of delayed planting and reduced yields during the
July–September period and beyond [648][644]. Rising fuel and fertilizer prices are further constraining agricultural and fisheries
activity: fuel costs have grounded many small-scale fishing boats and reduced fish supply (increasing fishmeal and
poultry/aquaculture costs), while global fertilizer disruptions are driving up input costs and prompting farmers to cut fertilizer use,
risking lower yields [637][638]. To strengthen resilience, World Vision reported that in 2025 tens of thousands of farmers — including in
Myanmar — received training on climate-smart agriculture, integrated nutrient management, and community water-management
measures such as rainwater harvesting and micro-irrigation [637].
Food and Livelihood Needs and Current Response
Humanitarian assistance in Myanmar is severely constrained by both access limitations and funding shortfalls. The World Food
Programme has halted assistance in parts of the country and domestic aid is declining, leaving large numbers of displaced people
without adequate food assistance [697][668]. Local responders report that emergency food stocks in displacement sites have
reached a critical breaking point and that basic food supplies are urgently needed to prevent further deterioration of food security
[651][668]. Civil society and rights groups are calling for cross-border humanitarian assistance to reach affected populations given
limited in-country delivery capacity and shrinking aid flows [697]. The 2–8 June regional snapshot documents that access constraints
and funding shortfalls are leaving large numbers of displaced people without adequate assistance [668]. Airstrikes between 27 May
and 2 June reportedly killed nine civilians and destroyed homes across Pakokku, Saw, and western Bago, further compounding needs
in areas where humanitarian reach remains limited [668].

Access to Education and Barriers
Conflict-driven displacement and attacks are major barriers to school access in Myanmar. Nationwide displacement since the 2021
coup exceeds 3.7 million, with Sagaing Region alone recording over 1 million displaced, aggravating disruption to schooling and returns
under precarious conditions [734]. UNHCR-Myanmar reported 244,550 people displaced in Kachin State as of 1 June 2026, an increase
of about 12,000 over the past year [734]. Since mid-May 2026, more than 44,000 people were displaced in Magway's Pakokku and Saw
townships and over 25,000 in western Bago following intensified operations and airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June that reportedly
killed nine civilians and destroyed homes [721]. In Rakhine/Arakan, 411 armed clashes between November 2025 and April 2026 included
136 airstrikes across 12 townships that destroyed hospitals, markets, IDP camps, and homes, further degrading education infrastructure
and access for Rohingya and other conflict-affected communities [751]. Global monitoring documented a surge in attacks on
education in 2024–2025 (attacks on schools up 64%), identifying Myanmar among the countries with the highest numbers of students,
teachers, and education personnel killed or injured in such incidents, undermining safe access to learning [724][718]. The UN has
identified attacks on schools as grave violations affecting children and noted that government forces were primarily responsible for
certain violations, including attacks on schools and hospitals, which has further disrupted learning and access to safe schooling [723].
Humanitarian response and education continuity are constrained by access limitations and funding shortfalls, leaving large numbers
of displaced children without adequate assistance or learning continuity [721][734].
Education Needs and Current Response
Displacement across Myanmar remains high—more than 3.5 million internally displaced and over 1.5 million people have fled
abroad—intensifying education needs and disruption for children and communities [723]. The World Food Programme has halted
assistance in some areas, exacerbating gaps in services for displaced children [734].

Availability and Access to Healthcare Services
Displacement and active hostilities have severely restricted access to healthcare across Myanmar. OCHA reported that displacement
rose sharply in early June 2026, with over 44,000 people displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships (Magway) since mid-May and over
25,000 in western Bago since late May, as airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June killed civilians and destroyed homes, further restricting
access to care [773]. In Myitche (Magway), more than 19,100 IDPs urgently need food and medical aid following junta operations
beginning 10 May, with severe overcrowding, lack of clean drinking water, and diarrhoea outbreaks across 12 displacement camps
requiring immediate medical response [776]. UNHCR reported approximately 244,550 people displaced in Kachin State as of 1 June
2026, with continued daily clashes and evacuation orders undermining service continuity and prompting returns driven by aid
suspension and poor living conditions [785]. The IRC noted that fuel and logistics crises have disrupted medical supply chains and
last-mile delivery, contributing to regular medicine shortages in Sittwe and delayed pharmaceutical deliveries [771]. OCHA and
humanitarian partners highlight that access constraints and donor funding shortfalls are limiting outreach, referrals, and the
frequency of in-person service delivery to displaced populations [773][771].
Disease Outbreaks
Overcrowded displacement sites and a critical lack of clean drinking water in Myitche, Magway Region have produced reported
diarrhoeal outbreaks among the more than 19,100 IDPs displaced since 10 May operations [776]. The downscaling of humanitarian
outreach and the transition of health services for some shelters into Ministry of Public Health district hospitals — which are absorbing
increased patient loads without matching budget allocations and with incomplete insurance coverage — has reduced vaccination
coverage and preventive maternal and child health services among affected populations [769]. Violence, community mistrust, and
attacks on health programmes are known drivers of interrupted vaccination campaigns and resulting immunity gaps in conflict
settings, increasing the risk of future outbreaks [770]. Access constraints and funding shortfalls continue to limit humanitarian health
and WASH responses needed to contain outbreak risks across displacement-affected areas [773].
Health System Challenges
Myanmar's health system faces compounding structural and conflict-driven challenges. Attacks and airstrikes have damaged health
infrastructure extensively, with 136 airstrikes recorded between November 2025 and April 2026 reported to have destroyed hospitals,
markets, and displaced persons camps [800]. In Myitche (Magway), junta attacks from 10 May to 5 June caused nearly 40 civilian
deaths, burned approximately 1,000 houses, and created overcrowded displacement sites where diarrhoea outbreaks and critical
water shortages were reported [776]. Attacks on health infrastructure and personnel have repeatedly disrupted maternal health care,
particularly affecting maternity wards and obstetric services [770]. Regular medicine shortages and disrupted deliveries have been
reported in Sittwe, Rakhine State, where IRC noted that fuel and logistics constraints are restricting movement and the timely delivery
of medicines, undermining continuity of care [771]. Humanitarian assistance has been further curtailed in some areas, with partners
reporting aid reductions and suspension of WFP assistance, reducing support available to displaced communities and increasing risks
of untreated illness and disease outbreaks [785]. Insecurity Insight's monitoring recorded 1,877 reported attacks on healthcare in
Myanmar between January 2016 and December 2025, reflecting the sustained and systemic nature of threats to health service delivery

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS)
Psychosocial needs among refugees, returnees, and internally displaced persons in Myanmar are substantial, while access to mental

Health Needs and Current Response
Displacement and active hostilities have driven urgent needs for emergency trauma care, primary healthcare, WASH, and
maternal-child services across multiple regions of Myanmar. In Rakhine State, intensified military airstrikes around Kyaukphyu and
nearby townships in early June 2026 injured displaced civilians and caused deaths from a drone strike on 5 June, increasing demand

approximately 12,000 year-on-year — while WFP has halted assistance and domestic aid is declining, exacerbating gaps in healthcare
and survival services for displaced populations [785]. Humanitarian health coverage remains limited in geographic reach and scale:
outreach, vaccination, maternal-child, and emergency services are constrained by access restrictions and funding shortfalls,

Impact on civilians
Displacement continued to rise sharply across Myanmar amid intensified military operations and airstrikes in mid-2026. More than
44,000 people were displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships (Magway Region) since mid-May, and over 25,000 were displaced in
western Bago Region since late May, with airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June reportedly killing nine civilians and destroying homes
[992]. In Myitche town (Magway), following a 10 May raid, some 19,100 IDPs were sheltering across 12 displacement sites by early June,
with nearly 40 civilians killed between 10 May and 5 June, approximately 1,000 houses torched, and critical food shortages,

forests and neighbouring areas as repeated airstrikes and village burnings forced people into bunkers and disrupted farming and

Waingmaw, Bhamo, and Hpakant, and authorities notifying residents of Shwegu Township to evacuate on 8 June [986]. Nationwide
displacement since the 2021 coup exceeds 3.7 million people, with Sagaing reporting over 1 million displaced [986].
Attacks on civilian infrastructure have caused significant harm. On 4 June, junta airstrikes hit a hospital in Si Thar (Shwegu Township,
Kachin State) and IDP camps in Demoso (Karenni State), killing at least three civilians, injuring five, and damaging hospital
infrastructure after four bombs were dropped on the compound [982]. On 9 June, Fortify Rights confirmed a mass killing in

early June injured civilians as fighting centred on the Taung Maw Oo naval base [1010]. Humanitarian response remains constrained by
access restrictions and funding shortfalls, leaving many displaced populations without adequate assistance or services [992][988].
Factors Driving protection risks
Since mid-May and into early June 2026, intensified military operations, airstrikes, and targeted attacks by the junta and other armed
actors have driven large-scale displacement, civilian deaths, and destruction of homes and markets [992][1010][1013][988]. Attacks on
civilian infrastructure and health services — including hospital destruction and arrests of health workers — have increased protection
risks and prompted calls for accountability [1013][1024][983]. The growing use of explosive weapons, drone strikes, and widespread
unexploded ordnance and ERW has caused civilian casualties, hampered access to farmland and services, and increased long-term
contamination risks [1020][983][1026].
Arbitrary arrests, enforced disappearances, torture, and documented sexual violence in detention — including systemic abuse of
female political prisoners — remain widespread, with thousands held as political detainees since the coup [991]. Denial of legal
protection and statelessness (notably of the Rohingya), reports of unlawful conscription, and attempts to exploit cleared lands for
commercial projects further compound protection vulnerabilities for minority and displaced populations [989][984]. Economic
deprivation driven by blockades, disrupted supply routes, and livelihood loss — notably in Rakhine and conflict-affected agricultural
areas — is forcing risky livelihood behaviours and increasing vulnerability to exploitation and trafficking [994][988]. Humanitarian
access constraints and funding shortfalls have limited assistance at scale, weakening protection services and response capacity in
affected areas [992][1006].

confirmed investigation into the 5 March 2026 airstrike and ground assault on Yaetwinkone village documented detentions, shootings,
and survivor accounts of severe physical and psychological harm among those who witnessed or survived the attack, which killed 28
civilians [1013][1024].

Violence against women and girls
Reports document systemic sexual violence by Myanmar security forces and in detention — including invasive strip-searches, sexual
humiliation, threats of rape, and other forms of torture — with survivors and former detainees describing a patterned use of sexual
violence as a tool of intimidation, based on interviews and investigations conducted since late 2025 [991]. The Assistance Association
for Political Prisoners (AAPP) and Fortify Rights report more than 6,400 women among thousands of political detainees, with around 40
female political prisoners reportedly having died in detention due to torture and medical neglect [991]. Conflict-related sexual violence
disproportionately impacts Rohingya women and children, with over 900 documented cases attributed to the junta from February 2021
to May 2025 [989]. The UN has named Myanmar among contexts of particular concern for conflict-related sexual violence and has
warned that recent funding cuts have reduced its capacity to provide care to survivors [980].
Attacks on health care and maternal services have been frequently reported in Myanmar, with maternity, obstetrics, and midwifery
services repeatedly affected, undermining survivors' access to medical, reproductive, and GBV-related care [983]. Survivors face
major barriers to reporting and accessing justice, including lack of legal aid, long distances to competent authorities, security risks,
fear of reprisals, and stigma [980]. Ongoing displacement and continued hostilities further exacerbate vulnerabilities to gender-based
violence among affected civilian populations [992].

attacks on education and the military use of schools, further amplify risks of child recruitment and exploitation in affected areas
[1061][1077]. Overall, elevated protection risks for unaccompanied and separated children persist amid limited registration and
family-tracing coverage, strained case-management and psychosocial services, and funding constraints [1055][1061][1086].
Pregnant and Lactating Women
Conflict-related violence has directly and repeatedly undermined maternal and reproductive healthcare in Myanmar. On 5 March
2026, an airstrike and ground assault on Yaetwinkone (Bago Region) killed 28 civilians, including a pregnant woman, and destroyed a
monastery sheltering civilians [1076][1089]. On 4 June 2026, military airstrikes struck a rural hospital in Si Thar (Shwegu Township,
Kachin State) and multiple IDP camps in western Demoso (Karenni State), killing at least three civilians, injuring others, and damaging
hospital buildings and dispensaries, further undermining local access to reproductive and maternal care [1063]. Insecurity Insight
monitoring shows Myanmar has sustained high levels of violence affecting maternal health services, with 53 post-2022 incidents
recorded involving the repeated targeting or disruption of maternity wards, obstetrics services and midwives [1103]. Humanitarian
access constraints, checkpoints, curfews and funding shortfalls are limiting women's ability to reach timely prenatal, delivery and
emergency obstetric care, with some women and clinicians reporting that deliveries—including C-sections—are being scheduled to
avoid travelling during active bombardment [1061][1103]. Surging displacement has compounded these pressures: more than 19,100
IDPs in Myitche (Magway) since 10 May face critical shortages of food and clean water and outbreaks of diarrhoea, creating urgent
need for medical assistance including maternal care [1060]. Conflict-related sexual and gender-based violence remains widespread,
with UN and civil society reporting documenting sexual violence by state and non-state actors and severe abuses of women in
detention that undermine survivors' access to specialised protection and health services [1056][1075][1080]. Rohingya women and
children have been disproportionately affected, with over 900 documented cases of conflict-related sexual violence attributed to junta
forces from February 2021 to May 2025, further compounding protection and maternal health needs among displaced populations
[1065].
Adolescents and Youth
Displacement and ongoing hostilities have sharply disrupted young people's lives and access to education. Attacks on education and
the military use of schools have risen, contributing to school disruption, increased risk of child recruitment and gender-based violence
in affected areas; Myanmar is among the contexts with the highest numbers of education-related casualties and incidents
documented in recent global monitoring [1077]. Rohingya individuals have been subjected to unlawful conscription by both the
Myanmar junta and the Arakan Army, creating direct recruitment and protection risks for youth from that community [1065].
Humanitarian response and services for young people—including psychosocial support—remain constrained by access limitations
and funding shortfalls, leaving many displaced children and adolescents without adequate assistance [1061]. Global monitoring notes
that over two-fifths of refugees and IDP returnees report stress-related symptoms, underscoring likely unmet mental health needs
among displaced youth from Myanmar [1067]. Civil society and youth networks in the region are active in advocacy and protection
campaigns, with youth forums among endorsers calling for rights and accountability for affected communities including the Rohingya
[1065].
People with Disabilities
The UN Secretary-General has warned of the extraordinary difficulties facing people with disabilities in armed conflict contexts,
including Myanmar [1056]. Myanmar is among countries with exceptionally high levels of attacks on health care, undermining
availability and access to essential health services for vulnerable groups including those with disabilities [1103]. Local reports from
recent junta raids near Bagan documented that many victims were elderly or disabled, underscoring acute protection risks for people
with disabilities in conflict-affected areas [1108]. In Rakhine State (Sittwe), humanitarian actors have reported regular shortages and
delivery delays of medicines, constraining access to health care for people reliant on humanitarian assistance [1086].
Elderly
Older people face worsening access to healthcare and medicines as health facilities are attacked, logistics and supply chains are
disrupted, and humanitarian access and funding shrink [1103][1086][1061]. In Mindat Township (Chin State), intensified operations and
airstrikes have driven more than 17,000 residents to flee by June 2026; local records show 766 deaths between 2021 and end-2024,
most linked to medicine shortages and untreated chronic illnesses, underscoring older people's elevated risk from healthcare
disruption [1068]. Reporting from villages raided near Bagan noted that many of the dozens of victims were elderly or disabled,
highlighting direct exposure of older people to violence and exclusion from protection during operations [1108]. In Magway (Myitche),
junta raids since 10 May caused mass displacement, leaving over 19,100 IDPs in overcrowded camps and facing diarrhoeal outbreaks
and critical shortages of food and medical aid—conditions that disproportionately affect older adults [1060][1061]. Airstrikes on 4 June
struck a hospital in Si Thar (Kachin State) and IDP camps in Karenni State, killing at least three civilians and damaging health
infrastructure that older patients depend on for treatment and referrals [1063]. Rising logistics and fuel costs have disrupted medicine
deliveries and reduced outreach to remote areas, further constraining mobility support, referrals and access to medications for older
patients across affected areas [1086].
Displaced
Displacement across Myanmar continues to rise, with more than 3.7 million people displaced nationwide since the 2021 coup [1058].
Kachin State had 244,550 people displaced as of 1 June 2026—an increase of about 12,000 over the previous year—with daily clashes
reported on 9 June 2026 [1058]. In Magway Region, more than 44,000 people were displaced in Pakokku and Saw townships since
mid-May 2026, and over 25,000 were displaced in western Bago Region since late May 2026, with airstrikes between 27 May and 2 June
reported to have killed nine civilians and destroyed homes across multiple villages [1061].

Operations beginning 10 May in Myitche (Magway) led to over 19,100 IDPs, nearly 40 civilians killed and approximately 1,000 homes
burned or destroyed; displaced people occupy 12 overcrowded sites facing critical shortages of food and clean water and outbreaks
of diarrhoea as of early June 2026 [1060]. Approximately 225,200 stateless Rohingya are internally displaced within Myanmar, with
sizable Rohingya populations also in Bangladesh, Malaysia and India [1067]. Returns to Myanmar in 2025 were numerically significant
but often occurred under pressure and into insecure, resource-depleted areas, raising concerns about the safety, sustainability and
livelihood prospects of returnees [1105]. Humanitarian response is constrained by access restrictions and funding shortfalls, with the
World Food Programme having halted assistance in parts of Kachin and domestic aid declining [1058][1061]. Attacks on health care
have been extensively documented in Myanmar, with 1,877 incidents recorded in the 2016–2025 monitoring period, undermining
displaced populations' access to medical services [1103]. Protection risks for displaced communities include widespread
conflict-related sexual violence against Rohingya women and children and the arrest or criminalization of health workers,
exacerbating protection and access gaps in displacement settings [1065][1103].
Minorities
Civil society actors have documented systemic discrimination and targeted violence against Rohingya communities, including
large-scale village destruction, numerous airstrikes, and conflict-related sexual violence (over 900 documented cases from February
2021 to May 2025), while warning of junta attempts to undermine international accountability and pressure states pursuing
universal-jurisdiction cases and ICC processes [1065]. Approximately 225,200 stateless Rohingya are internally displaced within
Myanmar, forming part of a broader stateless population challenge totalling hundreds of thousands in the country [1067]. Intensified
operations in southern Chin State have forced more than 17,000 residents from Mindat and surrounding villages into flight, with
repeated airstrikes and damage to civilian infrastructure and health services reported [1068]. A high share of recorded attacks on
health care has been linked to the Myanmar Armed Forces since 2021, and education has experienced among the highest casualty
figures globally from attacks on schools and personnel, exacerbating exclusion and protection gaps for minority communities
[1103][1077]. ASEAN-level engagement has begun to widen beyond junta-centric channels, with the Philippines' special envoy and
other member-state envoys planning consultations with ethnic armed organizations to support more inclusive dialogue under the
Five-Point Consensus [1085][1066][1094].
