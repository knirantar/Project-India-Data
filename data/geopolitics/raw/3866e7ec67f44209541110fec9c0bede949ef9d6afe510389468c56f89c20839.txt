About UNRWA
The United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) is a United Nations 
agency established by the General Assembly in 1949 with a mandate to provide humanitarian assistance and 
protection to registered Palestine Refugees in the Agency’s area of operations, namely the occupied West Bank, 
including East Jerusalem, the Gaza Strip, Jordan, Lebanon and Syria, pending a just and lasting solution to their 
plight. Thousands of Palestine Refugees who lost both their homes and livelihoods because of the 1948 War have 
remained displaced and in need of significant support for more than 75 years. UNRWA helps them achieve their 
full potential through high-quality services in education, healthcare, relief and social services, protection, camp 
infrastructure and improvements, microfinance, and emergency assistance. UNRWA is funded almost entirely by 
voluntary contributions.

2025 unrwa department of health - annual report
I am pleased to introduce the UNRWA Health Annual Report 
for 2025. This report details UNRWA’s efforts to protect 
the health of Palestine Refugees by strengthening service 
delivery, ensuring the continuity of care, and responding to 
urgent healthcare needs in exceptionally difficult conditions.
In 2025, Palestine Refugees continued to endure prolonged 
conflict, displacement, and deepening socioeconomic 
hardship, with implications for both physical and mental 
health. In this context, UNRWA’s health services proved vital 
in all fields of operation, reaching millions of children, women 
and men with primary healthcare and emergency assistance. 
In 2025, UNRWA delivered approximately nine million medical 
consultations across the region. 
Operational conditions in the Occupied Palestinian Territory 
remained highly challenging in 2025. Access and movement 
restrictions, and safety-related constraints repeatedly 
disrupted services. Even so, UNRWA health teams maintained 
the provision of essential care—often under extremely 
challenging circumstances and at great personal risk—to 
those most in need .
I pay tribute to UNRWA health staff for their professionalism 
and dedication. Their life saving work—delivered day after day 
in conditions of uncertainty and insecurity— exemplifies the 
best of humanity and the Agency’s steadfast commitment 
to delivering on its mandate to protect and assist Palestine 
Refugees.
UNRWA’s health work is enabled through close collaboration 
with the World Health Organization and other partners 
supporting critical interventions such as midwifery services, 
immunization, and the supply of essential medicines. 
The sustainability of our efforts also depends on the 
continued and steadfast support of Member States. Adequate 
and predictable funding remains indispensable to sustaining 
and strengthening UNRWA’s health services. I wish to express 
my profound gratitude to all donors who have supported 
the Agency’s health programmes over the past year, and to 
appeal to Member States to consider providing additional 
financial contributions in the coming year to help bridge 
the gap between the critical needs of the Palestine Refugee 
population and the Agency’s financial capacity to meet those 
needs. 
With the commitment and support of its staff, partners and 
donors, UNRWA will continue to adapt its health programme 
to the meet evolving needs of Palestine Refugees, while 
striving to ensure access to high-quality of services for thise 
in need. 
Christian Saunders 
UNRWA Commissioner-General

Foreword by the UNRWA Commissioner-General
unrwa department of health - annual report 2025
I am pleased to extend my congratulations on the release 
of UNRWA’s Department of Health Annual Report 2025. 
This report reflects the Agency’s continued commitment to 
delivering essential health services to Palestine Refugees 
under exceptionally challenging and evolving conditions.
Over the past year, the operating environment has remained 
highly constrained, particularly in the Gaza Strip, where the 
health system continues to function under severe limitations 
despite a fragile ceasefire. In this context, partnership with 
World Health Organization – Eastern Mediterranean Regional 
Office (WHO-EMRO) has been  more important than ever in 
sustaining essential health services and protecting public 
health.
The continued restriction on the entry of medical supplies 
into the Gaza Strip has significantly impacted the availability 
of essential medicines and health commodities. This has 
stressed the importance of close coordination among health 
partners to optimise limited resources and maintain priority 
health interventions.
In 2025, WHO and UNRWA worked closely, alongside partners 
including UNICEF , to implement routine immunisation catch-
up campaigns, reaching approximately 600,000 children 
across the Gaza Strip. UNRWA played a key role in this effort, 
contributing 1,700 health personnel and supporting service 
delivery through 555 mobile and fixed teams at nine health 
centres and 50 medical points. These efforts have been 
critical in mitigating the risk of vaccine-preventable disease 
outbreaks in a highly vulnerable population.
WHO has also continued to support the broader health 
response through strengthened disease surveillance 
systems, enabling timely detection and response to public 
health threats. In a context of disrupted supply chains and 
constrained service delivery, sustained collaboration has 
been essential to maintaining core public health functions.
Looking ahead, WHO EMRO reaffirms its commitment to 
working closely with UNRWA to sustain essential health 
services and strengthen health system resilience. As the 
situation remains fragile, our shared focus on advancing 
health equity and ensuring access to quality healthcare for 
all Palestine Refugees remains firm.

This annual report presents a comprehensive overview of 
the work undertaken by UNRWA’s Department of Health, 
highlighting the Agency’s continued presence, mandate, 
and indispensable role in delivering essential services to 
Palestine Refugees. It showcases UNRWA’s achievements 
in sustaining quality and effective healthcare services 
despite increasingly complex and protracted emergencies 
across its five fields of operation, including the Gaza Strip 
and the occupied West Bank. In recent years, UNRWA and 
its staff have operated under extraordinary pressure marked 
by conflict, displacement, and resource constraints. Despite 
these challenges, the Agency has remained committed to 
ensuring equitable access to care and safeguarding public 
health.
This report aims to document operational performance and 
programmatic outcomes and reaffirm the critical importance 
of UNRWA’s mandate and operational role. In doing  so, it 
highlights the Agency’s responsiveness during crises, its 
continuous efforts to strengthen emergency preparedness 
and response capacities, and its strategic investments 
in health system digitalisation, telemedicine, and service 
improvement. Simultaneously, the report provides forward-
looking insights into priorities, challenges, and opportunities, 
outlining a vision for enhancing resilience, efficiency, and 
sustainability in health services delivery.
Ultimately, this report serves as both a record of accountability 
and an instrument of advocacy, demonstrating that, amid 
ongoing adversities, UNRWA remains essential, adaptive, 
and committed its mission to protect and promote the health 
and dignity of Palestine Refugees, now and into the future.
Holistic care themes
UNRWA’s Department of Health provides holistic healthcare 
to registered Palestine Refugees across its five fields of 
operation: the Gaza Strip, the occupied West Bank, including 
East Jerusalem, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon. 
Primary healthcare is the main pillar of the health programme, 
ensuring adequate coverage and comprehensive healthcare. 
Additionally, support is provided to patients requiring 
secondary and tertiary healthcare, including coordination of 
referrals and financial coverage of outpatient consultations 
and inpatient care (i.e., hospital admission for more 
specialised treatment or surgery).  
vision and purpose of the 2025 health annual report 
Vaccination, newborn screening, and detection of growth 
problems, along with referrals, are offered to children below 
five. The essential programme of immunisation follows the 
host country programme, and UNRWA serves as a partner 
in essential immunisation and in reporting on vaccine-
preventable outbreaks. 
Services provided to women include pre-conception, 
antenatal and post-natal care, as well as family planning 
services, multiple micronutrient supplementation (MMS), 
support against gender-based violence, and other outpatient 
services. Nutrition services are provided to pregnant women, 
infants, and children, especially in the Gaza Strip and 
Lebanon, where malnutrition is more common than in other 
fields due to conflict, instability, and displacement. 
Nursing and midwifery services play a pivotal role in linking 
maternal and child health interventions and in delivering 
planned training programmes to frontline workers. This work 
is carried out through workshops and discussions held at 
field offices and headquarters, thereby opening avenues for 
contact across the fields.
In the context of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), UNRWA 
provides a robust programme for preventing and managing 
diabetes and hypertension. Prevention interventions 
include screening for NCDs and health promotion, while 
management includes counselling, medications, and insulin 
supply, following technical instructions designed by UNRWA 
which are aligned with the latest WHO protocols. 
Mental health is a key domain of UNRWA’s services. 
The Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) 
programme systematically screens vulnerable population 
groups, including pregnant women, children, and NCDs 
patients with uncontrolled disease, for mental health and 
psychosocial conditions. Individuals identified as being in 
need receive appropriate, tiered psychosocial and mental 
health interventions, including support for depression, with 
different levels   of assistance provided for those with higher 
depression scores. 
Outpatient services include dental care, physiotherapy, 
laboratory services, and radiology. Oral screening is 
performed for first-year school students, along with medical 
and physical examinations.

health profile
For over 75 years, UNRWA has been a key provider of 
healthcare for Palestine Refugees. Since 1950, when the 
refugee population numbered approximately 750,000, the 
number of Palestine Refugees registered at UNRWA was 
around 6.8 millions in all fields, including the Gaza Strip, 
by the end of 2025. Today, in the five fields, approximately 
3.25 million people have either sought healthcare or are 
potentially in need of health services provided through 
UNRWA’s health system. Nearly one-third of the population 
across the five fields lives in 58 official refugee camps, 
often in overcrowded settings with fragile infrastructure 
and limited resources.
Palestine Refugees faced unprecedented challenges in 
2025. The war in the Gaza Strip, escalating settler violence 
in the occupied West Bank, instability in Syria, and economic 
collapse in Lebanon have severely disrupted health service 
delivery. In the Gaza Strip, widespread destruction of 
health facilities, repeated displacement, and restrictions 
on the entry of medicines and supplies have left many 
without essential care. Health workers themselves face 
extreme stress and trauma while continuing to serve their 
communities. In Lebanon and the occupied West Bank, 
displacement and movement restrictions further limit 
access to health services.
Despite these conditions, UNRWA remains committed to 
delivering primary healthcare with minimal interruptions. 
Maternal and child health remain core priorities. With 23 
per cent of registered Palestine Refugees aged 18 years 
or younger, the need for targeted interventions is urgent. 
In 2025, across the Agency’s four fields of operation, 
excluding the Gaza Strip , UNRWA supported approximately 
65,442 pregnant women through maternal health services. 
Preconception care and family planning services were 
provided to 29,436 women registered in the services in 2025. 
In addition, UNRWA teams delivered care to 200,033 infants 
and children under five, across the four fields. 
The incidence of non-communicable diseases continued 
to rise. In 2025, UNRWA treated over 87,135 patients with 
hypertension, 31,399 patients with diabetes mellitus, and 
98,088 patients with both conditions across the four fields, 
excluding the Gaza Strip. 
Communicable diseases have re-emerged due to 
overcrowding in camps, poor sanitation in some fields 
and areas, and displacement. In 2024, polio and cholera 
were recorded in the Gaza Strip and Lebanon, respectively. 
Immunisation campaigns were implemented urgently. No 
vaccine-preventable outbreaks were declared last year.

Challenges
UNRWA faced a complex set of operational and public 
health challenges, driven primarily by conflict, access 
constraints, and resource limitations. In the Gaza Strip, the 
situation was particularly severe, with continuous outbreaks 
of respiratory diseases, diarrheal diseases, and Hepatitis 
A reported throughout the year. The humanitarian crisis 
escalated further in August 2025 when  famine (IPC Phase 5) 
condition was confirmed by IPC in Gaza City , necessitating 
urgent, life-saving nutrition interventions. Community-based 
Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) programmes 
were scaled up despite damaged infrastructure and 
significant access constraints, highlighting both the scale 
of need and the operational difficulties. Infrastructure 
destruction and widespread displacement severely limited 
service capacity, while service suspensions led to a sharp 
decline in hospitalisations and created critical gaps in 
access to care.
In the occupied West Bank,  service delivery was directly 
affected by the forced shutdown of three main health centres 
in Tulkarm, Jenin and Nur Shams. Across all fields, physician 
workload exceeded WHO standards. On average, doctors 
handled 71.11 consultations per day, reflecting sustained 
pressure on frontline services and raising concerns about 
the quality of care and staff wellbeing amid ongoing staffing 
constraints. Supply chain disruptions posed another major 
challenge. Approximately 30 per cent of health centre 
pharmacies experienced shortages of essential medicines 
and supplies at various times due to military restrictions and 
instability. In the Gaza Strip, pharmaceutical shortages were 
particularly acute, with up to 58 per cent of medicines out 
of stock. These shortages, combined with severe conflict, 
restricted access, and logistical barriers, significantly 
constrained service continuity.
At the same time, population health needs intensified. The 
burden of care remained high, with 25 per cent of pregnant 
women in the fields excluding the Gaza Strip classified as 
high-risk, requiring more intensive and higher-quality primary 
healthcare. The demand for chronic disease care remained 
high, while funding constraints continued to limit system 
capacity. Mental health needs surged under the strain of 
conflict and displacement, with 4 per cent of women screened 
for mental health illnesses identified as being exposed to 
gender-based violence in the four fields, excluding the Gaza 
Strip. Although services expanded, they did so amid severe 
access and resource gaps. Overall, the combination of rising 
health needs, limited resources, and a volatile operating

Achievements
In the Gaza Strip, UNRWA sustained primary healthcare 
through six–eight health centres 3 and 53 medical points 
despite system collapse. A total of 1.7 million internally

through 112 health teams. High service volumes were

care visits and 127,000 vaccinations delivered. The nutrition 
response was also scaled, with 163,704 MUAC screenings 
conducted among children aged 6–59 months, and 12,181 
cases of global acute malnutrition referred to therapeutic 
feeding programmes.
Despite the ongoing crisis, the health teams in the Gaza 
Strip continued to report reliable data throughout the 
period, ensuring continuity of monitoring and accountability. 
In parallel, the health teams in the occupied West Bank 
established emergency clinics to maintain health service 
provision for displaced populations. In the occupied West 
Bank, more than 90 per cent of patients with diabetes in 
Jenin, Tulkarm and Nur Shams were fully covered, and 
patients in the north of the occupied West Bank continued 
to receive insulin, chronic medications, and appropriate care.
Health communication efforts through multiple channels, 
including social media and information, education, and

outreach and visibility during crises, as well as its partnerships 
and emergency response by expanding access to public health 
information and services. At the same time, data analysis 
and research conducted under UNRWA’s health programme 
continued to reinforce the Agency’s global health role.
The life-cycle primary health care (PHC) model ensured 
integrated care for refugee families, with strengthened 
preconception care improving early detection of abnormalities 
and maternal outcomes. Maternal services continued to 
integrate quality, mental health, and protection standards,

across all fields. In 2025, more than 43,000 pregnant women

compared to 17 per 100,000 in 2024. Between 84–92.4

clinics. Infectious disease surveillance was maintained 
through manual data collection and Early Warning, Alert, 
and Response System (EWARS), and health centres in East

contexts
The operating environment of the UNRWA Health Programme 
in 2025 was shaped by rapidly evolving political, security, 
and socio-economic dynamics across its fields of operation. 
In the Gaza Strip, the protracted war and its aftermath 
have fundamentally altered the public health landscape. 
Widespread infrastructure damage, repeated massive 
population displacement, and severe restrictions on the 
entry of essential supplies have contributed to a sustained 
deterioration in health determinants. The collapse of basic 
services including water, sanitation, and electricity, has 
increased the risk of communicable disease outbreaks, 
while disruptions in continuity of care have exacerbated non-
communicable diseases and maternal and child health risks. 
At the same time, the psychological toll of prolonged exposure 
to violence and displacement has led to an unprecedented 
burden of mental health conditions, particularly among 
children and vulnerable populations. These factors have 
required continuous adaptation of service delivery modalities, 
including flexibility, dynamic planning, decentralisation of care 
and expanded outreach mechanisms.
In the northern areas of the occupied West Bank, escalating 
security operations, movement restrictions, and displacement 
from refugee camps have increasingly constrained access 
to health services. Frequent incursions and closures 
have disrupted routine service provision, hindered patient 
mobility, and limited the ability of health staff to operate 
safely and effectively. These dynamics have contributed to 
delayed care-seeking, interruptions in treatment for chronic 
conditions, and growing protection concerns, all of which 
have direct implications for population health outcomes.
In Lebanon, the ongoing economic crisis has further 
deepened vulnerabilities among Palestine Refugees. At the 
same time, socio-economic hardship has intensified food 
insecurity and negatively affected health-seeking behaviours, 
particularly among the most vulnerable groups, including 
women, children, and older persons.
Across all the five fields, these crises have underscored the 
imperative of sustaining a resilient, adaptable, and equity-
focused primary healthcare approach. In line with the 
overarching principle of “Stay and Deliver,” the UNRWA Health 
Programme has continued to operate under severe and 
protracted constraints, ensuring the continuity of essential 
services, prioritising the most vulnerable populations, 
and upholding standards of quality and integrity of care. 
This commitment to remain present and responsive in the 
most challenging environments defined the programme’s 
operational posture in 2025. These contextual realities are 
fundamental to interpreting programme performance and 
shaping strategic priorities for the period ahead.

the e-health software
UNRWA’s adoption of the e-Health electronic system 
represents a critical advancement in the digital transformation 
of primary healthcare delivery and monitoring. By enabling 
real-time data entry at the health centre level, the system 
ensures that patient information, service utilisation, and key 
health indicators are captured accurately and consistently 
at the point of care. This immediate digitisation minimises 
transcription errors associated with paper-based systems, 
enhances data completeness, and supports standardised 
reporting across all fields of operation.
A central strength of the e-health system lies in its integration 
with a centralised data warehouse, where information from 
all health centres is pooled, validated, and stored securely. 
This architecture facilitates efficient data aggregation and 
longitudinal tracking of health trends, enabling evidence-
based decision-making at both operational and strategic 
levels. The linkage of this warehouse to interactive 
dashboards further amplifies its utility. Health managers 
and UNRWA officers can access up-to-date, visualised 
data through user-friendly interfaces, allowing for rapid 
interpretation of trends, identification of service gaps, 
and timely programmatic adjustments. This significantly 
enhances responsiveness, accountability, and performance 
monitoring across the health programme.
Importantly, the system’s flexibility has proven essential in 
humanitarian contexts. During emergencies and crises, 
including situations of displacement or restricted access, 
UNRWA has dynamically extended access to the e-Health 
platform to emergency units and mobile clinics. This ensures

outcome 1: palestine refugees maternal and child health
Maternal health 
overview 
UNRWA delivers a comprehensive and integrated package 
of health services designed to protect, promote, and sustain 
the health and well-being of Palestine Refugee women, 
children, and families across all fields of operation. These 
services include maternal healthcare, child health services, 
school health, disability care, nutrition interventions, gender-
based violence (GBV) response, gender mainstreaming, and 
adolescent- and youth-friendly health services, delivered 
through UNRWA’s primary healthcare (PHC) system. This 
integrated model reflects a life cycle approach, recognising 
the intergenerational determinants of health and the critical 
importance of continuity of care across key stages of life.
What are the maternal health services provided?
Maternal health remains a cornerstone of UNRWA’s primary 
healthcare programme and a critical investment in reducing 
preventable maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality 
among Palestine Refugees. UNRWA’s maternal health services 
encompass family planning (FP), preconception care (PCC), 
antenatal care (ANC), delivery care through referral networks, 
and postnatal care (PNC). These services are delivered through 
UNRWA health centres and are designed to ensure early risk 
identification, timely intervention, and continuous follow-
up, even in highly constrained and crisis-affected settings. 
Approximately 43,500 pregnant women were registered and 
have accessed antenatal care services at UNRWA health 
centres across the four fields of operation in 2025, excluding the 
Gaza Strip. Additionally, around 22,000 pregnant women who 
were registered in 2024 have accessed maternal care in 2025 
across the four fields of operation, excluding the Gaza Strip.
Family planning
UNRWA health centres ensure universal access to integrated 
family planning services, enabling women and couples to 
make informed decisions on fertility, birth spacing, and 
pregnancy timing. These services are delivered through 
counselling, modern contraceptive provision, and initiatives 
that promote male engagement, contributing directly to 
improved maternal and child health outcomes within primary 
healthcare. Around 92,000 women accessed UNRWA’s 
family planning services in the four fields of operation last 
year, excluding the Gaza Strip.  Approximately 11,000 of the 
women were newly enrolled in the programme.
Preconception care
Over the past decade, UNRWA has progressively strengthened 
preconception care (PCC) as a strategic, evidence-based 
intervention to reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes, 
maternal complications, and preventable maternal and 
neonatal mortality. Since its introduction as a standard 
service in 2011, PCC has become an essential pillar of 
maternal health services delivered across all UNRWA health 
centres, reflecting a shift towards upstream, preventive, and 
life cycle oriented maternal healthcare.

Preconception care aims to prepare women of reproductive 
age for pregnancy by optimising their health status prior 
to conception. Women attending PCC undergo systematic 
assessment for medical and psychosocial risk factors that 
may affect pregnancy and birth outcomes. This includes 
screening for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, anaemia, oral 
health conditions, and other chronic or acute illnesses, as 
well as targeted health counselling, lifestyle modification 
support, and health promotion interventions. Preventive 
measures include routine folic acid supplementation to 
reduce the risk of congenital anomalies, thereby improving 
maternal and newborn outcomes before pregnancy begins.
A key added value of preconception care lies in its contribution 
to early pregnancy detection and timely registration for 
antenatal care. Women who engage with PCC services are 
more likely to recognise pregnancy early and to register 
promptly for ANC, allowing the early initiation of evidence-
based, quality antenatal care. Early registration remains 
a cornerstone of UNRWA’s ANC programme, as it enables 
comprehensive risk stratification, personalised follow-up, and 
timely management throughout the continuum of pregnancy. 
Around 72.7 per cent of pregnant women registered- during 
the first trimester at UNRWA’s ANC services and efforts are 
ongoing to increase the per cent across all fields. 
Recognising its high impact, the Health Department placed 
renewed strategic emphasis on strengthening preconception 
care in 2025 as a core intervention to reduce preventable 
maternal morbidity and mortality. This prioritisation 
translated into measurable improvements across the 
Agency. The percentage of women newly registered in PCC 
services increased by 3.72 per cent Agency-wide, indicating 
enhanced uptake, awareness, and engagement with pre 
pregnancy services. In parallel, the strengthened focus on 
PCC contributed to a 3.42 per cent increase in the proportion 
of newly registered pregnant women, underscoring improved 
early pregnancy identification and stronger linkage between 
preconception and antenatal care services.
UNRWA registered 43,450 new pregnant women across the 
four fields (excluding the Gaza Strip) in 2025, compared to 
42,206 in 2024, representing a 2.95 per cent year on year 
increase. These gains highlight the effectiveness of upstream 
investments in preconception and early pregnancy services 
and reinforce the role of PCC as a critical entry point into the 
maternal health continuum, particularly in fragile and crisis 
affected settings where timely access to care is essential for 
safeguarding maternal and newborn lives.
Strengthening quality, mental health, and protection in 
maternal care
UNRWA’s Health Department advanced a major 
transformation of its maternal health programme last year, 
reinforcing an integrated, people centred model of care 
across all five fields of operation. A central achievement of this 
effort was the comprehensive revision of the maternal health 
framework, which now consolidates clinical quality, maternal 
mental health, and patient protection into interdependent, 
mutually reinforcing pillars of service delivery.
Updated maternal health technical instructions were 
developed through a collaborative, consultative process 
involving field teams and technical experts and were aligned 
with the latest World Health Organization (WHO) evidence 
based recommendations. These updates embedded explicit 
quality of care standards, strengthened referral pathways, 
and institutionalised routine maternal mental health screening 
within daily clinical practice. This integrated approach enables 
the early identification and management of psychosocial 
risks alongside biomedical care, ensuring more holistic and 
responsive services for pregnant and postpartum women.
To support consistent implementation across diverse and 
often challenging operational contexts, 15 Trainers of Trainers 
(TOTs) from the five fields were trained to lead cascade 
trainings at field level, aimed at strengthening frontline clinical 
competencies, psychosocial support skills, and adherence 
to updated protocols. This investment has promoted greater

 Gender-based violence (GBV)
Gender-Based Violence (GBV) services within UNRWA’s 
health programme are embedded within a broader 
framework of reproductive health, protection, and gender 
mainstreaming, reflecting a rights-based and survivor-
centred approach. GBV response is integrated into maternal 
and reproductive health services, allowing women and girls 
to access care through familiar and less stigmatising entry 
points such as antenatal, postnatal, and family planning 
services. This integration facilitates early detection of GBV 
cases and ensures that survivors receive timely, confidential, 
and appropriate care. UNRWA’s GBV services encompass 
clinical management of rape, psychosocial support, referral 
pathways to specialised services, and coordination with 
protection actors, including UNFPA and other partners. In 
crisis settings such as the Gaza Strip, where the breakdown 
of social systems and overcrowded shelters increase risks of 
violence, GBV services have been adapted to operate through 
medical points and mobile teams, ensuring continued 
access despite infrastructure collapse. The Agency has 
also strengthened its collaboration with partners to provide 
essential supplies, including reproductive health kits and 
dignity kits, and to deploy trained midwives and health 
personnel capable of responding to GBV-related needs. 
Importantly, GBV interventions are linked with community 
awareness and risk mitigation strategies, aiming to reduce 
stigma and encourage reporting. The integration of GBV into 
health services also supports continuity of care, as survivors 
can receive medical, psychological, and social support within 
a coordinated system. Despite significant challenges such 
as resource constraints, insecurity, and access limitations, 
UNRWA has maintained a functional GBV response that 
prioritises dignity, confidentiality, and comprehensive 
care, reinforcing its commitment to protecting vulnerable 
populations in both stable and emergency contexts.

Infant and child health 
Infant and childcare
UNRWA continues to provide comprehensive child healthcare 
as a cornerstone of its primary healthcare programme, 
protecting and improving the health and well-being of Palestine 
Refugee children through a life-cycle, preventive approach. 
Delivered through the Family Health Team model, child health 
services span from maternal and newborn care to early 
childhood and adolescence, ensuring continuity and integration 
across critical stages of development. Core services include 
newborn assessments, periodic physical examinations, routine 
immunisation, growth monitoring and nutritional surveillance, 
micronutrient supplementation, preventive oral healthcare, and 
timely referral for specialised care.
Last year, UNRWA health centres provided preventive 
services to 198,159 children under the age of five.  
Immunisation coverage remained high across fields, 
reaching 98.4 per cent among children aged 12 months 
and 97.7 per cent among those aged 18 months, reflecting 
strong service delivery and effective follow-up. Growth 
monitoring and nutritional surveillance, supported by the 
e-Health system and aligned with WHO standards, continued 
to identify the persistent double burden of malnutrition. 
In response, UNRWA intensified nutrition counselling, 
updated clinical guidelines and health education materials, 
and continued routine iron and vitamin A supplementation, 
anaemia screening, and referral for hereditary anaemias 
when indicated, reinforcing child health services as a 
lifelong investment in resilience, wellbeing, and human 
development. (For the infant and child healthcare report,

School health
UNRWA’s School Health Programme delivers a 
comprehensive package of preventive, promotive, and 
protective health services to Palestine Refugee students 
through close collaboration between the Health and

Nutrition 
Nutrition is a cross-cutting service integrated within 
UNRWA’s primary healthcare programme, supporting 
Palestine Refugees across all life stages. It contributes to 
the prevention and management of both Non-Communicable 
Diseases (NCDs) and Maternal and Child Health (MCH) 
conditions. UNRWA continued efforts to strengthen 
early detection of malnutrition among children under five 
through growth monitoring and screening, while addressing 
nutritional deficiencies among pregnant and lactating 
women (PLW) and children. Nutrition services also included 
counseling on breastfeeding and complementary feeding, 
as well as individual nutrition consultations for patients with 
NCDs such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and 
obesity. Additional details are presented in relevant sections 
of this report, including maternal health services, child health 
services, school health, and non-communicable diseases.
Nutrition needs and programme priorities vary across 
UNRWA fields of operation. In the Gaza Strip, the 
humanitarian disaster that began in October 2023 continued 
throughout 2025, severely affecting food security and access 
to essential services. Despite major operational constraints, 
UNRWA maintained life-saving nutrition services. As an 
active member of the UNICEF-led Nutrition Cluster and its 
Technical Working Groups, UNRWA supported screening for 
acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months using 
Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) measurements in line 
with WHO and UNICEF guidance.
The situation deteriorated further in mid-2025. In August

Nutrition surveillance system on child malnutrition in the 
Gaza Strip 
During the two years of war in the Gaza Strip, child malnutrition 
became an increasing concern. In collaboration with 
UNICEF , WHO, and OCHA, UNRWA launched the Community 
Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) programme 
across health centres and medical points in January 2024. 
In health centres, children aged 6-59 months visiting clinics 
for vaccination, growth monitoring, malnutrition screenings, 
or sick visits were screened for acute malnutrition using 
mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) tapes donated by 
UNICEF . At Medical Points, which are primary care stations 
inside shelters and tented camps, UNRWA staff conducted 
outreach to inform families living in shelters and surrounding 
communities about the availability of nutrition services. Any 
children aged 6-9 months present at the medical points were 
screened for acute malnutrition using MUAC measurements. 
Children with MUAC results under 125 mm were enrolled in the 
therapeutic feeding programme and followed up periodically.

Multiple micronutrient supplementation (MMS)
Maternal anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies are a 
major public health burden among Palestine Refugees in the 
Middle East. Since the 1990s, UNRWA Health Programme has 
provided antenatal iron and folic acid (IFA) supplementation 
to address maternal anaemia. However, the effectiveness of 
the IFA supplementation remained uncertain. In alignment 
with the 2020 WHO recommendation to use MMS in the 
“context of rigorous research”, UNRWA has advanced efforts 
to enable, plan, pilot and evaluate the replacement of the 
IFA regimen with the United Nations International Multiple 
Micronutrients Antenatal Preparation (UNIMMAP)-formulated 
MMS as the standard of care within its antenatal care 
system. In collaboration with Vitamin Angel Alliance, Johns 
Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, Sight and Life 
Foundation, and Kirk Humanitarian Foundation, UNRWA 
conducted 10-month implementation research in Jordan in 
2023 to compare implementation outcomes between MMS 
delivery and the previous IFA regimen. The study documented 
improvements in MMS clinics in coverage, adherence, fewer 
side effects, greater acceptability, improved mid-pregnancy 
haemoglobin levels with a lower risk of anaemia, and some cost 
savings for patients and the health system. Based on the key 
findings, the Agency has decided to scale up the MMS system-
wide. Since 2024, all 25 clinics in Jordan distribute UNIMMAP-
formulated MMS at registration, reaching approximately 
20,000 Palestine Refugee pregnant women annually. In 2025, 
the Agency worked on scaling up MMS across 26 UNRWA 
facilities in Lebanon and 26 UNRWA clinics in Syria, through 
pre-assessment, trainings of health officers and clinicians, and 
integration of MMS surveillance system. As of December 2025, 
all UNRWA health centres and mobile clinics in Lebanon and 
Syria provide a 180-count bottle of MMS to pregnant women at 
registration. Concurrently, the UNRWA Health Department has 
expanded the MMS surveillance system, which has enabled 
system-wide evaluation of inputs, throughputs, and outputs 
during MMS scale-up and implementation. Key findings 
on programme fidelity and sustainability are continuously 
communicated to the key programme staff to inform 
programmatic and policy adjustments.

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs)
Overview of the programmes
Disease prevention within UNRWA’s health programme is a 
core strategic priority aimed at reducing morbidity, mortality, 
and long-term health system burden among Palestine 
Refugees. The burden of non-communicable diseases 
(NCDs) continues to rise, driven by increased life expectancy, 
lifestyle changes, and disruptions in healthcare continuity 
caused by ongoing crises. Cardiovascular diseases, diabetes 
mellitus (DM), hypertension, chronic respiratory diseases, 
and cancer remain the leading causes of morbidity among 
Palestine Refugees. In 2025, UNRWA provided services to 
216,622 NCD patients, distributed as follows: 87,135 for 
hypertension, 31,399 for DM, and 98,088 for both conditions 
across the four fields, excluding the Gaza Strip. Economic 
hardship has driven up risk factors such as malnutrition, 
obesity, and smoking, underscoring the importance of 
sustained prevention and management strategies. UNRWA’s 
digital health initiatives, including its NCD mobile application, 
remain crucial for patient engagement and treatment 
adherence. The Agency adopts an integrated, primary 
healthcare-based approach that combines prevention, 
early detection, and continuous management of both 
noncommunicable diseases and mental health, along with 
psychosocial support services. 
(For full NCD report please see Annex 9.)
The estimated Agency-wide (excluding Gaza) prevalence 
of DM and hypertension has slightly varied from the 2024 
figures. Among patients aged 18 years and above, the 
prevalence of DM is 18.6 per cent, while the prevalence of 
hypertension is 26.7 per cent. Among those aged 40 years 
and above, the prevalence of having DM and HTN together 
reaches 31.3 per cent and 45.5 per cent, respectively. For 
individuals aged 60 years and above, the rates increased 
to 47.6 per cent for DM and decreased to 68.4 per cent for 
hypertension.
Noncommunicable disease (NCD) prevention focuses 
primarily on diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension 
(HTN), which represent a significant and growing burden. 
UNRWA implements standardised, WHO-aligned protocols 
emphasising risk factor modification, early diagnosis, regular 
monitoring, and continuity of care. Preventive efforts include 
lifestyle counselling, screening of at-risk populations, and 
structured follow-up systems to improve disease control and 
reduce complications. 
Recognising the strong link between physical and mental 
health, UNRWA also integrates Mental Health and Psychosocial 
Support Services (MHPSS) into primary care. These services 
focus on early identification, basic mental healthcare, and 
referral pathways, particularly for vulnerable populations and 
patients with chronic conditions such as DM and HTN.

Measures, standards, interventions, and guidelines followed 
to ensure high-quality care
In 2025, UNRWA’s Disease Prevention and Control (DPC) 
programme delivered quality care guided by a comprehensive 
set of evidence-based technical standards, operational 
guidelines, and quality-assurance mechanisms aligned with 
global public health norms and adapted to humanitarian 
settings. The programme was implemented within UNRWA’s 
Primary HealthCare (PHC) model, ensuring continuity, equity, 
and person-centred care across all five fields of operation. 
Core interventions were guided by UNRWA Technical 
Instructions and Guidelines on Disease Prevention and 
Control, which standardise prevention, early detection, 
treatment, and follow-up for both communicable and 
non-communicable diseases (NCDs). These guidelines 
are explicitly aligned with WHO normative guidance. The 
quality of care was further strengthened through the Family 
Health Team (FHT) approach, which promotes integrated, 
multidisciplinary, and longitudinal care across life stages.
 
To ensure clinical quality and accountability, UNRWA applied 
standardised screening protocols, risk stratification tools, 
and clinical audit mechanisms, particularly for priority NCDs 
such as diabetes and hypertension. Regular monitoring, use 
of indicator frameworks aligned with WHO and Sustainable 
Development Goal (SDG) targets, and structured referral 
and follow-up pathways supported continuous quality 
improvement across health centres.
 
Collectively, these measures enabled UNRWA to provide safe, 
effective, and equitable disease prevention services in 2025, 
even amid conflict, displacement, and resource constraints, 
while contributing practical lessons to global public health 
and humanitarian practice.
Outcomes achieved and expected impact of implemented 
outputs
In 2025, the systematic implementation of UNRWA’s Disease 
Prevention and Control (DPC) interventions translated 
programmatic outputs into measurable health service 
outcomes and sustained population-level impact, despite 
severe operational constraints. Strengthened application of 
standardised technical guidelines, integrated service delivery, 
and continuous monitoring contributed to improved service 
continuity, early detection, and quality of non-communicable 
disease care. 
Consistent application of screening, risk stratification, 
and follow-up protocols resulted in earlier diagnosis and 
improved clinical monitoring of patients with diabetes 
and hypertension. Integration within the Family Health 
Team approach supported continuity of care, adherence to 
treatment, and management of multimorbidity, contributing 
to improved control of disease progression and prevention 
of avoidable complications. Additionally, mental health 
integration strengthened health-seeking behaviour and risk 
awareness, particularly among vulnerable groups.
Despite all the challenges, including difficult security 
situations and restrictions on access to UNRWA health 
services In the Gaza Strip and the occupied West Bank, 
the screening for NCDs continued. The health programme 
across the Agency screened 160,000 patients with risk 
factors for diabetes in 2025. Six per cent of them were 
found to have diabetes, hypertension, or both (two per cent 
DM, three per cent HTN, and one per cent, both conditions). 
The detection rate remained the same as the previous 
year across the agency. Moreover, control status among 
patients with diabetes has improved compared with 2024 
across the four fields, excluding the Gaza Strip (34 per cent 
vs 32 per cent).
Using the GHQ-12 tool, 139,000 Palestine Refugees were 
screened for mental health and psychosocial issues. Of 
those, 12 per cent were identified to have MHPSS issues 
(score >6), and 99 per cent were provided with assistance. 
This is a significant improvement compared to 9.85 per cent 
detection in 2024 and reflects the magnitude of the mental 
health and psychosocial issues among Palestine Refugees. 
Collectively, these outcomes are likely to yield long-term 
impact through reduced avoidable morbidity and disability, 
preservation of the functionality of overstretched health 
services, and increased resilience of refugee communities. 
The 2025 experience further reinforced UNRWA’s role in 
generating operational public-health evidence applicable to 
humanitarian and protracted crisis contexts worldwide.
Challenges and limitations hindered optimal outcomes and 
impact 
Despite significant achievements in 2025, the scale and 
impact of disease prevention interventions were constrained 
by a complex set of contextual, operational, and systemic 
challenges. Protracted conflict, insecurity, and extensive 
infrastructure damage, particularly in the Gaza Strip and 
the northern part of the occupied West Bank, disrupted 
routine preventive services, referral pathways, and follow-up 
care, while repeated population displacement undermined 
continuity of screening and long-term management of non-
communicable diseases.

Prevention of Diabetic Foot and Retinopathy among Palestine 
refugees in Syria and Lebanon (World Diabetes Foundation 
(WDF) Project)
During 2025, the three-year World Diabetes Foundation (WDF) 
project, which began in October 2023 and targets Palestine 
Refugees in Syria and Lebanon, expanded its coverage by 
equipping an additional 21 health centres with the necessary 
furniture and medical equipment, thereby ensuring that all 
52 targeted health centres are now fully supported under 
the initiative. Capacity-building efforts were strengthened 
through the completion of in-service training on diabetic 
retinopathy in both Lebanon and Syria, benefiting a total of 
50 medical officers and 80 nurses. In parallel, on-the-job 
training on diabetic foot prevention was sustained across 
all 52 health centres, reinforcing clinical competencies and 
standardising care practices. 
Procurement activities were also completed during the 
year, including the acquisition of 76 monofilaments and 48-
foot Doppler devices to enhance screening and diagnostic 
capacity. To support patient awareness and self-care, 
approximately 70,000 health education materials on 
diabetic retinopathy and diabetic foot prevention were 
produced and distributed. Screening activities yielded 
significant outputs, with 9,768 patients in Lebanon 
and 7,730 patients in Syria assessed for diabetic foot 
complications. Approximately 16 per cent of them were 
identified as having abnormalities and were subsequently 
provided with appropriate health education, clinical 
interventions, and referrals, as needed. Furthermore, 
screening for diabetic retinopathy was initiated using 
portable fundoscopic cameras, implemented on a 
rotational basis across the targeted facilities. 
Throughout the reporting period, project monitoring was 
maintained through regular online meetings and supervisory 
field visits, ensuring continuous oversight and quality 
assurance. Updates on progress and achievements were 
systematically compiled and communicated to the donor.

Mental health 
UNRWA’s Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) 
services are a core component of its primary health care 
approach, particularly in contexts of protracted conflict, 
displacement, and socioeconomic hardship affecting 
Palestine Refugees. Integrated within the Family Health 
Team (FHT) model, MHPSS is embedded into routine primary 
care to enable early identification, referral, and management 
of common mental health conditions, while addressing 
psychosocial stressors linked to conflict and poverty.
In 2025, despite severe disruptions, especially in the Gaza 
Strip and the northern occupied West Bank, UNRWA sustained 
MHPSS delivery through 123 health centres across the four 
fields, excluding the Gaza Strip, and adapted services in high-
intensity settings via shelters, mobile teams, and remaining 
operational facilities. In the Gaza Strip, services continued 
under extreme conditions through emergency shelters and 
medical points serving internally displaced populations, 
including psychological first aid, basic counselling, and 
targeted support for vulnerable groups. Aligned with 
WHO’s mhGAP framework, UNRWA’s approach emphasises 
task-shifting and capacity-building among non-specialist 
providers, reflecting a resilient and adaptive model that 
ensures continuity and accessibility of mental healthcare 
within primary health systems.

Infectious disease surveillance 
Infectious disease surveillance is a core public health 
function that enables the timely detection, reporting, 
analysis, and response to communicable diseases of public 
health importance. It supports early outbreak detection, 
disease trend monitoring, targeted prevention and control 
measures, and protection of population health through 
evidence-based action. UNRWA maintains an infectious 
disease surveillance system supported by its electronic 
platform, with a weekly reporting system and an infectious 
disease dashboard. 
UNRWA continues its cooperation with host authorities and 
WHO and participates in immunisation campaigns across 
all fields where required. UNRWA’s focus on strengthening 
surveillance of emerging and re-emerging diseases remains 
active. Close coordination with the host-country Ministries 
of Health continues, including surveillance of communicable 
diseases, outbreak investigation and response, supply of 
vaccines, and exchange of information.

Immunisation     
Child immunisation remains the most effective primary 
prevention method. UNRWA health services provide 
immunisation against several vaccine-preventable diseases in 
alignment with host country national vaccination programmes. 
UNRWA offers vaccines for tetanus-diphtheria (DT/Td), 
pertussis, tuberculosis (TB), measles, rubella, mumps, polio, 
haemophilus influenza type B (Hib), and hepatitis B across 
all fields. Additionally, the rotavirus vaccine is provided in 
all fields except Syria, while the pneumococcal vaccine is 
available in the West Bank, the Gaza Strip, and Lebanon, and 
was added to the national immunisation programme in Jordan 
in September 2025. Since 2020, UNRWA has also introduced 
the Hepatitis A vaccine in Jordan as part of the country’s 
national vaccination programme. In 2025, the occupied 
West Bank experienced a shortage of BCG, MMR, OPV , and 
Rota vaccines. Immunisation coverage varied across fields, 
ranging from 96.5 per cent in the occupied West Bank to 99.9 
per cent in Jordan, bringing Agency-wide coverage, excluding 
the Gaza Strip, for the aforementioned diseases to 98.4 per 
cent for children aged 12 months, which is above the WHO-
referenced target for high population immunity. Coverage for 
booster doses among children aged 18 months was 97.7 per 
cent. Vitamin A coverage remained high at 97.7 per cent. The 
use of the e-MCH application for mothers has played a key 
role in maintaining high vaccination coverage. This digital 
tool sends timely reminders to mothers based on each child’s 
vaccination schedule, helping reduce the number of missed 
doses and minimising the need for nurse follow-ups.

Pharmaceutical services 
In 2025, UNRWA continued to provide essential medicines 
and medical supplies to Palestine Refugees across all 
fields of operation, maintaining a central role in supporting 
primary healthcare services despite a highly constrained 
environment. Through adaptive supply chain management 
and strong partnerships, the availability of medicines 
remained relatively stable across several fields.
Relatively stable supply conditions were maintained in 
Jordan and Lebanon, where no prolonged stockouts of 
critical medicines were reported. This was supported by 
stable procurement channels and effective coordination 
mechanisms. In Lebanon, this outcome was achieved 
despite a deteriorating economic context that continues to 
place pressure on health systems. In Syria, improvements 
were observed following the introduction of more efficient 
regulatory processes in the host country, which significantly 
reduced approval timelines and enabled faster clearance of 
medical supplies. 
Partnerships remained a cornerstone of pharmaceutical 
service delivery throughout the year. Contributions from 
WHO, UNICEF , UNFPA, and other humanitarian partners 
supported the provision of essential medicines, vaccines, 
micronutrients, and reproductive health commodities. In 
particular, WHO support played a critical role in sustaining 
medicine availability, through the procurement of substantial 
volume of essential medicines that were procured and 
distributed across multiple fields of operation in 2025. 
In parallel, supply optimisation measures were implemented 
across the Agency, including the redistribution of medicines 
that could not enter the Gaza Strip before expiry to other 
fields of operation, in line with operational guidance and 
programme needs. This approach helped mitigate losses 
associated with delivery delays and supported the planned 
requirements for 2026 particularly in Syria, Lebanon, and 
Jordan.
Overall, these combined efforts contributed to maintaining a 
generally stable stock status across most fields, with minimal 
and short-term stockouts of critical medicines reported.
The most severe disruptions were observed in the Gaza 
Strip, where the restrictions affecting crossings and the 
entry of supplies led to widespread shortages of medicines 
and medical supplies. In some months, up to 58 per cent of 
essential medicines were out of stock, alongside significant

shortages in laboratories, dental, and other medical supplies. 
Delays in the entry of shipments resulted in the accumulation 
of medical supplies outside the Gaza Strip, including in Egypt 
and Jordan, where some items were damaged or expired 
while needs inside the Gaza Strip remained critical.
During the temporary ceasefire in early 2025, UNRWA was 
able to facilitate the entry of approximately 520 pallets of 
medical supplies into the Gaza Strip. However, the resumption 
of access restrictions led to renewed stockouts, including 
shortages of insulin and other life-saving medicines, 
requiring emergency support from WHO and other partners. 
By December 2025, WHO deliveries of approximately 720 
pallets of medicines significantly improved the supply 
situation, reducing stockouts to around 27 per cent of 
essential medicines and restoring partial availability across 
key treatment categories.
For the way forward, UNRWA will continue to strengthen its 
pharmaceutical services with a greater focus on improving 
clinical outcomes and cost-efficiency, particularly in the 
management of NCD. A key priority will be the gradual 
introduction of clinically appropriate newer-generation 
medicines for NCDs, subject to clinical guidance, 
procurement feasibility and available funding, especially 
for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), that offer improved 
glycemic control while remaining cost-effective within the 
Agency’s resource-constrained context.

Hospitalisation
Introduction
In 2025, UNRWA facilitated access to secondary and tertiary 
hospital care for Palestine Refugees who would have otherwise 
faced prohibitive financial barriers. Support is provided based 
on medical urgency, availability of alternative services, 
and economic vulnerability. Cases are prioritised based on 
clinical severity and household vulnerability to ensure that 
limited resources reach those most in need. These services 
complement Primary Health Care (PHC) services provided 
at UNRWA health centres. The Hospitalisation Support 
Programme (HSP) is designed to alleviate overwhelming health 
expenditures and support access to  quality care, especially 
when local systems are under strain. Through strategic 
partnerships with contracted hospitals and robust support 
mechanisms for urgent cases, our efforts are dedicated to 
protecting human dignity and preserving lives. UNRWA’s HSP 
facilitates access of Palestine Refugees to critical secondary 
and tertiary healthcare services across the Gaza Strip (during 
the first quarter of 2025 only), the occupied West Bank, Syria, 
Lebanon, and Jordan. Despite challenges in 2025, including 
conflict and financial constraints, our interventions enabled 
timely referrals for lifesaving treatments, safe deliveries, 
medically urgent cases, and essential outpatient services 
such as care for cancer patients.
The programme provides comprehensive secondary, 
tertiary, and mental health services, including emergency 
care, surgeries, intensive care, complex treatments, such 
as cardiovascular, neurological, paediatric, and oncological, 
as well as specialised outpatient services not available at 
UNRWA health centres. Equity is maintained by prioritising 
vulnerable groups, including Social Safety Net Programme 
(SSNP) beneficiaries, ex-Gazans, and patients without 
alternative insurance coverage. Services are delivered 
effectively through contracted hospitals selected for quality, 
accessibility, and affordability, while accountability is ensured 
through monitoring of referrals, claims, expenditures, annual 
audits, patient satisfaction surveys, and adherence to 
technical instructions.

unrwa department of health - annual report 2025
Services are delivered through governmental, private, 
and PRCS hospitals, with pricing and reimbursement 
agreements ensuring predictable cost coverage. Cost-
sharing varies by field and patient category. For instance, 
in Lebanon, secondary care coverage ranges from 90 
to 100 per cent of hospital invoices, tertiary care 60 per 
cent, and outpatient services 50 per cent, with additional 
support for SSNP patients and catastrophic cases through 
the Medical Hardship Fund (MHF). The Hospitalisation 
Management System supports comprehensive tracking of 
patients, expenditures, and outcomes. This support includes 
quarterly and annual reporting to analyse trends and inform 
programmatic decisions.
Programme outcomes
The Hospitalisation Support Programme (HSP) has led to 
improvements in access to care and is expected to support 
health outcomes across all fields. In terms of health outcomes, 
the programme has contributed to reducing complications 
among patients with chronic diseases, improving maternal 
and neonatal outcomes for women delivering in supported 
hospitals, and enabling early treatment and continuity of care 
for cancer patients and other vulnerable populations. The 
programme also improved access to hospitals that would 
otherwise be out of reach due to financial or geographic 
barriers, ensuring timely care for critical conditions. 
Additionally, coverage under the Social Safety Net Programme 
(SSNP) and for other high-risk groups has been maintained, 
strengthening equitable and prioritised access to services.
Inpatient hospitalization 
In 2025, 84,189 patients received hospitalisation support 
across UNRWA’s five fields of operation, in addition to 
6,553 patients admitted to UNRWA’s Qalqilya hospital in the 
occupied West Bank, compared with 104,008 patients in 
2024. This 19.1 per cent decrease was primarily due to the 
suspension of hospitalisation services in the Gaza Strip from 
April 2025, which substantially reduced the overall number 
of supported hospitalisations (by at least 25,077 patients). 
Excluding the Gaza Strip, hospitalisations in the remaining 
fields, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and the occupied West Bank, 
increased from 75,016 to 80,274 patients, an approximate 7 
per cent year-on-year increase. 
In Jordan, hospital admissions increased from 3,218 in 
2024 to 4,342 in 2025, representing a 34.9 per cent increase, 
indicating both growing demand and improved reporting. 
In Lebanon, admissions rose from 22,333 to 25,091, a 12.3 
per cent increase, reflecting stable demand for chronic and 
specialised care. In Syria, there was a modest increase from 
23,347 to 24,691, representing a 5.8 per cent rise, indicating 
continued reliance on UNRWA for secondary and tertiary 
care amid ongoing economic challenges. In the Gaza Strip, 
hospitalisations declined sharply from 28,992 to 3,915, an 
86.5 per cent decrease, following the suspension of services 
after Q1. In the occupied West Bank, admissions rose from 
19,612 to 26,150, an increase of 33.3 per cent, demonstrating 
consistent use of referral pathways and increased demand 
for services outside Qalqilya Hospital. Overall, agency-wide 
hospitalisations decreased by 7 per cent, from 97,502 to 
90,742, while all fields outside the Gaza Strip continued to 
show upward trends.

In 2025, UNRWA’s outpatient support focused on 
essential diagnostics, laboratory services, prosthetics,

financial sustainability.

Telemedicine 
Telemedicine is a critical component of UNRWA’s healthcare 
strategy, particularly in humanitarian and emergency 
contexts. Its origins trace back to the COVID-19 pandemic 
in 2020, when UNRWA first institutionalised telemedicine 
to maintain continuity of care amid lockdowns, movement 
restrictions, and social distancing requirements. During 
the pandemic, remote consultations proved essential for 
managing non-communicable diseases (NCDs), maternal 
and child health (MCH), and psychosocial support, 
establishing the foundation for the Agency’s current 
telemedicine framework.
Building on this foundation, telemedicine became a central 
lifeline during the 2023-2025 Gaza emergency, when health 
facilities were overwhelmed, movement was restricted, and 
vulnerable populations, including pregnant women, children, 
the elderly, and NCD patients, faced significant barriers to 
care.
Key impacts include:
• Ensuring continuity of care during conflict and 
displacement. 
• Reducing patient exposure to movement-related security 
risks.
• Alleviating pressure on overstretched health centres and 
healthcare staff. 
• Enabling timely clinical decision-making and early 
identification of high-risk cases. 
• Supporting equitable access to healthcare across the Gaza 
Strip, the occupied West Bank, including East Jerusalem, 
Jordan, and Syria. 
Telemedicine addresses logistical barriers, limited mobility, 
and access constraints, and supports continuity of care 
while reinforcing health system resilience in crises.
How Telemedicine Improves Health and Service
In 2025, UNRWA’s telemedicine services played a substantial 
role in  patient care and health system performance across 
the Gaza Strip, the occupied West Bank, Jordan, and Syria. 
A total of 563,938 consultations were delivered, accounting 
for approximately 30 per cent of all health centre visits in 
the Gaza Strip, underscoring the critical role of telemedicine 
in maintaining continuity of care despite conflict-related 
disruptions and restricted mobility. By the end of the year, 
telemedicine had been expanded to 25 health centres across 
the occupied West Bank, Syria, and Jordan, extending 
equitable access to healthcare for vulnerable populations, 
including children, elderly patients, and those with limited 
physical access to health facilities.
The quality and safety of services were reinforced by 
implementing standardised clinical protocols, ensuring 
consistency in diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up, and 
enabling timely identification of high-risk cases. This structured 
approach contributed to improved patient safety and more 
efficient use of clinical resources. Telemedicine also enhanced 
chronic disease management, enabling regular monitoring and 
follow-up for patients with diabetes, hypertension, asthma, 
and other non-communicable diseases. By providing these 
continuous, remote services, the programme helped prevent 
disease complications, improve treatment adherence, and 
promote better long-term health outcomes.

How data was reported and validated
Reporting under bombardments, incursions, and IPC Phase 
5 conditions: data quality amid a severe humanitarian crisis
The reporting channel and process for health data in the Gaza 
Strip, as of August 27, 2025, are outlined as follows: Data 
collection at the field level was based on paperwork, offline 
Excel sheets, WhatsApp texts, and screenshots of records. 
The data collectors were assigned focal points (e.g., nurses, 
physiotherapists, nutritionists, and medical officers) to 
collect raw data from health centres or medical points using 
the aforementioned offline tools in addition to episodically 
available online electronic medical records and other 
systems, such as the WHO’s EWARS. The first-level review 
was conducted by reporting focal points who performed a 
«spot check» of the division›s output report and EWARS data. 
They then uploaded the IMT sheet data to UNRWA’s protected 
SharePoint. The reporting team then conducts a second-level 
review of the files uploaded to the encrypted SharePoint. 
They hold review meetings to evaluate the reporting progress 
and identify anomalies or irregularities. The root causes of 
anomalies are addressed by the Gaza Field Office (GFO) staff. 
The health monitoring, evaluation, reporting, and learning 
officer (MERL) conducted the third level of data review and 
validation, which involved a comprehensive monthly review 
of input data, including triangulation, identification, and 
remediation of the root causes of potential exceptions. 
Additionally, the MERL officer compared the GFO input data 
with that of other stakeholders (e.g., the Health Cluster) for 
validation. Information dissemination was conducted at the 
GFO and HQ levels through weekly slide decks created by the 
reporting team. In addition, weekly situational reports and 
bi-weekly Gaza Crisis Reports were produced. Outputs were 
shared with external actors, such as the Health Cluster and 
WHO, for further review and action.

Surveillance in the Gaza Strip
The occupied West Bank, including East Jerusalem
Northern occupied West Bank
The escalations in the north of the occupied West Bank 
peaked in late 2024. According to UN reporting, demolitions 
and related displacement affected more than 4,000 
people, while the operation known as “Iron Wall” reportedly 
displaced approximately 40,000 people in January 2025, 
the largest displacement recorded in the occupied West 
Bank since 1967. At the same time, around 70 per cent 
of health facilities in the north were reported as closed or 
only partially functional. These developments substantially 
reduced access to essential health services, increased 
protection risks for displaced families, and heightened the 
need for emergency outreach and continuity of care.
UNRWA operates 43 comprehensive health centres (HCs) 
across the occupied West Bank, including two HCs in East 
Jerusalem, serving 917,942 registered Palestine Refugees. 
In late 2024, three main HCs in the northern area were 
forcibly shut down by the Israeli forces amid the escalation 
that began in mid-2024. The displaced people spread 
across various areas of the occupied West Bank, with the 
majority settling in villages near the closed HCs, such as 
Anabta, Attil, and Beit Qad. In response to this precarious 
situation, UNRWA established two emergency clinics and 
two emergency mobile clinics in Jenin and Tulkarm by the 
beginning of 2025. In the first three months of 2025, the 
UNRWA West Bank Field Office team mobilised resources 
and expanded the types of services provided by Qabatiya 
HC, adding new services to those already in place
In addition, a new emergency clinic was established in Al-
Saraya location near Tulkarm to meet the huge need for 
healthcare in the area. All these clinics were registered 
on e-health for immediate reporting and monitoring. The 
emergency clinics played a vital role in maintaining access 
to healthcare for vulnerable populations.

To identify locations where displaced populations settled, the 
West Bank field team contacted the UNRWA relief department 
and the local [KC38.1]municipalities. These sources of 
information were key during the early planning phase of 
the emergency response. In addition, the staff of nearby 
HCs began collecting information from visitors displaced 
from the three camps. Visitors were asked where they, their 
neighbours, and their relatives lived at the time, and about 
the characteristics of the displaced populations living around 
them (children, elderly). UNRWA also liaised with community 
committees and leaders to better understand the needs of 
displaced people. The UNRWA West Bank team selected the 
five locations with the highest numbers of displaced people. 
The team prioritised emergency clinic locations that were as 
safe and accessible as possible and offered the strongest 
catchment coverage. For instance, an emergency clinic was 
opened in the Korean Health Centre (rented) but later closed as 
it was close enough to Jenin and therefore unsafe for patients, 
especially amid the recurrent and expanded military operations 
around Jenin. The emergency clinic was moved to Jenin Sport 
Club. A similar example was the Tulkarm Emergency Clinic 
(Thenabeh), which was difficult to reach due to unreliable, 
expensive transportation and its proximity to the Tulkarm area 
affected by access and security constraints. Consequently, 
a new emergency clinic was established in the Al-Saraya 
area (Tulkarm Emergency Clinic 2), in addition to the one in 
Thenabeh. As many displaced people could not reach existing 
or emergency HCs due to long distances, high costs, or military 
operations, mobile clinics were deployed to these areas on 
specific days of the week as a solution. The establishment 
of new clinics and the selection of locations, as well as the 
movement of the mobile clinics, were coordinated with the 
UNRWA security department. Telemedicine was implemented 
to improve access to emergency care, particularly for patients 
with difficulty commuting and those not reachable by mobile 
clinics. 
The UNRWA West Bank Field Office team, in collaboration 
with and supported by headquarters in Amman, prepared a 
slide deck to disseminate information on technical and risk 
communication and to raise funds. The slide deck provided 
an overview of the health centres' status in the occupied 
West Bank, covering several domains: the number of medical 
consultations, emergency clinics, mobile clinics, surveillance, 
and data on UNRWA employees' commuting disruptions 
caused by roadblocks imposed by Israeli authorities. 
Moreover, an incident management team was formed using 
an IMT sheet to update, follow up on, and track the progress 
of actions and interventions. Weekly meetings were held to 
discuss updates on challenges and follow up on implemented 
actions. UNRWA undertook technically and operationally 
efforts to respond to the emergency and sustain health 
services. However, neither quantitative nor qualitative work 
has been done to examine how effective the actions were, 
especially for vulnerable patients such as pregnant women, 
infants and children, and people with morbidities.

East Jerusalem 
Where services exist but not for all: UNRWA’s role in 
addressing access gaps in East Jerusalem
Not all Palestinians with Israeli ID are covered by Kupat 
Holim, the Israeli public health insurance system. Kupat 
Holim and Palestinian governmental insurance do not 
cover several services, referrals, and medications. 
Patients with insurance pay a monthly subscription fee 
and a percentage of the medication and laboratory costs. 
Patients with NCDs suffer the most in terms of affording 
the cost of medications and laboratory services. Access 
to affordable family planning, ANC, and ophthalmology 
is severely limited. Most patients cannot afford 
transportation costs and cannot physically commute to 
health centres because of long distances and checkpoints. 
Services and medications are not continuously available 
in the Palestinian health centre. UNRWA clinics in East 
Jerusalem are easily accessible and provide free, holistic, 
and sustainable health services.

UNRWA’s health centres in East Jerusalem play an 
indispensable role for Palestine Refugees—both those 
holding Jerusalem (Israeli) IDs and those holding West Bank 
IDs—by filling critical gaps left by the Israeli and Palestinian 
governmental health systems. Four interrelated factors 
underpin their continued high utilisation: reliable medication 
supply, proximity and ease of access, affordability, and 
comprehensive, holistic care. Taken together, these factors 
explain why two-thirds of Jerusalem-ID holders and nearly 
all Shufat clinic visitors continue to seek UNRWA services 
despite formal eligibility for other health systems. If the 
recently passed Knesset laws were enforced to close the 
Al-Zawya and Shufat health centres, hundreds of vulnerable 
patients—many with complex chronic conditions, limited 
mobility, or low income—would lose their primary source of 
affordable, accessible, and comprehensive care.
In summary, UNRWA’s health centres in East Jerusalem 
are a critical  component of health security for Palestine 
Refugees. Closure or prolonged disruption of these services 
would risk worsening inequities in access, affordability, and 
quality of care for some of the most at-risk populations in 
the Occupied Palestine Territory.
Telemedicine in the middle of the escalation
The health system in the occupied West Bank, offers 
telemedicine services, which, in addition to being a growing 
global practice, are highly needed in the Palestinian 
context. Elderly patients and those with disabilities can 
face difficulties commuting to and accessing   HCs under 
ordinary circumstances. In the occupied West Bank, 
including East Jerusalem, the roadblocks and movement 
restrictions result in longer waiting times and detours 
to bypass roadblocks, checkpoints, and movement 
restrictions, which make it more difficult for these patients 
to reach the clinics. The healthcare workers in the clinics 
and HCs conduct phone consultations with patients who 
are unable to reach the HCs, record these consultations in 
e-health, and dispense medications to patients› relatives. 
Overall, 879 patients were serviced by both the emergency 
clinics and the preexisting HCs through telemedicine in 
2025 (January to June inclusive). Of these patients, 76 per 
cent were NCD patients, 15.8 per cent were outpatients, 
and 8.2 per cent were MCH patients.  
Evaluation of UNRWA’s Health Department emergency 
response in the Gaza Strip and the occupied West Bank
In response to the severe challenges following October 
2023, the UNRWA Health Department initiated a strategic 
collaboration with Johns Hopkins University to undertake 
a comprehensive and independent evaluation of its health 
response in the Gaza Strip and the northern part of the 
occupied West Bank, including East Jerusalem. This 
partnership reflects a deliberate shift toward evidence-
driven learning, aiming to document performance during 
crises and critically assess how services were sustained, 
adapted, and delivered amid extreme disruption.
The analysis places particular emphasis on service 
continuity, tracking key indicators such as consultations, 
maternal and child health services, vaccinations, and non-
communicable disease care, while exploring operational 
adaptations such as mobile clinics and alternative delivery 
mechanisms. Simultaneously, qualitative engagements 
aim to capture the lived experiences of providers and 
Palestine Refugees and communities, shedding light on 
determination, coordination, and system-level challenges. 
The findings of this evaluation are expected in the second 
quarter of 2026 and will serve as a critical milestone in 
reinforcing UNRWA›s commitment to accountability, 
learning, and the continuous strengthening of its 
emergency health operations.

The new EMR
UNRWA has decided to replace the current e-health system 
with a new Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system. The 
main aim is to enhance functionality to support additional 
business processes and patients› needs, as the e-health 
system requires continuous upgrades and integrations to 
deliver health services that meet these needs. 
  
The following weaknesses would be improved by 
implementing the new EMR:
• The current e-health system is based on old technologies 
dating back to 2009 and is prone to performance lags and 
other issues 
• Key functionality, required for the efficient provision and 
management of UNRWA healthcare services, is missing 
in the existing e-health system, such as: 
• An efficient and flexible ad-hoc reporting tool for 
administrative and epidemiological surveillance needs. 
• A restricted access to sensitive information, such as 
survivors and persons affected by abuse, and adopting 
the use of survivor-centred terminology in the context. 
• Clearly specified role-based access controls, standardised 
consent forms, and confidentiality procedures.
• Clinical documentation and workflow support for 
management of mental health, management of gender-
based violence (GBV), school health, and physiotherapy. 
• Comprehensive clinical decision support functionality. 
• Patient portal. 
• Expandable telemedicine service integrated in the EMR 
system to include all provided health services, which 
will reduce walk-in clients and address the overcrowding 
issues in clinics. 
Key Deliverables
• A modern and architecturally sound EMR system, 
high-performance, patient-centric, and sustainable, is 
implemented and rolled out in all 123 health centres 
Agency-wide by 2027. 
• At least 3000 clinical and administrative staff of health 
centres, field offices and HQ are trained to use the new 
EMR system; 
• Patient engagement portal is implemented, and e-health 
services are provided to Palestine Refugees;

I am the pancreas: a mother’s voice from Gaza must not be 
silenced
We are mothers from Gaza. My son has lived with type 1 
diabetes for eight years. Since the war began in October 
2023, caring for him has become a daily struggle for survival. 
With electricity cuts, limited food, and no stable access to 
insulin, I had to become his pancreas—playing the role of a 
health worker and protector. We are not alone. Through a 
WhatsApp group of over 40 mothers, we share life-saving 
tips and emotional support to manage our children’s diabetes 
in a collapsing healthcare system. These are our shared 
stories. We documented our lived experiences over 20 
months (October 2023 - July 2025) through conversations, 
messages, and voice notes exchanged in the group. This 
case narrative reflects our collective coping strategies and 
emotional burden of being under siege. We had to reduce 
the long-acting insulin from 32 to 10 units to make it last. On 
days when only one meal was consumed, rapid-acting insulin 
was skipped. Without power, we stored insulin in wet clothes, 
cooling bags, or neighbours’ fridges. Sugar has become a 
life-saving medicine, costing up to $100/kg, and is hidden for 
emergencies. One mother lost her daughter to hypoglycaemia 
due to a lack of sugar at home. We shared these tragedies in 
the group—not just as grief but as urgent lessons. We stayed 
up at night to monitor our children’s breathing. We told our 
daughters, “This sugar is for your brother. His life depends on 
it.” We became the pancreas of our children. In Gaza, where 
the health systems have collapsed, mothers are the last line 
of defence. Diabetes care in emergencies must support us 
with insulin, storage solutions, access to sugar, and mental 
healthcare. Our voices must be heard. Our children’s lives 
depend on this.

cold-chain incidents across the humanitarian supply chain

insulin is critical but highly challenging. In the Gaza Strip,

Gaza’s insulin emergency: conflict-related disruption and  
glycemic outcomes in type 1 and 2 patients (2023–2025)
This study offers the first operational evidence from the Gaza 
Strip showing how prolonged conflict and access restrictions 
have rendered insulin-dependent diabetes care highly 
unstable in the region. Without access to food, refrigeration, 
diagnostics, hygiene, or appropriate insulin, treatment 
becomes unsafe. Since March 2025, food access has been 
severely constrained, leaving patients reliant on canned food, 
while physicians are forced to adjust insulin dosing based 
on the only available type, often unsuitable amid severe 
malnutrition. The lack of diabetes-appropriate nutrition 
highlights a critical gap, as standard humanitarian responses 
may not be adequately equipped to meet the needs of this 
vulnerable population. This study examined insulin-treated 
patients with type 1 and 2 diabetes at UNRWA/Gaza with at 
least two HbA1c or glucometer records (excluding GDM and 
incomplete files), analysing how conflict-related malnutrition 
and diagnostic gaps affect care. It compares HbA1c trends, 
hypoglycemia, admissions, and treatment changes across 
the pre-crisis (≤ October 7, 2023), escalation (March –May 
2024), and peak crisis (January–June 2025) periods. Using 
UNRWA electronic health records, a retrospective cohort 
of approximately 2,300 insulin-treated patients with type 1 
and 2 diabetes was analysed across the three time periods. 
Patient records were combined with interviews to capture 
clinical trends and system adaptations. Worsening glycemic 
control, rising hypoglycemia, higher hospitalisation rates, and 
increased treatment improvisations are anticipated, including 
unsafe dose swaps, missed doses, and the use of oral agents 
driven by availability rather than clinical guidance. The final 
analysis is expected to be completed by October. This study 
provides critical evidence and underscores the urgent need 
to safeguard basic diabetes care to prevent iatrogenic harm, 
rising complications in patients with type 2 diabetes, and 
underreported NCD deaths. To avert avoidable mortality 
during crisis, diabetes care must evolve through adapted 
insulin protocols, inclusion of diabetic nutrition in NCD kits, 
practical diagnostic alternatives, and improved coordination 
between UN agencies and the pharmaceutical industry.
