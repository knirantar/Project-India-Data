The Situation Hub's analytical process uses a structured Human-in-the-Loop workflow that combines automated analysis retrieval
through GANNET Virtual Assistant, DFS's AI-powered humanitarian assistant, with expert review by the DFS analysis team. GANNET
automates the extraction and generation of crisis-related insights based on structured prompts, which are aligned with a tailored
analytical framework. These outputs are then reviewed, validated, and refined by analysts to ensure accuracy, contextual relevance,
and operational utility. The workflow is organized into four key phases: planning and setup, ongoing operations, quality assurance, and
system evolution, ensuring a balance between speed and analytical rigor.
Planning, Setup & Quality Control
In the planning phase, analysts develop a contextualized analytical framework, largely based on the Joint Intersectoral Analysis
Framework (JIAF), and convert it into structured prompts for AI-assisted data extraction. These prompts are refined over time to
improve output quality. Data sources are vetted for reliability, and translations are reviewed for accuracy. Using a centralized Content
Management System (CMS), analysts review and curate AI-generated outputs, validate them against external sources, and make
refinements to enhance clarity and relevance. The CMS enables collaborative editing and version tracking, ensuring outputs align with
humanitarian needs.

Analysts are crucial in identifying and correcting inaccuracies, especially in automated categorization. Future improvements aim to
enhance source credibility checks and sentiment analysis, further strengthening the reliability of the Situation Hub's outputs.

Summary
Myanmar has been in crisis since 1 February 2021, when the military (Tatmadaw) overthrew the democratically elected government.
This coup sparked widespread protests and armed resistance, leading to a severe political, economic, and humanitarian crisis. The
military regime, known as the State Administration Council (SAC), has responded with brutal force, resulting in thousands of civilian
deaths and widespread human rights abuses. As of March 2026, approximately 3.7 million people are internally displaced across the
country — a dramatic increase from roughly 300,000 pre-2021 — with overall displacement figures — including those who fled to
neighbouring countries — crossing 5.3 million. 
In attempt to seek international legitimacy, the military held general elections in three phases over December 2025 and January 2026
for the first time since the coup. The overwhelming majority of seats were won by the pro-military Union Solidarity and Development
Party (USDP) and saw SAC chairman Min Aung Hlaing elected as president in April 2026. The elections have been widely denounced as
neither free nor fair. Despite the transition to a quasi-civilian presidency, armed clashes have continued in multiple parts of the
country, including Sagaing, Magway, northern Shan, Kayah, Kayin, Kachin, Chin, and Rakhine. The Arakan Army (AA) now controls all
but three of Rakhine State's 17 townships, while resistance forces including the Karen National Liberation Army (KNLA) have
consolidated influence across parts of central and southeastern Myanmar. The UN's 2026 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan
(HNRP), released 10 December 2025, projects over 16 million people will require humanitarian assistance this year.
Recovery from the March 2025 earthquake remains slow, with an estimated 3.5 million tonnes of debris and reconstruction hampered
by insecurity, shortages of heavy machinery and labour, and limited access. Many affected communities, particularly in Magway,
Sagaing, and Mandalay, continue to live in temporary shelters, while essential services remain disrupted. Approximately 24,200 houses
were damaged (24% completely destroyed), and 132 health facilities were damaged — half in Mandalay.
The Rohingya crisis continues to worsen, with the junta's blockade of Rakhine preventing international aid delivery since late 2023,
leaving hundreds of thousands of IDPs with severely limited assistance. On 10 December 2025, a military airstrike destroyed Mrauk-U
General Hospital, killing at least 33 people and injuring 76-80 — one of the deadliest attacks on healthcare in the conflict. Chronic
poverty now affects over 80% of the population; the World Bank projects GDP will contract 2.0% in FY2025/26 with inflation remaining
above 20%, and market disruptions have deepened food insecurity nationwide. The 2026 HNRP appeals for approximately USD 890
million but reflects severely constrained funding and narrowed operational space, forcing agencies to prioritise only 2.6 million people
with the most severe needs for life-saving support.
Key Insights
1. Myanmar's humanitarian crisis continues to deepen amid persistent conflict, economic collapse, and the lingering impacts of the
March 2025 earthquake. An estimated 16 million people — nearly one in three citizens — are projected to require humanitarian
assistance in 2026, as conflict-driven displacement, natural disasters, funding shortages, and humanitarian access constraints
exacerbate vulnerabilities nationwide.
The 28 March 2025 earthquake remains a defining event in the humanitarian landscape. Official figures from SAC confirm 3,770 deaths
and over 5,000 injuries, with the Early Recovery Cluster reporting over 24,200 houses damaged (24% completely destroyed), 4,857
apartments affected, and 132 health facilities damaged. An estimated 3.5 million tonnes of debris blocks roads and markets, with
clearance impeded by shortages of heavy machinery and labour. Nearly three-quarters of the most affected townships continue to
face critical shelter and livelihood gaps — 64% of communities reported job losses and 30-31% reported damage to crops and
workplaces — while disrupted water systems (44% of groundwater sources contaminated) have fuelled outbreaks of cholera and
dengue.
2. Following the junta-run general elections held over December 2025 and January 2026, as well as ceasefires between the regime and
some ethnic armed organisations in northern Shan, the number of armed conflict incidents has gradually decreased through early
2026. Nonetheless, air and ground offensives continue in the Central Dry Zone, as well as Rakhine, Chin, Kayah, Kayin, and Kachin
States. The new government's 100-day roadmap announced on 21 April 2026, which invites resistance groups to enter peace talks and
to surrender, have not been widely accepted.

Economy
Current Economic Situation
The World Bank estimated earthquake damage at about USD 11 billion (around 14% of GDP) and projected a contraction in FY2025/26
with only a moderate rebound in FY2026/27 driven mainly by reconstruction spending [2]. Myanmar entered a severe fuel crisis in
March 2026, with gasoline roughly doubling and diesel nearly tripling, driving inflation to 24.6% in April 2026 and sharply raising
operating costs across the economy [4]. Fuel and urea fertilizer prices remained well above year-earlier levels—the urea reference
price fell from 157,000 MMK to 140,000 MMK in June 2026 but remained far above the prior season's 94,000 MMK—eroding fertilizer
profitability and prompting some farmers to reduce cultivated area or ploughings, which risks lower monsoon-season agricultural
output [1][3]. National rice prices were broadly stable in May 2026 (+2.2% year-on-year; +1.6% month-on-month) but showed
pronounced regional disparities, while maize prices rose on strong export demand ahead of Thailand's June duty-free window and
following Ayeyarwady Region announcements of direct maize exports to Thailand via Ranong Port [1][3]. Reopening of the
Myawaddy–Mae Sot Friendship Bridge No.2 and the Mandalay–Myitkyina trade route is expected to improve formal agricultural and
domestic trade connectivity, but border trade recovery more broadly remains slow amid security concerns and continued difficulties
restoring crossings [1][8]. Depreciation in the parallel-market kyat sustained upward pressure on import and transport costs, further
weighing on prices [46]. Development assistance for long-term projects has been suspended by some donors since the 2021 coup
and was publicly queried by the junta on 30 June 2026 when it urged resumption of development aid, underscoring ongoing policy
and financing constraints [15][7]. Large-scale illicit drug seizures and destructions—officials burned seized narcotics valued at nearly
US$600 million on 26 June 2026—remain an economic factor in parts of the country [35].
Conflict Economic Landscape
Persistent conflict and displacement are severely straining economic recovery and service delivery. UN agencies reported an
estimated 3.8 million internally displaced people (IDP) and overall 5.4 million forcibly displaced within or from Myanmar as of end-May
2026, increasing pressure on markets, labour pools, and host-community services [7][20]. By late 2025, roughly 45% of affected firms
had returned to pre-earthquake activity levels while only about half of affected households had begun repairs, even as major
transport corridors and market supply chains gradually resumed operation [2]. Recovery remains deeply uneven: elevated fuel and
fertilizer costs, trade disruptions, widespread displacement, and constrained humanitarian financing are undermining business
recovery, job creation, and household livelihoods—particularly for women and small traders who report rising transport and capital
costs and job losses [2][4]. Global attention and funding have been diverted by other crises, further stressing response and
reconstruction finance, while calls by the junta for resumption of development aid have met international scrutiny given rights
concerns [7][15]. Local fuel prices spiked in some areas—motorcycle petrol reached 15,000–20,000 MMK per litre in parts of Waw Ray
Township in March 2026—contributing to higher consumer prices and transport costs [16]. About 15% of households near the March
2025 earthquake epicentre reported loss of income or employment, reflecting the compounding impact of disaster and conflict on
livelihoods [2].
Stability of Currency and Bank System
The official exchange rate remained unchanged at 2,100 MMK/USD in May 2026 and has held at that level for three years, according to
the Central Bank of Myanmar [1]. The parallel (online) trading rate rose 1.2% year-on-year in May 2026, and exporters must convert 15%
of export earnings at the official rate and 85% at the online rate, creating a two-tier FX regime that affects export revenues [1].
Depreciation in the parallel-market MMK has sustained upward pressure on import and transport costs, further eroding purchasing
power and input affordability [46]. Myanmar imports over 90% of its fuel and holds no domestic refining capacity, making the
economy highly vulnerable to fuel price shocks: by end-May 2026, diesel was approximately 73% higher and petrol 83% higher than
pre-crisis levels, with diesel rising 108% and petrol 66% year-on-year, contributing to fuel scarcity and elevated transport costs across
the country [1][4]. These fuel and currency pressures compound fiscal and financial-system vulnerabilities already strained by
earthquake damage estimated at approximately USD 11 billion (~14% of GDP) and the projected economic contraction in FY2025/26
[4][2].

Politics
Governance Structures and Rule of Law
Since the 2021 coup, the military has consolidated power through tightly restricted elections and Min Aung Hlaing's move from army
chief to president, measures described as efforts to rebrand military rule [104][98][84]. Aung San Suu Kyi was transferred to a
designated residence in late April 2026 and the junta denied ASEAN's special envoy access, citing her ongoing sentences and stating
that meetings would only be permitted afterwards [104][98]. ASEAN has kept Myanmar effectively barred from high-level summits
under the FivePoint Consensus (5PC), while member states remain divided between calls for conditional engagement and continued
isolation [84][98]. The junta's denial of access to detained political leaders and diminished international oversight hinder prospects for
legal remedies and meaningful rule-of-law engagement [104][98][84]. Territorial control is fragmented, with ethnic armed
organizations (EAOs) and resistance forces administering services and authority in large areas, complicating accountability,
humanitarian access, and engagement with de facto authorities [70]. The UN OHCHR reported over 700 civilians killed in continuous
military attacks (mostly airstrikes) between August 2025 and 31 January 2026, and conflict monitors estimate total conflict-related
fatalities since the coup exceed 100,000 [95][108]. OHCHR further identified foreign actors supplying weapons and fuel as facilitating
rights violations and warned that cuts in international assistance have degraded emergency healthcare, medicine supply chains, and
health facilities [95].

administrative restrictions, and violence against humanitarian personnel [95]. UNHCR reported 5.4 million people forcibly displaced

prosecutions and detention of high-profile opposition figures. Aung San Suu Kyi is serving a sentence reported at approximately 18

Security And Stability
Security Overview
Armed conflict remains intense, with continued junta airstrikes and shelling causing civilian deaths and repeated attacks on
populated areas [151][175]. OHCHR reported over 700 civilians killed in continuous military attacks between August 2025 and 31 January
2026, mostly from airstrikes in the Northwest and Rakhine State [151]. As of end-May 2026, UNHCR reported an overall 5.4 million forcibly
displaced due to the Myanmar situation, with an estimated 3.8 million people internally displaced inside Myanmar, while humanitarian
access and funding have been strained by diverted resources to the Middle East [130]. Humanitarian access is further impeded by
denial of assistance, military blockades, NGO registration issues, and reductions in international support, degrading emergency health
services and medical supply chains [151]. Attacks on health care are being systematically tracked, and partners reported intensifying
impediments to health workers, patients, and supplies, prompting cluster-level AWD preparedness and incident reporting measures in
late June 2026 [128][151]. De facto authorities — notably the AA in Rakhine — have consolidated control over large areas and
increasingly perform governance functions including administration, policing, and taxation, complicating engagement and aid
delivery [164].
Cross-border pressures are also mounting: Bangladesh officially closed its border, with 1,893 people denied entry between 1 January
and 31 May 2026, biometric identification completed for 2,260 new arrivals in the same period, and over 1,000 arrivals recorded into
Mizoram, India, following instability and airstrikes in Northwest Myanmar [130]. Environmental and logistical risks are rising
simultaneously, with monsoon flood warnings issued for the Ayeyarwady Delta and Yangon areas, the Start Fund flagging flooding risk
in July 2026, and an emerging El Niño potentially increasing drought risk later in 2026 [173][147].
A fuel crisis that began in March 2026 has caused severe shortages and price spikes, constraining humanitarian operations and
protection services and producing notable gendered protection impacts: 84% of respondents reported feeling less secure due to
reduced public lighting, 68% reported delays in GBV and maternal referrals, 70% noted increased dependence of women on male
family members, and 52% reported women-led organizations were excluded from mitigation discussions [170][173][145]. Overall,
intensified hostilities, territorial fragmentation, constrained access, and supply shortages present major, overlapping threats to civilian
protection, aid-worker safety, and the continuity of humanitarian operations in mid-2026.

Demography
Demographic Overview
As of endMay 2026, UNHCR reported 5.4 million people forcibly displaced, including an estimated 3.8 million internally displaced inside

concentrated in regions including Sagaing, Rakhine, Chin, Kachin, Shan and Magway, while ACLED recorded over 1,200 distinct armed

In Bangladesh, UNHCR recorded 1,197,411 registered refugees as of 31 May 2026 (51.5% female), with approximately one million Rohingya
hosted across 16 camps in Cox's Bazar [199][209]. Between 1 January and 31 May 2026, 2,260 new arrivals were biometrically identified,
bringing the total identified since 2025 to 152,029 individuals (39,881 families); the biometric exercise issues family cards but does not
issue individual refugee documents [179][199][209]. During the same period, 1,893 people were reportedly denied entry to Bangladesh

Crossborder flows into India increased due to instability in Northwest Myanmar, notably in Chin and Sagaing, with more than 1,000
reported crossings into Champhai district, Mizoram; most new arrivals reside dispersed with relatives or host communities rather than
in centralized shelters [179]. Boat arrivals to Malaysia included a high proportion of children and unaccompanied or separated
children, prompting urgent protection, familytracing and communitybased care needs; UNHCR advocacy contributed to the release
and reunification of 15 people, predominantly children, in May 2026 [179].
Socio-Cultural
Socio-Cultural Overview
Local civil society and community-based actors—including women-led, disability-focused, and refugee-led organizations—have
assumed greater leadership in humanitarian response in Myanmar, driven more by necessity than by intentional localisation
processes, but operating in a severely restricted civic space where local voices are often sidelined by international actors and
decision-making is constrained by security and access limitations [223]. UNHCR has expanded partnerships to strengthen localized
response capacity, working with 16 national and nine international partners in Myanmar coordination efforts [245]. Regional AAP/PSEA
survey findings underscore that national and local NGOs are central to accountability efforts (46% of respondents were national/local
NGOs), but that coordination, feedback loops, and collective community engagement remain uneven, limiting meaningful community
participation in humanitarian decision-making [235][237].
Large-scale displacement is placing heavy pressure on local protection and social networks, with an estimated 3.8 million IDPs as of
early June/end-May 2026 and 5.4 million forcibly displaced within the broader Myanmar situation as of end-May 2026 [230][245].
Localized military offensives have compounded this pressure; for example, fighting around Mindat since May 2026 displaced over
17,000 people [268]. Rising violence in Rakhine—including repeated airstrikes and reported clashes involving the AA and Arakan
Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA)—has increased pressure on Rohingya communities, heightened intergroup tensions, and produced
fears of new cross-border displacement to Bangladesh [246][240].
Governance on the ground is fragmented: de facto authorities and ethnic armed groups increasingly perform administrative, justice,
and service functions, complicating interactions between communities, humanitarian actors, and formal/state actors, and shaping
local perceptions of legitimacy and safety [224]. Attacks on health services and reprisals against health personnel—including the
reported arrest and torture of a CDM-affiliated health worker on 19 June 2026—are eroding trust in service delivery and undermining
locally driven protection and health mechanisms [222][230].
Gender Dynamics
A June 2026 brief by the Myanmar GiHA Working Group—co-chaired by UN Women and UNFPA—found that the March 2026 fuel crisis is
deepening gender inequalities and constraining women's access to services, justice, and decision-making [219][218][217]. The analysis
draws on in-person consultations with 21 women-led organizations and an online survey of 63 women-led organizations, civil society,
9
and humanitarian actors conducted over two weeks in May 2026; respondents were predominantly women aged 36–50 with highest
representation from Mandalay, Yangon, Southern Shan, and Kachin [217]. Key findings include:
- 70% reported fuel shortages increased women's dependence on male family members [217].
- 56% reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit because of rising travel costs [217].
- 52% reported women-led organizations are not being engaged in fuel-crisis mitigation discussions, reducing women's influence
over decisions that affect them [217].
- 46% reported electricity and connectivity disruptions are limiting women's access to mobile money, cash transfers, and financial
services [217].
- 44% reported higher transport costs are affecting girls' school attendance or contributing to dropout [217].
The crisis is also driving harmful coping strategies: 62% reported increased household borrowing, while respondents also reported
labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%), survival sex for money (16%), and sex for food or fuel (8%) [217]. Women and girls are
carrying increased unpaid care burdens, and women sustain much of the frontline humanitarian response. The brief recommends
prioritizing fuel access for services women and girls depend on—including health, GBV referrals, ambulances, outreach, and mobile
services—and sustaining women-led organizations at the frontline of the response [219][217][218].
Intensifying conflict and cuts to international assistance have degraded emergency health provision and supply chains, further
limiting women's access to health and protection services in affected areas [230]. Regional AAP/PSEA findings note that gender and
social norms restrict women's access to formal feedback channels and participation—a barrier that is acute in conservative and
conflict-affected areas such as Myanmar [235].
Impact of Socio-Cultural Drivers on Aid
Socio-cultural barriers significantly shape both the delivery and uptake of humanitarian aid in Myanmar. Regional AAP/PSEA
assessments (April 2026) identify a trust deficit and multiple participation barriers—including language and literacy challenges,
gender and social norms restricting women's use of feedback channels, geographic remoteness, and fear of retaliation—all
undermining accountability and safe reporting in conflict-affected settings [237][235]. Reports of increased sexual violence linked to
trafficking and cyberscam compounds in the region, including cases in Myanmar, further compound stigma and barriers for survivors
seeking assistance [256].
Humanitarian guidance emphasizes adapting partnerships and funding to address these socio-cultural constraints—covering costs
for participation (transport, translation, staff time); deliberately engaging and resourcing women-led, disability-focused, and
grassroots groups; and sharing risk and mentorship to improve inclusion and social cohesion [223]. In practice, however, the
localization shift has been uneven: 52% of surveyed respondents reported that women-led organizations are not being engaged in
crisis-mitigation discussions, and coordination, feedback loops, and collective community engagement remain inconsistent
[217][235][237].
Environment
Environmental Conditions
Myanmar's geographic and climatic context creates compounding vulnerabilities across multiple dimensions. The country's inclusion
in the Hindu Kush–Himalaya region links it to broader shifts in mountain and watershed dynamics—changing rain patterns, increased
landslide risk, and altered river systems—that compound agricultural and water-resource vulnerabilities [286]. Regional ENSO outlooks
in late June–July 2026 indicate a likely El Niño/positive IOD through OND 2026, raising the probability of a drier-than-normal monsoon,
elevated drought risk later in 2026, and greater food, water, and WASH stress across affected areas [314][299][300]. Climate modelling
also warns of increased frequency of episodic flooding that could severely affect large Southeast Asian cities, explicitly naming
Yangon among urban centers vulnerable to such events from combined climate and sea-level variability [285]. Environmental
contamination from unregulated rare-earth and other mineral extraction in Kachin and Shan States has degraded water quality and
river sediments, creating toxic waste (notably arsenic) and bioaccumulation risks for riverine ecosystems and food chains in border
river systems, with calls for stronger regulation of operators [291].
Environmental Challenges
The ongoing conflict in Myanmar is compounding existing environmental and health vulnerabilities. Military airstrikes in April 2026 in
Mu Traw (Hpapun) District destroyed houses, a clinic, and a school, and damaged or destroyed productive assets including rice mills,
ploughing tractors, machine saws, diesel engines, and generators, forcing villagers to flee to nearby forests or across the border to
Thailand [319][322]. As of May 2026, displaced populations remained without shelter, food, or external support, and the combined
effect of infrastructure and asset destruction, displacement into forest areas, and constrained access to health and WASH services is
undermining livelihoods and increasing environmental health risks across affected communities [319][322]. The approaching
monsoon is further intensifying risks of acute watery diarrhoea (AWD), malaria, and dengue, while limited diagnostics continue to
delay detection and response efforts [300].
Environmental Risks and Mitigation
Forecasts and early-July models indicate heavy rainfall and high flood risk across Myanmar in the first two weeks of July, with GLOFAS
and NOAA projecting significant rainfall and the Department of Meteorology and Hydrology forecasting moderate to heavy rain [299].
The Start Fund monthly bulletin similarly flagged flooding in Myanmar as an anticipated hazard for early July [273].
10
IFPRI reported flood warnings for low-lying areas including the Ayeyarwady Delta, Bago, the Sittaung River Basin, and Yangon, with
fisheries authorities advising pond owners to take preventive measures against pond flooding and fish losses [272]. Short-term heat
risk was also forecast for late June–early July, with abnormally hot temperatures expected in parts of Myanmar during that period
[314]. A new El Niño phase is expected to develop in the coming weeks, raising medium-term drought risk even as short, intense rain
events continue to threaten floods and landslides, creating concurrent drought–flood exposure for agriculture and water supplies
[272][300]. Monsoon rains have already destabilized mining waste heaps: a late-June collapse of a slag/mine-waste heap in Hpakant
(Kachin) killed at least five people and left others missing, illustrating acute landslide and site-stability hazards at mining sites during
heavy rainfall [274][277][292]. In response, localized AWD outbreaks in Kachin and Rakhine have been rapidly managed through
coordinated health–WASH responses [278]. AWD preparedness workshops and use of the WASH Cluster's AWD Prioritization Tool
(tested in Kachin on 4–5 June) are being implemented, and UNICEF-led AWD preparedness actions and Health Cluster preparedness
activities are planned or ongoing to prioritize high-risk townships [300][278]. Multisectoral zonal contingency planning, Health Cluster
preparedness measures (surveillance, market and supplier assessments, training), and joint AWD action planning are underway to
strengthen readiness for flood- and monsoon-related health risks, while early-warning forecasts (GLOFAS/NOAA/MDMH) and Start
Fund alerts are being promoted to enable anticipatory action where feasible [300][278][299][273].
Conflict
Overview of the Ongoing Armed Conflict
The conflict began after the February 2021 military coup and has evolved into a fragmented civil war that monitoring groups estimate
has caused over 100,000 conflict-related deaths and produced more than 3.7 million IDPs [348][339]. Active fighting and intensified
aerial bombardment continue across multiple states and regions, notably Rakhine, Chin (Mindat and Kanpetlet), Kachin, Sagaing,
Magway and parts of Kayin, with frontline clashes and frequent airstrikes reported in late June 2026 [381][364][360]. Junta air
campaigns around Mindat intensified from mid-May and culminated in heavy bombardment on 29 June 2026 that killed three
children and involved dozens of bombing runs and multiple bombs in a single day [364][381]. The military has employed heavy
explosive ordnance—including fighter jets, Y-12 aircraft, large 300–500 lb bombs, rockets and reported cluster munitions—causing
wide-scale destruction of villages and civilian infrastructure in affected areas [345][339]. Attacks on health care have been sustained:
monitoring groups and the Health Cluster document roughly 1,900–1,940 incidents against health services since 2021 (about one
attack per day), with around 175 health workers killed, over 930 arrested, and health facilities damaged on at least approximately
506–509 occasions [344][350][360]. Destruction of clinics, schools and places of worship, and repeated bombardment have driven
large-scale displacement and localized flight to forests or across the Thai border, with many displaced communities reporting little or
no external assistance [345]. Humanitarian access is constrained by militarization of civilian areas, troops billeting in homes, local
monopolization of food supplies by junta actors, and restrictions on returns, aggravating livelihood and assistance shortfalls
[366][379]. The conflict's fragmentation—ACLED records over 1,200 distinct armed groups—complicates ceasefire prospects, local
command-and-control and the delivery of principled humanitarian assistance [339].
Conflict Stakeholders
The primary state actor is the military regime—now a military-backed civilian administration since the general elections—, facing a
highly fragmented array of non-state opponents: pro-democracy PDF units, numerous EAOs (including the AA, Kachin and Karen
armed groups), and newly formed federal-unit forces such as the Magway Federal Unit and Chin resistance formations
[353][342][359]. External involvement includes Chinese diplomatic and political support that analysts say has shifted dynamics in
favour of the junta and facilitated truces with some ethnic armies, while the junta employs Russian- and Chinese-supplied aircraft in
airstrikes [342]. Non-state actors are increasingly performing governance functions in areas they control—notably the AA, which has
consolidated authority across much of Rakhine and runs local administration, policing and taxation, complicating humanitarian
access and future planning for returns or reconstruction [353]. Since mid-May, coordinated resistance operations have been reported
in southern Chin State, where CPU/CPA, ULA/AA and Magway Federal Unit forces have jointly resisted a large junta offensive around
Mindat and Kanpetlet, with heavy clashes and significant junta troop deployments noted [359]. In late June 2026, internal resistance
tensions surfaced: on 28 June, over 90 pro-revolution and civil society organizations issued a joint call for disputes to be settled
non-violently after clashes between an NUG-affiliated PDF unit and the People's Liberation Army in Mandalay that reportedly killed at
least four PDF fighters [335]. The junta has also sought to link some armed groups' resistance to illicit financing from narcotics; state
authorities publicly destroyed large drug seizures on 26 June 2026—over 50 tonnes burned and valued at nearly US$600 million—citing
ties between drug profits and armed group revenues [384]. At the international level, the UN Special Envoy visited Myanmar and a new
UN Special Rapporteur on human rights was appointed in April 2026, while UNHCR reported an estimated 3.8 million internally
displaced by end-May 2026 [331].
Peace Negotiations
Peace efforts are effectively stalled: the junta has repeatedly ignored ASEAN's 5PC and refused ASEAN requests to meet Aung San Suu
Kyi, including a formal denial at a 30 June press conference [375]. ASEAN remains officially united on the 5PC but is frustrated, with
several member states continuing deeper bilateral engagement with Myanmar while maintaining restrictions on junta participation in
ASEAN summits [377][375]. Regional and international influence is polarising the process: China has deepened ties with the
junta—backing truces with two powerful ethnic armies and providing political and material support—while Myanmar's leader carried
out recent diplomatic visits to China and India to secure agreements [342][348][377][338]. Opposition fragmentation and recent
intra-resistance clashes further complicate mediation prospects, with more than 90 pro-revolution and civil society groups on 28 June
urging non-violent resolution after clashes between NUG-affiliated PDF and the People's Liberation Army in Mandalay Region that killed
at least four PDF fighters [335].

The UN has engaged diplomatically—its Special Envoy, Julie Bishop, visited Myanmar for the first time since the late 2025-early 2026
general elections were held— but access and implementation constraints limit tangible progress toward a negotiated settlement
[331].
Conflict Outlook
Prospects for rapid negotiated de-escalation are limited: major resistance blocs have rejected the junta's peace overtures, and the
multiplicity of de facto authorities—including the AA exercising consolidated governance across much of Rakhine—complicates any
single, nationwide settlement [342][353]. External dynamics are likely to sustain conflict momentum: China's support and
Beijing-backed truces have strengthened the military's position, while recent high-level outreach—including Min Aung Hlaing's visit to
Laos on 3 July 2026—signals growing regional engagement that may reduce Myanmar's isolation without resolving fighting or unifying
pressure for ceasefire [342][377]. Economic shocks and climate risks compound humanitarian pressures: the March 2026 fuel crisis
has sharply raised transport and input costs and inflation, and El Niño/flood warnings threaten agricultural production and livelihoods
in the coming months [371][363]. Over one in five people face acute food insecurity [342], and protection concerns are widespread:
- 56% of surveyed organisations reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit because of rising travel costs [371].
- Common coping concerns include increased borrowing (62%), labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%), survival sex
(16%) and sex in exchange for food or fuel (8%) [371].
Overall, the balance of factors—ongoing airstrikes and attacks on services, countrywide fragmentation, external state support for the
military, and weak prospects for a unified political settlement—point to a high risk of conflict intensification, further displacement and
humanitarian need in the coming months, unless there is a major shift in external pressure or a credible, inclusive settlement
[350][342][377][371].
Displacement
Displacement Overview
UNHCR reported large-scale, ongoing internal and cross-border displacement driven by escalating hostilities and protection risks in
Myanmar [398][399]. As of end-May 2026, approximately 3.8 million people were IDPs within Myanmar, part of a regional total of 5.4
million forcibly displaced within the Myanmar situation [398][399]. About 1.85 million Rohingya remain stateless within the Myanmar
situation (reference October 2025 figures) [398].
Cross-border movements reflect significant displacement pressures across the region. The Bangladesh border remains officially
closed, with 1,893 people denied entry in May 2026 alone (bringing total denials since 2024 to 14,380), while biometric exercises
identified 2,260 new arrivals during January–May 2026 and a cumulative 152,029 individuals (39,881 families) identified since 2025
[398][394]. Refugee registration in Bangladesh remains suspended since May 2022, and biometric identification does not issue
individual refugee documents [398]. Arrivals into India rose amid intensified fighting, with over 1,000 people reported crossing into
Champhai district (Mizoram), many residing with relatives or host communities rather than centralized shelters, producing dispersed
border-area populations [398]. Malaysia reported boat arrivals with a high proportion of children, raising protection concerns [398].
UNHCR also reported small-scale spontaneous returns of Rohingya from Bangladesh to Rakhine, cautioning that returnees face
detention, exploitation and other protection risks [398].
Localized militarization further drove displacement, as in Dooplaya District (January–March 2026) in Kayin, where villagers were forced
to flee to farm huts, forests or across the Thai border, with some unable to return by March 2026 due to ongoing military presence and
house burnings [425][405]. Forced displacement is protracted, linked to trends since 1 February 2021 and ongoing airstrikes, shelling,
forced recruitment, extortion and arbitrary detention [398].
Living Conditions of the Displaced Population
As of end-May/early June 2026, the scale of displacement—3.8 million IDPs internally and 5.4 million forcibly displaced
regionally—places heavy strain on protection and assistance capacities [398][409]. UNHCR and partners reported delivering core
relief items to over 74,000 people, shelter support to around 3,000 people (559 families) and multi-purpose assistance to
approximately 3,300 people (1,146 families) by end-May 2026 [398]. Despite these efforts, funding shortfalls and diverted resources are
compounding gaps in shelter, WASH and basic services, leaving needs unmet across high-severity areas such as Sagaing and
Rakhine and reducing durable livelihood opportunities for displaced households [398][409].
Health services are disrupted and at risk of outbreaks, with clusters noting diagnostics shortages delaying detection, measles
preparedness needs in Rakhine and Chin, acute watery diarrhoea (AWD) risks ahead of the monsoon, and rising malaria among
pregnant women in Southeast Myanmar, despite continued sexual and reproductive health (SRH) and mobile clinic efforts [409].
Intensifying insecurity and militarization—including air strikes and checkpoints that caused displacement and civilian casualties in
April–June 2026—are undermining safety, restricting movement to markets and health facilities, and damaging livelihoods [430][405].
The March–June 2026 fuel crisis and soaring transport costs have further eroded economic stability and mobility, limiting referrals,
outreach and access to care [400][402]. Administrative hurdles, including NGO registration requirements, have forced some partners
to cease activities entirely, further impeding service delivery to populations in need [409].
Drivers of Displacement
Armed conflict—encompassing escalating airstrikes, shelling, forced recruitment, extortion, arbitrary detention, family separation and
restrictions on movement—is the primary driver of displacement in Myanmar [398][409].
12
Humanitarian access constraints are severe: of 251 townships assessed, 47% face high access constraints and only 8% are relatively
accessible, with Sagaing and Rakhine among the worst-affected areas accounting for over one million people in need, and access
difficulties increasing in dozens of townships [409].
Economic shocks have compounded displacement pressures. A severe fuel crisis beginning in March 2026 roughly doubled gasoline
prices, nearly trebled diesel costs and drove inflation to approximately 24.6% in April 2026, raising food and input costs, undermining
livelihoods and slowing recovery [400][410]. These pressures are gendered: surveys of local organizations found 56% reporting women
losing jobs or being forced to quit due to rising travel costs, with households turning to increased borrowing (62%) and reporting
incidents of labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%) and survival sex (16%) as coping strategies [400].
Protracted persecution and statelessness—most notably affecting the approximately 1.85 million stateless Rohingya—alongside
small-scale spontaneous returns from Bangladesh that expose returnees to detention and exploitation, further compound
displacement drivers [398]. Pre-existing environmental shocks, including the March 2025 earthquake that left extensive damage,
continue to interact with conflict and economic pressures to impede returns and drive secondary displacement [410]. UNHCR and
Protection Cluster assessments additionally note rising psychosocial needs and gaps in referral services, notably in Kachin State, while
livelihood priorities remain acute in Shan and Kayah [398].
Integration
Integration prospects for displaced populations inside Myanmar and in host countries remain severely constrained. Within Myanmar,
fuel and transport price rises, insecurity and active combat operations have sharply limited economic opportunities; residents report
soaring prices and a lack of jobs in towns such as Kyaukphyu in Rakhine, while high petrol costs have limited travel to markets and
healthcare in areas like Dooplaya District in Kayin [436][405]. Women are disproportionately affected, with 56% of surveyed
respondents reporting women losing jobs or being forced to quit because of rising travel costs, and other harmful coping
strategies—including increased debt and labour exploitation—rising as incomes contract [400].
Education and skills support remain limited by conflict and displacement: students in some areas were unable to study due to fear of
airstrikes, and UNHCR's 2026 planning for Rohingya responses explicitly prioritizes education, skills and capacity-building as part of
durable solutions and protection objectives [405][394]. Protection Cluster assessments identified growing psychosocial support needs
and gaps in referral services, notably in Kachin State, while earthquake recovery gains are being undermined by rising fuel and input
costs that slow livelihood recovery and service restoration [398][405][410].
In host countries, policy environments present a mixed picture. Malaysia launched a Refugee Registration Document Programme in
2026, with stakeholders signaling interest in linking documentation initiatives to more regulated employment pathways, while UNHCR
and partners continue advocacy on documentation and lawful employment in Thailand [398]. Bangladesh has suspended regular
refugee registration since May 2022, relying on biometric family-card exercises that do not grant individual identity documents, with
UNHCR continuing to advocate for full registration [395][398]. Social cohesion is under pressure: rising misinformation and negative
public narratives in Malaysia since late May 2026 have increased anxiety and protection risks for refugees, while competition over
scarce resources is driving tensions in displacement-affected areas of Myanmar [398][436].
Humanitarian Access
Humanitarian Access Overview
OCHA's May 2026 Humanitarian Access Severity Monitoring found that, of 251 townships, 47% face high access constraints and only 8%
were relatively accessible, with the most difficult-to-reach townships concentrated in Sagaing and Rakhine and together accounting
for over 1 million people in need [451]. Compared to October 2025, the situation worsened in 21 townships and access challenges
increased in 53, driven by checkpoint impediments, administrative restrictions—including NGO registration requirements imposed by
de facto authorities—and violence against humanitarian personnel and assets, causing some partners to cease activities [451].
OHCHR and monitoring groups recorded sustained attacks on civilians and attacks on health care—driven by airstrikes and shelling in
Northwest and Rakhine—which have further reduced safe humanitarian operating space; OHCHR documented over 700 civilians killed
in continuous military attacks between August 2025 and 31 January 2026 [451][460]. UNHCR and partners report that reductions in
international funding and the Gulf-driven fuel crisis have forced programme cuts, raised operational costs, and increased reliance on
national and local partners, area-based/zonal coordination groups, and trusted intermediaries for remote or technical support
[450][452][451]. Humanitarian delivery modalities currently include camp-based distributions (including LPG provision jointly by
UNHCR and IOM), solarization of water systems, and market-based supply chains, but these modalities are vulnerable to
monsoon-related road blockages, supply-chain disruptions, and fuel shortages [452][463][458][451]. Accountability and protection
mechanisms are under strain: regional AAP/PSEA surveys and cluster partners report AAP/PSEA coordination operating under heavy
pressure and call for administrative simplification, dedicated duty-of-care resources, and protected funding for AAP/PSEA functions
[442][444][451].
Barriers to Humanitarian Access
Humanitarian access in Myanmar is heavily constrained by active conflict (airstrikes and shelling), checkpoint and administrative
impediments, rising fuel and transport costs, restrictive border and political measures, onerous donor/agency compliance that
excludes many local partners, surveillance and community distrust, and direct risks to health facilities and aid workers including
confiscation of supplies and detentions [451][440][458][452][460][442]. Strict administrative and compliance requirements prevent
many grassroots health and humanitarian actors from being contracted or funded, as partners struggle with legal registration,
opening bank accounts, complex English-language reporting formats, and requests for operational details—including GPS
coordinates—that pose security risks [451].

Aid operations are further hampered by harassment and targeting of staff and assets, including arrests/detentions, checkpoint
seizures, confiscation of medical equipment and relief consignments, and the need to conceal or split shipments to avoid
confiscation—practices that raise costs and reduce delivery frequency and coverage [440][460]. Rising fuel prices and shortages,
linked to broader regional instability, have driven up transport and operational costs, constrained referrals and mobile services, and
reduced the reach of services in remote areas [458][452][453]. Fuel- and mobility-related barriers disproportionately affect women's
safety, livelihoods, and participation, with reports that fuel shortages have increased women's dependence on male household
members and that women-led organizations are often excluded from crisis-mitigation discussions [445]. Community engagement
and feedback channels are also under strain: collective AAP mechanisms are scaled back, feedback loops remain weak, and fear of
retaliation or surveillance leads many communities to refuse sharing detailed beneficiary IDs, undermining needs assessment and
targeting [442][444][440]. Cross-border access to Bangladesh remains constrained: between 1 January and 31 May 2026, 1,893
individuals were reportedly denied entry (14,380 denied since 2024), while 2,260 newly arrived people were biometrically identified
through a government-joint exercise (total identified since 2025: 152,029), and regular refugee registration remains suspended
[452][453][463].
Humanitarian Access Gaps
As of 8 June 2026, an estimated 3.8 million people were internally displaced in Myanmar according to UNHCR, with separate reporting
indicating some 5.4 million people forcibly displaced within the Myanmar situation as of end-May 2026 [451][463][450]. Active
frontline fighting and intensified airstrikes have sharply constrained humanitarian access across multiple states—notably Chin
(including heavy combat around Mindat and Kanpetlet since mid-May), Kachin, Sagaing, Shan, Kayah, and Rakhine—with sustained
offensives and heavy clashes reported in late June 2026 [483][484][463]. Attacks on health care and related incidents recorded
between 10–23 June 2026 further limited medical service delivery and safe access for responders [460]. Critical unmet needs affect
IDPs, Rohingya refugees and asylum-seekers at borders, women and girls who face sharply reduced access to health, justice, and
local services due to the March 2026 fuel crisis and reduced capacity among women-led organizations, and separated or
unaccompanied children among recent boat arrivals [463][454][456]. Thai authorities reportedly deported Rohingya before UNHCR
access and restricted shelter access for Rohingya children [463]. Sectoral shortfalls include disrupted health referrals and
psychosocial support (notably gaps in Kachin), insufficient livelihoods assistance in Shan and Kayah, and worsening food-security
pressures driven by fuel scarcity and rising transport and input costs [463][492]. Humanitarian coordination and AAP/PSEA
mechanisms are operating at reduced capacity, with local and national actors increasingly shouldering response leadership amid
stretched resources [444][440]. UNHCR and partners have expanded partnerships (16 national and nine international partners) and
advocated for resumption of full refugee registration to improve reach to hard-to-reach areas, but border denials, protection risks,
and service gaps remain unresolved [463]. Anticipatory hazards—including forecast flooding in July 2026 and an emerging El Niño
increasing drought risk—threaten to further restrict access and amplify humanitarian needs [499][492].
Information, Communication and AAP
Information Landscape
Humanitarian information on Myanmar in late June–early July 2026 is produced by UN clusters and agencies, local civilsociety
monitors, and regional AAP/PSEA bodies that publish regular situation and cluster bulletins and incident reports [512][506][545][503].
UN sources document high displacement and ongoing response activity: UNHCR reported 5.4 million forcibly displaced within the
Myanmar situation as of endMay 2026, while the Health Cluster reported 3.8 million internally displaced as of 8 June 2026, and UNHCR
and partners delivered core relief items to over 74,000 people alongside shelter and multipurpose assistance in 2026 [506][512].
Coordination and datasharing occur through cluster structures and interagency mechanisms — UNHCR leads Protection and Shelter
coordination in Myanmar, the Rohingya Coordination Team (RCT) was introduced in January 2026 for the Bangladesh response, and
joint UNHCR–WFP helpdesks and Health Cluster bulletins are used to share assessments and strengthen complaint/feedback handling
at distribution points [506][517][523][512]. Local monitoring organizations provide communitygathered incident data—including
trained community researchers and detailed incident logs—which supplement UN/cluster reporting, but validation and verification are
constrained by access, security incidents (including attacks on health services), and administrative restrictions reported in May–June
2026 [545][536][512]. Seasonal and anticipatory information (WMO/ENSO analysis, Start Fund risk bulletins) was circulated in July 2026
to inform preparedness and early action, offering meteorological forecasting inputs that humanitarian actors can use alongside
operational needs data [530]. Overall, the information landscape is multisourced and operationally active but hampered by
restricted humanitarian access, security risks, and weak feedbackloop closure that reduce the reach, timeliness, and perceived
reliability of information for affected populations and frontline responders [512][545][500].
Communication Channels
Information is exchanged via monitored public social media and opensource monitoring, formal online reporting platforms (notably
WHO's Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care), online surveys and Kobo questionnaires, and communitylevel awareness
sessions and complaints/feedback mechanisms including inperson consultations [536][505][512][506][502]. Use of mobile and
digital platforms is undermined by geographic remoteness and a digital divide, fuel and connectivityrelated disruptions that limit
access to mobile money and services, fear of retaliation in conflict areas, and administrative and reporting barriers—including legal
registration requirements, Englishlanguage reporting formats, and risks from sharing GPS coordinates—that can prevent partners
from engaging or reporting safely [500][502][518][512]. To improve timeliness and reporting despite these constraints, clusters and
agencies have promoted targeted tools and trainings, including AWD prioritization workshops, Kobo questionnaires, SSA awareness
sessions, and community feedback and complaints awareness initiatives [512][505][506].

heavy monsoon rains and flooding for early July 2026 risk further isolating communities and disrupting communications and access
to information [530]. Partners and clusters have urged strict data anonymization, use of trusted intermediaries, and inclusion of
security and dutyofcare resources to mitigate these risks, as recommended in the Myanmar Health Cluster bulletin of 30 June 2026
[512].
Misinformation and Rumors
Surveys and humanitarian assessments document a clear trust deficit and the prevalence of rumours in conflictaffected settings in
Myanmar. Communities have been reported as sceptical that feedback will lead to change and fearful of retaliation, limiting reporting
to formal channels, based on a survey conducted in April 2026 [500]. Perceptions that organisations or partners are politically
affiliated—and the consequent scrutiny and operational risks for local staff—have been reported as drivers of harmful rumours and
operational obstacles in the country [504]. As of 30 June 2026, intensifying insecurity, administrative impediments to NGO registration,
and severely restricted access have reduced outreach and community engagement, further aggravating miscommunication around
assistance and information flow [512]. In the Rohingya response during April–May 2026, UNHCR and partners rolled out messaging
campaigns, joint WFP–UNHCR helpdesks at distribution points, and strengthened complaintandfeedback mechanisms to address
community questions and reduce confusion during changes to foodassistance targeting [523][517]. Regional AAP/PSEA findings from
April 2026 call for simpler, contextualized feedback tools, stronger links between feedback channels and safe PSEA referral pathways,
and peer learning to restore credibility of information channels—measures applicable to Myanmar where collective AAP mechanisms
continue to operate but are under heavy pressure from access, security, and connectivity constraints [500][503]. Humanitarian
partners have also recommended strict data anonymization, use of trusted intermediaries, and inclusion of security and dutyofcare
resources (evacuation plans, psychosocial support, safety reserves) to rebuild trust and protect field staff and affected communities
[512].
Accountability Needs and Transparency
Accountability mechanisms exist in Myanmar but are operating under heavy pressure from access, security, and connectivity
constraints, and several interagency AAP coordination platforms have been suspended or scaled back since 2025 [503][500]. A
regional survey captured the following:
- 81% of surveyed respondents reported implementing AAP-related activities across sectors [500].
- 97% reported that a PSEA policy is in place [500].
- 43% reported participation in an AAP or community engagement working group, indicating weak collective coordination [500].
UNHCR reported strengthening response capacity through partnerships with 16 national and nine international partners and
expanding access to assistance during April–May 2026 as part of localization efforts [506][501]. Health Cluster and WHO actions in late
June 2026 included plans to increase local partner roles in the 2027 HNRP, localization workshops held 16–19 June 2026, and proposals
to preposition NCD supplies and include NCD questions in emergency assessments [512]. However, many local actors remain unable
to be contracted or fully participate because of restrictive registration, contracting, and reporting requirements, with partners flagging
complex English reporting formats and demands for GPS coordinates as security and operational barriers [512]. Womenled
organizations were consulted in May–June 2026 on the fuel crisis, and the Myanmar GiHA Working Group (cochaired by UN Women
and UNFPA) recommended flexible, multiyear funding, direct funding to womenled organizations, and dutyofcare measures to
sustain frontline gender services [502][514][515]. Learning briefs and surveys note that increased local leadership in Myanmar is often
driven by necessity rather than deliberate localization, with smaller grassroots and marginalized groups frequently sidelined and
collective AAP coordination limited [504][510]. Attacks on health care and military access restrictions have further eroded community
trust and impeded feedback and reporting channels, prompting calls to strengthen surveillance and reporting systems for attacks on
health and to protect communitybased accountability mechanisms [512][536]. Regional recommendations emphasize funding
AAP/PSEA as core operational functions, investing in shared feedback and referral systems, and supporting national actors to lead
AAP/PSEA working groups to close these gaps [500][503].

Legal and Policy
Legal and Policy Framework
The UN Special Envoy visited Myanmar and the UN Human Rights Council appointed a new Special Rapporteur on Myanmar in April
2026, reflecting ongoing UN-level political and human-rights engagement [566][568]. ASEAN's 5PC remains the bloc's stated
framework but has gone unimplemented, and ASEAN has blocked Myanmar from summits since late 2021; the junta also denied
ASEAN's requests to allow access to Aung San Suu Kyi, signaling refusal of regional scrutiny even as some member states have
increased bilateral engagement [586][574][584]. Myanmar's leader Min Aung Hlaing made state visits to China, India and Laos in
June–July 2026, securing multiple agreements—at least 18 with China—and signing memoranda of understanding with Laos
[584][601]. Japan has suspended new development assistance to Myanmar since the February 2021 coup, and Human Rights Watch
urged Tokyo on 30 June 2026 not to resume development aid while junta abuses and obstruction of humanitarian aid persist [588].
UNHCR leads the Protection and Shelter, Land and Site Coordination Clusters, and has strengthened regional mechanisms such as the
Rohingya Coordination Team for the Bangladesh response, but UN agencies face severe access constraints that limit implementation
of international response frameworks [568][566][570]. Commentators and analysts have consistently emphasized the absence of an
agreed international framework for engaging de facto authorities that exercise effective control over territory—a gap that complicates
legal, access, and reconstruction planning in Myanmar [565].
Humanitarian Impact of Legislation
De facto authorities' control over NGO registration has forced some humanitarian partners to cease activities, restricting legal
operating space for local and international actors [570]. Administrative restrictions, checkpoint impediments, and violence against
humanitarian personnel and assets left 47% of 251 townships with high access constraints and only 8% relatively accessible, with
access worsening in 53 townships as of May–June 2026 [570]. Aid workers face rising arrests, detentions, checkpoint harassment, and
digital surveillance, while requirements to collect detailed beneficiary identification deter community cooperation due to fears of
surveillance [571]. Attacks on health care have been pervasive and systematic since the 2021 coup: open-source monitoring recorded
roughly 1,939–1,943 incidents of violence against or obstruction of health care through mid-2026, including at least 175 health workers
killed, over 930 arrested, and health facilities damaged on at least 509 occasions, with 65% of attacks attributed to the
military—severely undermining service delivery and protection [581][585][572]. Cross-border legal and policy barriers further constrain
asylum and freedom of movement: Bangladesh reported 1,893 denied-entry incidents between 1 January and 31 May 2026 (14,380
denials since 2024), and individual refugee registration has been suspended since May 2022, with biometric family-card exercises
issuing family cards but not individual refugee identity documents [575][582]. UNHCR and partners are supporting improved access to
civil documentation and lawful employment pathways in parts of Southeast Myanmar and in neighbouring countries, while national
initiatives such as Malaysia's 2026 Refugee Registration Document Programme and restrictive practices in Thailand — including
deportations and limited access to government shelters for Rohingya children — are reshaping protection and labour rights for
refugees [568][575][582]. Reduced international funding and diversion of resources have compounded legal and administrative
barriers by forcing programme cuts, increasing reliance on local actors, and eroding protection and service continuity [566][570].
Humanitarian partners call for administrative simplification, strict data-anonymization and confidentiality protocols, and principled,
conditional engagement with authorities in line with international humanitarian law [570][565].
International Agreements Impacting the Crisis
The OHCHR reported over 700 civilians killed in continuous military attacks between August 2025 and 31 January 2026, and stated that
foreign actors supplying weapons and fuel have facilitated human rights violations and denial of humanitarian assistance,
highlighting contradictions between some states' policies and international protection obligations [570][585]. UNHCR reported 5.4
million forcibly displaced people and an estimated 3.8 million internally displaced within Myanmar by end-May 2026 [568][566].
Human Rights Watch urged governments—notably Japan—to keep development aid suspended and to tighten targeted sanctions
against the junta [588]. UN agencies and humanitarian coordination mechanisms remain engaged but face severe access
constraints that limit implementation of international response frameworks and delivery of assistance across large parts of the
country [568][570].
Limitations of Current Policies
The March–June 2026 fuel crisis exposed significant policy and funding fragilities: the World Bank and the Myanmar GiHA Working
Group reported sharp fuel price and inflation spikes—with gasoline roughly doubling, diesel nearly tripling, and inflation reaching
approximately 24.6% in April—while 56% of surveyed actors reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit, and women-led
organizations face rising operating costs requiring flexible, predictable funding [564][567]. Accountability and community feedback
mechanisms have been scaled back since 2025 and remain uneven in Myanmar, weakening AAP and PSEA implementation, as well as
the closure of feedback loops with affected communities [590]. At the international policy level, there is no coherent framework for
engaging de facto authorities, creating a policy vacuum that complicates planning for humanitarian access, reconstruction, and
voluntary refugee return [565]. Partners have cut programmes due to a combination of access, funding, and operational limitations,
while the absence of durable legal solutions—including suspended refugee registration and widespread border denials—leaves
displaced populations without adequate legal protection or pathways to durable solutions [566][570][575][582].

Sectoral Analysis
Food Security and Livelihoods
Access to Food and Market Functionality
Local markets and supply chains have largely recovered following the March 2025 earthquake, with major corridors such as
Yangon–Mandalay operational and the majority of townships reporting moderate to full market functionality, enabling resumed
deliveries of food, household items and construction materials and supporting continued use of cash-based assistance approaches
[615]. The reopening of the Mandalay–Myitkyina domestic route and the expected reopening of the Myawaddy–Mae Sot Friendship
Bridge No.2 are projected to improve domestic and cross-border agricultural trade and ease transport constraints in the coming
months [621]. Key price and cost indicators in May 2026 include:
- Rice prices rose modestly by 2.2% year-on-year and 1.6% month-on-month [617][621].
- Maize prices continued to increase, driven by strong export demand ahead of Thailand's duty-free window [617][621].
- Fuel prices remain substantially elevated: diesel was about 73% higher and petrol about 83% higher than pre-conflict levels, with
diesel up roughly 108% and petrol up about 66% versus May 2025 [621].
- Animal-source food prices, including fish, mutton and beef, remained substantially higher year-on-year [617].
Elevated fuel and fertilizer costs have eroded fertilizer profitability and led some farmers to reduce ploughings or cultivate smaller
areas, threatening monsoon-season production and eroding household purchasing power, while the March 2026 fuel crisis has
deepened economic pressures and produced pronounced gendered impacts on livelihoods and access to services [617][625].
Conflict and control of logistics continue to disrupt market functionality in specific areas: junta actors have imposed tight restrictions
on logistics into Kyaukphyu since late 2023, limiting imports to a few monthly shipments received by junta-connected traders who
resell goods at inflated prices, and residents report that a family of four needs around 30,000 MMK per day to prepare basic meals in
the town [633]. Fuel shortages and high transport costs have constrained movement and trade in conflict-affected townships such as
Waw Ray (Dooplaya), where motorcycle petrol prices rose from about 7,000–8,000 MMK/litre to 15,000–20,000 MMK/litre between
January–March 2026 and staple commodity prices also increased [623][613]. Humanitarian actors are conducting market
assessments and supplier mapping (with a draft list of reliable suppliers expected in July 2026) and continue market monitoring to
inform response options, including cash-based assistance where markets are functional [629][615].
Coping Mechanisms
Households in Myanmar are adopting negative coping strategies as rising fuel and transport costs, market disruptions and conflict
constrain livelihoods and purchasing power [621][615]. The fuel crisis has had pronounced gendered impacts: 56% of respondents
reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit because of rising travel costs, and 46% reported electricity/connectivity disruptions
limiting women's access to mobile money and cash transfers; 44% said higher transport costs are affecting girls' school attendance
[609]. Fuel shortages increased household stress (87% reported), pushed women and girls to travel further to collect cooking gas
(59%), and increased women's dependence on male family members (70%) [609]. As incomes fall, 62% of respondents reported
increased borrowing; more severe negative coping strategies reported included labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%),
survival sex for money (16%), and sex in exchange for food or fuel (8%) [609].
Conflict-related market controls and monopoly of food supplies have reduced affordable access to staples in some areas, with
growing reliance on begging to meet basic food needs reported in Kyaukphyu [633]. Economic shocks from the 2025 earthquake and
its aftermath also depressed livelihoods, with about 15% of households near the epicentre reporting loss of income or employment,
slowing recovery and increasing reliance on coping strategies [615].
Livelihood Disruption
Myanmar's fuel crisis, which began in March 2026, has sharply increased fuel prices and operating costs—by end-May 2026 diesel was
about 73% higher than pre-conflict levels and up 108% year-on-year, while petrol rose 66% year-on-year—eroding transport
affordability for households and businesses [621][625]. The crisis is also gendered, eroding women's livelihoods, increasing unpaid
care burdens and dependence on male household members, and constraining the capacity of women-led organisations to engage in
crisis mitigation and service delivery [625]. High fuel and fertilizer costs are reducing agricultural profitability and prompting some
farmers to cultivate smaller areas or reduce ploughing ahead of the monsoon, threatening production and farm incomes [617].
Plantation-dependent communities in Dooplaya (Waw Ray/Win Yay) reported falling returns for local produce alongside rising input
and petrol costs between January–March 2026, including large declines in prices received for betelnut, reducing household incomes
and market opportunities [613]. Movement restrictions, intensified militarisation and Burma Army checkpoints have constrained
access to jobs, markets and services, caused displacement and forced villagers to avoid main roads, further limiting livelihood
activities [613]. Localised market control and conflict-related pressures have driven sharp price increases and job losses in some
areas; junta authorities' control of food flows in Kyaukphyu has pushed prices up and increased acute livelihood hardship among
low-income families [633].
Recovery from the 2025 earthquake remains incomplete: by late 2025 only about 45% of affected firms had returned to
pre-earthquake activity levels and around 15% of households near the epicentre reported loss of income or employment, prolonging
economic vulnerability [615]. Humanitarian actors have warned of additional hazards—including flood risk during the monsoon and
ENSO-related shocks—that could further disrupt agriculture, markets and household incomes in the coming months [634][629].

Impact on Agricultural Activities and Production
Fuel and fertilizer costs have risen sharply: diesel and petrol prices in May 2026 were reported substantially above pre-conflict and
year-earlier levels, and the reference price for a bag of urea was 157,000 MMK in May before being reduced to 140,000 MMK in June,
compared with 94,000 MMK in the previous monsoon season (roughly 67% and 49% higher than that prior benchmark) [621]. Higher
input and transport costs have eroded fertilizer profitability and prompted some farmers to cultivate smaller areas or reduce the
number of plowings, risking lower monsoon-season production [621][617]. In conflict-affected townships such as Waw Ray (Dooplaya
District), high petrol prices and fuel shortages in January–March 2026 impaired travel and disrupted plantation farming (rubber, lime,
mango), aggravating livelihood challenges [613].
Late June 2026 flood warnings were issued for parts of the Ayeyarwady Delta, Bago, the Sittaung River Basin and Yangon, and the
Department of Fisheries advised fish farmers to take preventive measures against pond flooding and fish losses [621]. A new El Niño
phase was expected in June 2026, increasing drought risk across much of Southeast Asia and raising concern for
monsoon-dependent planting and water availability in Myanmar [621][629]. One-year post-earthquake assessments note that,
although transport corridors and markets have largely reopened, rising fuel and transport costs continue to slow livelihood recovery
and increase the cost of agricultural inputs and market access [615].
Food and Livelihood Needs and Current Response
Humanitarian food and livelihood support in Myanmar is constrained by widespread access restrictions and economic shocks,
including rising fuel and transport costs that are undermining recovery and market access [629][615]. Key figures as of early June
2026:
- An estimated 3.8 million people remain internally displaced within Myanmar, with UNHCR reporting 5.4 million people forcibly
displaced within the Myanmar situation as of end-May 2026 [629][640].
- Nearly half of townships (47%) face high humanitarian access constraints, with only 8% considered relatively accessible, limiting
delivery of assistance across large areas [629].
- UNHCR and partners delivered core relief items to over 74,000 people, shelter support to approximately 3,000 people (559
families), and multi-purpose assistance to approximately 3,300 people (1,146 families) in Myanmar during the April–May 2026
reporting period [640].
Humanitarian modalities in use include distribution of core relief items, shelter support and multi-purpose cash or cash-based
approaches where markets have resumed [640][615]. 
Critical gaps include areas with active conflict or recent air strikes where displaced households lack external support—for example, Mu
Traw/Lu Thaw displaced communities reported no organisational assistance as of May 2026—as well as local monopolisation of food
supplies driving sharp price increases in locations such as Kyaukphyu, and disrupted access to agricultural inputs and markets
[661][633][615]. Recovery and livelihood restoration remain partial: the World Bank reported around 45% of affected firms had returned
to pre-earthquake activity by late 2025 and only about half of affected households had begun repairs [615]. Climate and seasonal
risks (monsoon flood warnings and a developing El Niño) and elevated fuel and fertiliser costs threaten agricultural production and
household purchasing power in the coming months, increasing the need for livelihood-support interventions [621][612][615].
UNHCR continues to lead and strengthen inter-agency coordination for refugee- and displacement-related responses, including
through the Rohingya Coordination Team introduced in January 2026, and health and area-based coordination groups are preparing
the 2027 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan with an emphasis on greater involvement of local partners [640][629]. Local and
national actors have assumed increased operational responsibility, highlighting the need to prioritise funding, cash-based assistance,
agricultural input provision, and support to local NGOs for sustained livelihood recovery; gender-sensitive targeting—including cash
transfers for women-headed households—is also recommended given rising fuel-related impacts on women's livelihoods [620][609].
Education
Learning Environment and Facility Conditions
In Waw Ray (Win Yay) Township, Dooplaya District (January–March 2026), some schools were operated by the Burma regime while
others rejected its authority; schools near Burma Army camps were closed and students studied in villagers' houses, though they
completed the 2025–2026 school year [663][666]. The military junta's crackdown has targeted educational institutions more broadly,
including university closures, imprisonment of scholars, and displacement of students [701]. In Magway region, a public school and a
monastery have been occupied by the Myanmar military since early 2025, restricting local education access [674]. The 28 March 2025
earthquake damaged or destroyed 2,596 schools across affected areas, creating significant ongoing infrastructure and rehabilitation
needs [667]. Burma Army air strikes in April 2026 destroyed at least one middle school and a school hall in Lu Thaw Township (Mu
Traw/Hpapun District), killing and injuring civilians and prompting further displacement that interrupted schooling [690].
Access to Education and Barriers
High petrol prices and frequent fuel shortages since March 2026 increased transport costs, making motorcycle rental unaffordable for
some families and creating practical travel barriers to accessing schools [666]. Villagers also faced travel restrictions to avoid Burma
Army checkpoints, further impeding movement to schools and contributing to displacement that disrupted schooling [666][663].
Rising transport costs were particularly noted to affect girls' school attendance or contribute to dropout, with 44% of respondents in a
June 2026 GiHA survey citing this impact [676]. By end-May/early June 2026, an estimated 3.8 million people were internally displaced
in Myanmar, a scale of displacement that significantly exacerbates barriers to enrolment and regular school attendance [668][670].

Education Needs and Current Response
Priority education-sector needs in the current response context include rehabilitation of the 2,596 schools damaged or destroyed by
the 28 March 2025 earthquake, heightened psychosocial support for children, and the establishment of safe learning environments in
conflict-affected areas [667][664]. In conflict-affected townships, some formal schools have closed or operate under conflicting

engagement approaches for Rohingya children through regional workshops and advocating for their inclusion in national education

Health
Availability and Access to Healthcare Services
Humanitarian monitoring indicates widespread attacks and obstructions to health care in Myanmar, with Insecurity Insight and Health
Cluster data recording roughly 1,940 incidents against health care since February 2021 through mid-2026, including 175 health workers
killed, approximately 931–932 arrested, and health facilities damaged on over 500 occasions [705][709]. Between 10–23 June 2026,
reported incidents included seizure of a makeshift clinic and base camp (11 June), confiscation of an X-ray machine (14 June),
long-term occupation of a sub-rural health centre, arrest and reported torture of a CDM-affiliated health worker (19 June), and
damage to an ambulance plus injuries from an airstrike (23 June) [707]. Airstrikes and combat incidents in 2026 have directly
destroyed or damaged clinics and ambulances — for example, April 2026 airstrikes in Lu Thaw Township destroyed a clinic and other
community infrastructure, triggering displacement and reduced access to care [724].
The Myanmar Health Cluster has warned that intensifying conflict and restrictions on NGO registration are severely impeding access
for health workers, patients and supplies, while the monsoon season is increasing risks of AWD, malaria and dengue, and medicine
supply chains are deteriorating [704]. Fuel shortages and price increases since March 2026 have further narrowed access to
time-sensitive care, with assessments reporting medicine stockouts, reduced clinic hours, interruptions to maternal and reproductive
care, cuts to mobile and outreach services, and delays in GBV and pregnancy referrals [717][716]. Local reporting from Dooplaya (Waw
Ray) District for January–March 2026 found a KDHW clinic with insufficient medicines and documented that sharp petrol price rises
and fuel shortages impeded travel for surgical and hospital care, pushing some patients to seek treatment across the border in
Thailand and leaving poorer patients unable to access treatment [703][711]. Sexual and reproductive health services continue to be
provided via mobile clinics, static facilities and community-based services, with UNFPA and SRH partners supporting emergency
referrals, maternity waiting homes and availability of selected Inter-Agency Reproductive Health kits [704].
Disease Outbreaks
As of late June 2026, localized Acute Watery Diarrhoea (AWD) outbreaks occurred in temporary settlements in Kachin and Rakhine and
were rapidly contained through coordinated Health–WASH responses, with WHO's global cholera update listing Myanmar among
countries reporting AWD cases [704][705][706]. The Health Cluster tested an AWD Prioritization Tool during a preparedness workshop
in Myitkyina on 4–5 June 2026, while UNICEF planned further AWD preparedness workshops for Rakhine, Northwest, Southeast, Shan
and Karenni; partners stressed data anonymization, non-financial technical support, and inclusion of security and duty-of-care
measures for field staff [704].
Monsoon conditions are driving increased risk across multiple diseases, with a reported surge in malaria from Chin, Kachin, Karenni,
Sagaing and Tanintharyi, and rising dengue cases in Rakhine and Karenni, while limited diagnostics are delaying detection of
outbreak-prone diseases [705][704]. Measles remains a cross-border threat — the outbreak in neighbouring Bangladesh is
decreasing but the risk for Rakhine and Chin remains high due to many children remaining unvaccinated, prompting continued
measles preparedness efforts [705].
Rising displacement, estimated at approximately 3.8 million IDPs as of 8 June 2026, and severe access constraints are intensifying
overcrowding and hampering outbreak response and routine services [704][708][705]. Health Cluster response actions include WHO
SSA reporting awareness sessions, market assessments, and a Phase II health facility survey planned for July to inform preparedness
and response [704][708][705].
Health System Challenges
Myanmar's health system faces severe and compounding structural challenges. Between 1 February 2021 and June 2026, there were at
least 1,939–1,943 attacks on health care, resulting in 175 health worker deaths, over 931 arrests, and damage to health facilities on at
least 506–509 occasions [705][709]. Between 1 and 30 June 2026, Insecurity Insight reported 13 new incidents affecting health care,
while WHO's Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care recorded one verified incident; the Health Cluster has planned awareness
sessions to improve incident reporting by partners [705]. OHCHR reported over 700 civilians killed by military attacks between August
2025 and 31 January 2026 and highlighted that reductions in international assistance are contributing to deterioration of emergency
healthcare provision, particularly medicine supply chains and health facilities [704].
The 28 March 2025 earthquake damaged or destroyed 713 health facilities, and reconstruction and recovery remain slowed by rising
fuel and transport costs and broader economic pressures [710]. The nationwide fuel crisis since March 2026 has driven medicine
stockouts, reduced clinic hours, interruptions to maternal and reproductive care, and cuts to mobile clinics and outreach, with a survey
reporting 68% of respondents experiencing delays in referrals for GBV survivors and pregnant women and 56% reporting cuts to mobile
and outreach services [717].

Intensifying conflict, barriers to NGO registration by de facto authorities, and administrative and security constraints — including risks
from sharing operational location data — are impeding partner operations, forcing some to cease activities and complicating supply
and staffing arrangements [704]. WHO and the Health Cluster have flagged shortages of skilled health workers and essential

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS)
Protection Cluster-led assessments in June 2026 identified growing psychosocial support needs and significant gaps in referral
services across Myanmar, with particular shortages reported in Kachin State [720]. UNHCR and partners delivered community-based
protection activities that included MHPSS awareness sessions, though coverage remains constrained by access and operational limits
[720].
Severe access constraints — with 47% of 251 townships classified as high access constraints and the most difficult-to-reach areas
concentrated in Sagaing and Rakhine — are impeding delivery of humanitarian services including MHPSS [704]. The March–June 2026
fuel crisis has further weakened service delivery and referral pathways for psychosocial support, with 68% of respondents reporting
delays in referrals for GBV survivors and pregnant women and 56% reporting cuts to mobile clinics, outreach, and community-based
services that provide psychosocial support [717].
Health partners have recommended the systematic inclusion of psychosocial support and duty-of-care measures — including
evacuation plans, safety reserves, and staff psychosocial support — within health and emergency programming to protect both
affected communities and frontline staff [704].
Health Needs and Current Response
The most urgent healthcare needs include emergency trauma care and continuity of maternal, child and sexual and reproductive
health services; restoration of medicine supply chains and health facility functionality; management of communicable disease
outbreaks (malaria, dengue, AWD, measles); and continuity of care for chronic NCDs amid rising displacement and severe access
constraints [704][705]. Internally displaced people rose to an estimated 3.8 million as of 8 June 2026, while OCHA reported that 47% of
251 townships face high access constraints and only 8% are relatively accessible, concentrating over 1 million people in need in
Sagaing and Rakhine and severely limiting health service reach [704].
Key health system gaps include medicine supply interruptions, reduced clinic hours, shortages of qualified staff as partners cut
programmes and personnel, under-resourced facilities, and constrained medical logistics due to fuel shortages and access
blockades [704][717][705]. Sexual and reproductive health services continue to be delivered through mobile clinics, static facilities and
community-based services, with UNFPA supporting capacity-building in June 2026 and selected Inter-Agency RH kits available,
though maternal referrals and access have been undermined by fuel shortages, medicine stockouts and cuts to outreach services
[704][717].
On the response side, WHO ran NCD-in-emergencies workshops in May–June 2026, and the Health and WASH Clusters tested an AWD
Prioritization Tool in Myitkyina on 4–5 June 2026 to better target preparedness and response [704][705]. Coordination is active but
strained: the Health Cluster is leading multisectoral contingency planning, sub-national AWD and measles preparedness with UNICEF,
WASH and Nutrition partners, and is promoting incident reporting into WHO's Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care to improve
monitoring [704][705].
Nutrition
Availability and Access to Nutrition Services
The Health Cluster reports severely restricted access impeding partners' ability to deliver services, with internally displaced people
reaching 3.8 million as of 8 June 2026 and 47% of townships facing high access constraints, limiting both outreach and facility-based
nutrition service coverage [740]. Open-source monitoring has documented at least 1,943 incidents of violence against or obstruction
of health care since 2021, with health workers killed and facilities damaged on hundreds of occasions, significantly undermining
nutrition and related health services [757]. Fuel shortages and sharply higher transport costs have further constrained patient and
staff mobility and access to care — for example, in Waw Ray township motorcycle fuel rose to approximately 15,000–20,000 kyats per
litre — with the March–May 2026 fuel crisis reducing mobile clinics, outreach and community-based services, and delaying referrals for
pregnant women and GBV survivors [752][754][746]. Shortages of diagnostics and administrative barriers to NGO registration are
delaying outbreak detection and response, and have forced some partners to cease activities in affected areas [740].
To address supply and coverage gaps, market assessments and visits to medical suppliers are underway, with a first draft list of
reliable suppliers expected in July; Phase II of a Health Facility Survey (Kobo questionnaire) is also being finalised to better map service
capacity [740][742]. A lack of consolidated, up-to-date facility-level nutrition service metrics — including numbers of nutrition service
points, workforce, and therapeutic food stocks — is implied by these ongoing assessments, the results of which are expected to inform
coverage and supply gaps in the coming weeks [740]. UNHCR will phase out support for nutrition programme implementation on
Bhasan Char effective 1 July 2026, with UNICEF having expressed interest in supporting continuation in coordination with WFP [743].
Health, WASH and Nutrition Clusters are planning AWD preparedness workshops across Rakhine, Northwest, Southeast, Shan and
Karenni using an AWD Prioritization Tool supported by UNICEF, and joint measles preparedness between Health and Nutrition Clusters is
ongoing in Rakhine and Chin due to large cohorts of unvaccinated children [740][742].

Nutrition Needs and Current Response
Macro-economic shocks — including sharp increases in fuel and fertilizer prices and elevated transport costs — are eroding
agricultural production prospects and keeping staple and animal-sourced food prices above last year's levels, increasing household
food security and nutrition risks ahead of the monsoon season [751][745][758]. These pressures compound the broader humanitarian
crisis, in which an estimated 3.8 million people remained internally displaced in Myanmar by end-May 2026, with severe access
constraints impeding humanitarian service delivery [739][740].
Acute threats to child nutrition include AWD outbreaks in Kachin and Rakhine, as well as a sustained measles risk in Rakhine and Chin.
In response, UNICEF is organising sub-national AWD preparedness workshops for Health-WASH-Nutrition partners using an AWD
Prioritization Tool, and capacity-building for measles diagnosis and treatment of health workers is ongoing [742][740]. Operational
actions also include joint AWD action planning and ongoing market and supplier assessments, with a first draft list of reliable medical
suppliers expected in July to support resumed health-nutrition supply chains [740][742].
WASH
Availability of Water Sources
The estimated increase in internally displaced people to about 3.8 million as of 8 June 2026 is placing additional pressure on WASH
service delivery in access-restricted areas [789]. Flood warnings were issued for parts of the Ayeyarwady Delta, Bago, the Sittaung
River Basin and Yangon because of heavy rain, elevated river flows and high tides, while a developing El Niño raises medium-term
drought risk — creating compound flood/drought threats to water availability [782][806]. Sharp fuel price rises and nationwide fuel
scarcity since March 2026 are disrupting fuel-dependent water pumping and irrigation, prompting some farmers to reduce
irrigation/ploughing and reducing reliability of water supplies [782]. One year after the March 2025 earthquake, major transport
corridors and electricity supply have largely returned — supporting recovery of basic services and markets — but many households
remain with incomplete repairs, which may limit restoration of community water systems in some areas [781].
Access to Sanitation Facilities and Hygiene Practices
During the monsoon season, localized AWD outbreaks occurred in temporary settlements in Kachin and Rakhine, and skin infections
were reported mainly from IDP camps in Kachin, Karenni and Rakhine [789][783]. These outbreaks were rapidly contained through
effective coordination between WASH and health partners [789][783]. Response and preparedness are constrained by intensifying
conflict, restrictions on NGO registration that have forced some partners to cease activities, military blockades, and shortages of
diagnostics that delay detection of outbreak-prone diseases [789][783]. Operational delivery of WASH items is further hindered
because large distributions can be unsafe — as they may be targeted by airstrikes — and beneficiaries often resist detailed
registration over fears of surveillance, complicating hygiene supply distributions [797].
Waste Management and Vector-Borne Disease Risks
WHO's global cholera update noted that Afghanistan and Myanmar reported acute watery diarrhoea (AWD) cases as of late June
2026 [800]. The monsoon season is increasing risks for AWD as well as malaria and dengue, with gaps in diagnostics delaying
detection of outbreak-prone diseases [789]. Independent reporting and environmental monitoring have documented toxic waste and
heavy-metal contamination from unregulated rare-earth and other mining operations in Shan State, including allegations of
contamination at levels up to nine times danger thresholds, highlighting river pollution risks from mining waste disposal [796]. A
hazardous mine-waste heap collapse in Hpakant in late June 2026 killed five scavengers and left about 15 missing, illustrating acute
physical hazards from unstable mining waste piles and the broader lack of safe mine-waste management [813][814]. WHO and
partners also flagged operational constraints — including difficulties for local organisations to meet registration and reporting
requirements and concerns about sharing precise location data that could pose security risks — which undermine timely detection,
data sharing and response to WASH- and vector-related threats [789].
Main WASH Needs and Humanitarian Response
The WASH Cluster tested an AWD Prioritization Tool at a preparedness workshop in Myitkyina on 4–5 June 2026, and UNICEF will support
roll-out of sub-national AWD preparedness workshops using the tool in Rakhine, Northwest, Southeast, Shan and Karenni [789][783].
Joint AWD action planning at sub-national level is ongoing as part of monsoon preparedness, led by the WASH, Health and Nutrition
clusters to prioritize high-risk townships [783]. A market assessment with a draft list of reliable suppliers is expected in July 2026 to
address WASH and medical supply shortfalls [789]. Scaling the WASH response remains constrained by intensifying conflict, access
restrictions, and limited diagnostics that hinder outbreak detection and service delivery [783][789].
Shelter and Non-Food Items (NFI)
Damage to Housing and Shelter Needs
The 28 March 2025 Mw7.7 earthquake caused widespread housing and infrastructure damage across central Myanmar, with more
than 11,995 homes, 2,596 schools, and 713 health facilities partially damaged or destroyed and over 200,000 people displaced [818].
One year on, many households have repaired or rebuilt damaged homes, but a substantial number remain in transitional shelters or
have incomplete repairs due to limited financial capacity and rising reconstruction costs [818]. Conflict-related strikes have further
compounded housing destruction: April 2026 air strikes in Za--- village (Mu Traw/Lu Thaw) burned 20 houses, damaged almost all
houses in the village, and destroyed a clinic, a middle school, and church buildings, forcing villagers to flee to forests or across the
border [838].
21
In Dooplaya (Waw Ray/Win Yay) township (January–March 2026), reports describe houses burned, Burma Army troops billeting in
homes, and consequent displacement, further reducing local shelter availability [829]. Internally displaced people rose to an
estimated 3.8 million as of 8 June 2026, severely constraining shelter availability, while 47% of townships face high access constraints
that impede shelter programming [826]. Immediate shelter needs include emergency shelter materials (tarpaulins), essential
household items, and temporary shelter solutions for families living in makeshift conditions or informal sites [838]. Key rebuilding
challenges are limited household financial capacity, rising construction and fuel/transport costs, and restricted humanitarian access
due to security and administrative barriers, all of which slow durable housing recovery and return [818][826].
Living Conditions in Camps or Settlements
Between 1 January and 31 May 2026, 2,260 new arrivals were biometrically identified through a joint exercise, bringing the total
identified since 2025 to 152,029 people (39,881 families), while 1,893 individuals were reportedly denied entry to Bangladesh in the same
period (14,380 denied since 2024) [821][824][819]. Shelter construction of double-storied temporary safer shelters in Block E of Camp
4-Extension resumed on 26 May 2026; 210 of 300 shelters are complete, the remaining 90 are more than 80% complete, and the
structures use approved local materials (bamboo, steel poles, plastic tarps) and are designed to be quickly dismantled rather than
serve as permanent housing [819]. Water and energy interventions include the Teknaf Water Project to address chronic shortages and
progressive solarization of camp water systems, with the Camp 26 solar-powered system fully operational and three substations
completed as of May 2026 [821]. UNHCR and IOM continue LPG provision to roughly 230,000 refugee households, with suppliers meeting
distribution requirements for April–May 2026 and funding projected to cover needs through end-September at current rates [824].
Fuel shortages and related service disruptions have elevated protection risks in displacement and host-community settings, including
reduced public lighting and delayed GBV and maternal referrals, increasing insecurity for women, children, older persons, and persons
with disabilities [828]. Health and hygiene risks persist across displacement sites: Acute Watery Diarrhoea (AWD) and other
water-related outbreaks in temporary settlements in Kachin and Rakhine have been rapidly contained through coordinated
Health–WASH responses, and AWD preparedness tools and workshops were conducted in early June 2026 to prioritise high-risk areas
[826][822]. Attacks on health care have been widespread and sustained, with open-source monitoring documenting roughly 1,900+
incidents of violence or obstruction against health care since 2021, health facilities damaged on hundreds of occasions, and health
workers killed or arrested—conditions that undermine service provision in camps and informal settlements [827][822][834]. Nearly half
of townships in Myanmar face high access constraints, limiting delivery of services and emergency healthcare to displacement sites
[826][820].
NFI and Shelter Needs and Current Response
UNHCR and humanitarian partners delivered core relief items to over 74,000 people in Myanmar, with approximately 3,000 people (559
families) receiving targeted shelter support and around 3,300 people (1,146 families) receiving multi-purpose assistance [821]. UNHCR
led Protection and Shelter, Land and Site Coordination Cluster activities to coordinate assessments and response planning [821]. A
one-year post-earthquake assessment noted that supply chains for essential goods—including household items and construction
materials—have largely resumed, supporting cash-based approaches; however, many households remain in transitional shelters or
have incomplete repairs and continue to need shelter materials and repair assistance [818]. Access constraints remain a critical
bottleneck, with checkpoints, administrative restrictions, and violence against personnel impeding NFI and shelter delivery, particularly
in Sagaing and Rakhine where over 1 million people in need are concentrated [826]. Funding gaps pose a further structural challenge:
UNHCR has cautioned that the global diversion of funds to the Middle East crisis has strained an already underfunded response in
Myanmar, increasing risks to sustained NFI and shelter programming [820].

Factors Driving protection risks
Escalating hostilities — including airstrikes, shelling and increased militarisation — are the primary driver of large-scale displacement
and civilian harm. The conflict's fragmentation, involving hundreds of armed groups and sustained frontline violence, sustains a
climate of impunity and indiscriminate harm [862][856][865][875]. Arbitrary arrests, enforced disappearances, forced recruitment
and extortion by armed actors further erode rule of law and compound civilian vulnerability [854][862].
Systematic attacks on health care and the targeting of health personnel have severely degraded protection and referral services.
Administrative measures such as restrictions on NGO registration, checkpoint impediments and blockades are limiting humanitarian
access and freedom of movement [854][862].
Legal and documentation gaps — including the suspension of regular refugee registration and limited civil documentation in
conflict-affected areas — restrict access to justice, social services and lawful status, increasing vulnerability to exploitation and
exclusion [873][854].
The March–June 2026 fuel crisis and rising inflation have driven acute economic deprivation, eroded livelihoods (notably for women),
increased household debt and pushed households toward harmful coping strategies including labour exploitation, forced marriage
and survival sex, thereby raising protection and GBV risks and constraining service continuity [867][876]. Reduced international
assistance and the diversion of resources to other crises have forced programme cuts and increased reliance on local protection
mechanisms that lack sufficient capacity and funding [856][862].

Psychological and mental health impacts
Internal displacement reaching approximately 3.8 million by early June 2026, combined with over 700 civilians killed between August
2025 and 31 January 2026, has generated widespread trauma and intensified psychosocial needs among affected populations
[862][869][854]. Field reports from April–May 2026 document acute psychological effects, including children traumatised and
exhibiting abnormal behaviour, widespread anxiety among villagers who fled after airstrikes, pregnant women facing delivery
challenges during displacement, and communities reporting no organisational support as of May 2026 [870][880].
The March 2026 fuel crisis has further intensified household stress: surveys of women-led organisations found 87% reporting that fuel
shortages increased household stress, 68% reporting delays in GBV and maternal referrals, and 79% noting additional challenges for
women staff and frontline workers [867].
Humanitarian actors report growing psychosocial support needs, but reductions in international assistance, NGO registration barriers,
checkpoint restrictions and staff cuts have forced partners to curtail MHPSS programmes [862][878]. Protection cluster assessments
identify persistent gaps in referral services — particularly in Kachin State — that limit the ability to detect and treat mental health and
psychosocial needs at scale [854][862].
Protection Needs and Current Response
Urgent protection needs include psychosocial support, systematic GBV and child-protection case management and referral
pathways, improved access to civil documentation and legal assistance, safe shelters and safe spaces, and prioritisation of fuel and
transport for protection and referral services [854][863][867]. Accountability and protection coordination systems are under strain —
regional AAP/PSEA mechanisms have been scaled back and collective accountability to affected populations remains uneven in
conflict-affected, access-constrained settings, weakening feedback, referral and safe-complaint pathways [855][864].
UNHCR serves as Protection Cluster lead, working with 16 national and nine international partners to strengthen localisation and access
to hard-to-reach areas. It supports measures to improve access to civil documentation in the South-East and delivers
community-based protection, complaints-and-feedback sessions, PSEA messaging, and MHPSS activities [854][862]. Health Cluster
actions include AWD preparedness workshops and Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care (SSA) awareness sessions to
improve reporting and preparedness [854][862]. UNHCR also facilitated the release and community-based reunification of 15 people,
predominantly children, in May 2026 [854].
Child Protection concerns
Escalating hostilities are exposing children to forced recruitment, arbitrary detention, family separation and restricted movement, with
protection assessments noting rising psychosocial needs and gaps in referral services, particularly in Kachin State [854][856]. Recent
arrivals include high proportions of unaccompanied or separated children, creating urgent needs for family tracing, reunification and
community-based care [854].
Airstrikes and shelling have damaged schools, injured children and prompted closures and interrupted schooling in multiple areas,
including Lu Thaw Township in April 2026 and Waw Ray/Dooplaya District between January and March 2026, with families reporting
children unable to study due to fear of strikes [870][872]. Monitoring and incident reports from April–May 2026 describe airstrike-driven
displacement where families including children fled with minimal belongings and now face acute shortages of food, shelter,
healthcare and education access [880][870].
Children and adolescents face heightened risks of trafficking, forced labour and sexual exploitation linked to transnational scam
compounds and smuggling routes, with documented cases of forced labour and sexual violence in regional scam centres affecting
victims trafficked through or inside Myanmar [879][854]. Economic shocks and the March–June 2026 fuel crisis are driving negative
coping strategies that increase protection risks for girls and adolescents, including reported rises in labour exploitation, forced
marriage and survival sex [867][868].
Violence against women and girls
The Myanmar Gender in Humanitarian Action (GiHA) Working Group, co-chaired by UN Women and UNFPA, reported in June 2026 that
the March 2026 fuel crisis is eroding women's economic security and driving harmful coping behaviours. Key findings include: 56% of
respondents reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit due to rising travel costs; 62% reported increased household
borrowing; 46% said electricity and connectivity disruptions limited women's access to mobile money and cash transfers; and 44%
reported higher transport costs are affecting girls' school attendance or contributing to dropout [867][868]. Reported harmful coping
included labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%), survival sex (16%), and sex for food or fuel (8%) [867][868].
GiHA findings also documented narrowing access to time-sensitive services for survivors: 68% reported delays in referrals for GBV
survivors and pregnant women, two-thirds reported reduced access to health and sexual and reproductive health services, and 56%
reported cuts to mobile clinics, outreach and community-based services [867][868]. Legal-access barriers compound these
challenges, with 37% noting disrupted ability of survivors to reach legal aid, courts or police, and 44% reporting reduced operational
capacity among legal assistance providers [867].
Reporting on trafficking and scam-compound rescues documents a rise in sexual violence and exploitation affecting women in
Myanmar-linked cyber-scam operations, including cases of rape, forced abortion and other severe sexual abuse [879][888]. An April
2026 AAP/PSEA regional survey noted that gender and social norms restrict women's access to formal feedback channels and that
many feedback mechanisms are not clearly linked to safe, confidential PSEA referral pathways, undermining survivors' ability to report
and receive follow-up [864]. Funding reductions, access constraints and operational pressures have forced organisations to cut
programmes and staff, further straining GBV referral pathways and the capacity of women-led organisations responding at
community level [854][862].

Vulnerable Groups / Special Needs Groups
Unaccompanied and Separated Children
In May 2026, UNHCR advocacy contributed to the release of 15 people, predominantly children, facilitating family reunification and
community-based care [907]. UNHCR and partners are supporting protection-risk assessments and care solutions, while regular
refugee registration remains suspended since May 2022 and the biometric family-card exercise does not issue individual refugee
documents, which may constrain formal identification and reunification processes [907][918]. Protection Cluster-led assessments in
Myanmar noted growing psychosocial support needs and gaps in referral services, notably in Kachin State, indicating limited
child-protection case management capacity in some areas [907].
Pregnant and Lactating Women
Between late June and early July 2026, maternal and reproductive health access in Myanmar remained highly constrained by attacks
on health care, fuel shortages and large-scale displacement that impeded referrals and facility access [919][929][934]. SRH partners
continued delivering essential sexual and reproductive health services through mobile clinics, static facilities and community-based
services in June 2026, while UNFPA supported SRH capacity-building and selected Inter-Agency Reproductive Health kits were reported
available [909]. Attacks on health care since 2021 have damaged facilities and resulted in killed and detained health workers, further
undermining availability of maternal services and safe referral pathways [910][919]. Key operational and protection indicators
reported by the Myanmar Gender in Humanitarian Action Working Group and partners include: 68% of surveyed organisations reported
delays in referrals for GBV survivors and pregnant women, risking missed emergency contraception, psychosocial support or timely
care during labour [929][916][914]; two-thirds reported reduced access to health and sexual and reproductive health services, and
56% reported cuts to mobile clinics, outreach and community-based services that reach those least able to travel [929][916][914]; and
79% reported additional operational challenges for women staff due to fuel shortages, while 87% reported increased household stress
from energy rationing affecting care responsibilities and staff availability [929][916][914]. Fuel shortages since March 2026 have
weakened facility operations, caused medicine stockouts, reduced clinic hours and interrupted maternal and reproductive care,
including emergency referrals, across affected areas [929][915]. Incident reporting from April–June 2026 documents airstrikes and
displacement that directly disrupted deliveries and created acute risks for pregnant women during displacement, increasing urgent
needs for maternal health and GBV referral services in affected locations [934][933][907]. Humanitarian access constraints —
including an estimated 3.8 million internally displaced people as of early June 2026 and OCHA reporting 47% of townships facing high
access restrictions — are limiting the reach of maternal health services and referrals in priority areas such as Sagaing and Rakhine
[909][908]. UNHCR announced a phasing out of nutrition programme support on Bhasan Char effective 1 July 2026, with UNICEF
expressing interest to continue nutrition support in coordination with WFP, a change that may affect maternal and infant nutrition
services for that population [911].
Adolescents and Youth
Schools in several areas were closed or shifted to informal home-based teaching because students feared Burma Army airstrikes,
disrupting the 2025–2026 school year in parts of Dooplaya (Waw Ray) Township between January and March 2026 [920][915].
Airstrikes in mid-April 2026 in Mu Traw/Lu Thaw Township caused civilian deaths, injuries and destruction, prompting mass flight from
villages and leaving children unable to return to school as of May 2026 [933][920]. Escalating hostilities have increased risks of forced
recruitment and conscription for young people, a protection concern noted by UNHCR and reported through large-scale conscription
measures activated in February 2024 [907][923]. Local reporting from early 2026 documents rising drug use among young people and
reduced safe mobility amid militarisation and travel restrictions, increasing protection and livelihood vulnerabilities for adolescents
and youth [915][920]. Protection-cluster and health partners identified growing psychosocial support needs and gaps in referral
services for adolescents, particularly in Kachin State [907][909]. Communities in Shan and Kayah continue to identify livelihoods as a
priority need amid displacement and insecurity, while local reports describe worsening household livelihoods and sharply rising
transport and fuel costs that hinder youth access to education and work [907][920][915]. Accountability and community engagement
mechanisms — including feedback and participation platforms — remain strained in Myanmar's access-constrained context, limiting
youth and community participation in planning and feedback processes [930][917][942].
People with Disabilities
UN and cluster reporting describe a deterioration in emergency health provision in Myanmar, including medicine supply-chain
disruptions and damaged health facilities, driven by reductions in international assistance, military blockades and ongoing hostilities,
undermining access to specialised medical care and referral services that persons with disabilities depend upon [909]. Severely
restricted humanitarian access further constrains delivery of inclusive services: OCHA's May 2026 Humanitarian Access Severity
Monitoring found that out of 251 townships, 47% face high access constraints and only 8% are relatively accessible, with the most
difficult-to-reach townships concentrated in Sagaing and Rakhine where over one million people are in need [909].
Elderly
Access to health care for older people has been severely disrupted by systematic attacks on health services, with the Myanmar Health
Cluster reporting at least 1,939 attacks on health care between 1 February 2021 and 24 May 2026 [910]. Specific incidents in June 2026 —
including a military takeover of a makeshift clinic, confiscation of medical equipment, arrest and reported torture of a health worker,
and ambulances and homes damaged in airstrikes — have further reduced local care availability [912]. Local primary clinics report
medicine shortages and transport barriers, with a Karen Department of Health and Welfare clinic in Dooplaya's Az--- tract having
insufficient medicines, while rising fuel costs and frequent fuel shortages impede travel to surgical care or hospitals for those unable to
afford transport [915]. Monsoon-related disease pressure — including increased risk of acute watery diarrhoea (AWD), malaria and
dengue — and localised AWD outbreaks are placing additional strain on health services during June 2026, compounding access
problems for vulnerable groups including older persons [909][910].
24
Humanitarian actors (UNHCR and partners) delivered core relief items and expanded community-based protection and MHPSS
activities in April–May 2026, including distributions reaching tens of thousands and MHPSS/community-protection sessions that aim to
reach vulnerable populations, which can include older persons in displacement settings [907]. Calls from regional actors for the
release of "elderly and infirm" detainees underscore specific protection concerns for older people in detention contexts [936].
Displaced
By end-May 2026, an estimated 5.4 million people were forcibly displaced in the Myanmar situation, including roughly 3.8 million
internally displaced people as reported in early June 2026, alongside 1.6 million refugees and asylum-seekers, of whom 1,197,411 were
registered refugees in Bangladesh as of 31 May 2026 [907][911][909]. Escalating hostilities inside Myanmar — including airstrikes,
shelling and checkpoint controls — have exposed civilians to forced recruitment, extortion, arbitrary detention, family separation and
restricted movement, while intensified access constraints left 47% of townships with high humanitarian access constraints in May 2026
[907][909]. Livelihoods deteriorated as fuel shortages and rising transport costs from March–June 2026 eroded women's economic
security: 56% of surveyed respondents reported women losing jobs or being forced to quit because of travel costs, and households
adopted harmful coping strategies including increased borrowing (62%), labour exploitation (33%), forced marriage (18%) and survival
sex (16%) [929]. Localized militarization and displacement in southeastern areas such as Dooplaya District between January–March
2026 disrupted plantation livelihoods, restricted movement at checkpoints and forced villagers to flee toward farms, forests, border
areas and camps [920][915]. In June 2026, around 80 Myanmar migrant workers who resigned from a garment factory near the
China–Myanmar border were detained amid allegations of unpaid overtime, exploitative recruitment and use of employer-sponsored
permits to discourage resignations [939]. Monsoon-related disease risks and localised AWD outbreaks in Kachin and Rakhine were
reported in June 2026, prompting AWD preparedness workshops and use of an AWD prioritisation tool for high-risk townships
[909][910]. Restrictions on NGO registration, rising arrests and detentions of aid workers and operational access constraints have
forced some partners to cease activities, reducing service delivery and complicating protection and referral pathways for displaced
populations [909][942].
Minorities
Open-source monitoring and the Health Cluster recorded a surge of attacks on health care across Myanmar since the 2021 coup — at
least 1,939–1,943 incidents by late May/early June 2026, with roughly 175 health workers killed, over 930 arrested and hundreds of
facilities damaged — constraining minority communities' access to health services in states including Kachin, Rakhine and Sagaing
[919][910]. Local and women-led organisations in Myanmar have assumed greater leadership of humanitarian response but report
heightened personal and operational risks; the Myanmar Gender in Humanitarian Action group recommends flexible, predictable
funding, duty-of-care measures and direct support to sustain these actors [942][929][916]. International actors and rights groups
have urged accountability for junta abuses and warned that continued obstruction of aid and denial of basic rights exacerbate
exclusion and legal insecurity for minorities, reinforcing calls for targeted protections and access to justice [926].
